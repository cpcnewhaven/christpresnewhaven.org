A Regional Church Model
A Church for the 21st Century

by Preston Graham, Jr.
Introduction:
Why do people drive in from places like Guilford, Cheshire, Orange, Milford, East and West Haven, Wilton, Hamden, Waterbury and other such "edge cities" and unite together with people in the heart of New Haven each Sunday?
A people who are united in a common vision for the Glory of Christ in Christian worship and community and who realize the strategic advantages and postive potential of a Gospel that is able to transcend the common town/gown, urban/suburban, ethnic, demographic and economic boundaries that have tended to fracture our greater New Haven neighborhood!
This short paper intends to further explain why this is so.

General Explanation:
Demographic studies have shown that there is a growing interrelationship and interdependence between a given center city like New Haven and its surrounding "edge cities." It is noted that both the advantages and disadvantages of a given "center city" are clearly related to the advantages and disadvantages of surrounding edge cities. As a city goes, so goes the region. For this reason, demographic experts are redefining the city in terms of major metropolitan regions, rather than self-contained and independent towns-- only some larger and others smaller in size.(For example, David Rusk's Cities Without Suburbs; Peter Salins, "Metropolitan Areas: Cities, Suburbs, and the Ties that Bind" in Interwoven Destinies, ed. Henry G. Ciosneros; Anthony Downs, Opening Up the Suburbs.) A metropolitan region is defined as consisting of many "edge cities", all of which share in common a "center city." For example, it has become increasingly obsolete to think of a state such as Connecticut as simply consisting of 169 cities. The demographic data simply doesn't allow for such a classification any more. Rather Connecticut is thought of as consisting of several major metropolitan regions, perhaps, Hartford, New Haven, Stamford, etc.

By implication, a church of the 21st century will have a vision that is regional in its mission-- a vision that seeks to respect the inter-relationship between the center city and the various edge cities so as to have the most profound impact for Christ. Such a vision in the New Haven region will respond to at least four crucial aspects relative to planting a church for the glory of Christ. Keep in mind that these are broad sweeping generalizations and would allow for some specific exceptions in a given situation. They are also focused upon the advantages for the church and its mission. These four generalizations do not sufficiently touch upon the advantages that a regional church model might have upon the greater city itself. As a church that is "pro-New Haven region," we certainly appreciate these advantages as well.

Four Crucial Elements of a Regional Strategy

1. Demographic Cruciality:
Both for the sake of attracting center-city people and gathering people from nearby towns surrounding New Haven, our chances of reaching a greater area and a greater number of people are from the center-city reaching out rather than from one edge city reaching into the center-city or even across the center-city to another edge city. Therefore what we do in common we will tend to do in the center-city, whereas what we do in smaller groups will tend to be located in the various sections most relevant to those participating-- for example Family groups and "Community Outreach and Bible Studies."

One of the more crucial factors in planting a church regards the volume of "new movers" into the selected region. Since "new movers" represent a people group that is least committed to a specific church and most open to finding a new church, "new movers" have always been a prime target upon which a new church is planted. Yet the fact is, we are seeking to build a church in the only state in America that is actually decreasing in population. A review of growth patterns in the greater New Haven area reveals that no single section yields the kind of growth that can readily sustain a new church, at least not humanly speaking-- not to mention the fact that we are a relatively unknown denomination to this region. Therefore, we must position ourselves so as to take best advantage of the widest circle of "new movers" representing regional growth. (Even a brief survey of present CPC members quickly reveals the wisdom of this strategy) The center city is psychologically and geographically more accessible to the various edge cities than say one of the edge cities would be to the rest of the edge cities. In short, "central cities" are what all the surrounding "edge cities" share in common providing unique opportunities for ministry in a region. It has also been noted that for several reasons, urban residents are much less likely to travel out to edge city churches than suburban residents are to travel into a center city church. (See for example Session of the City, produced by Mission to North America, PCA.) Since a large percentage of our regions "new movers" are people that are locating near the Yale campus, our strategy would want to take this into consideration.
2. Cultural Cruciality:
A center-city/regional approach to church planting will tend more to address the leading cultural issues of it's day merely because it has not isolated itself from the major institutional gatekeepers that are stationed in the city. Yet it also keeps in mind that one cannot reach the institutions without also reaching the families that support these institutions, many of which are living in the edge-cites. If you want to impact the "culture wars" so as to "win the society" you must therefore win both the center-city and the edge-cities together.
Whereas there is a current "decentralization" in technical, retail and professional services from the center city (largely due to modern communications and technology), there remains, and probably always will remain, a "centralization" of the arts, education/research and government centers in the central city. Modern central cities like New Haven remain the "cultural hubs" of society such that the greatest potential for cultural influence is strategically reserved for the central-city church. This is further accentuated in New Haven by the mere fact that it is host to one of the more significant educational/research institutions in the world. Yet it is also true that many of the people that work in center-city institutions also live in the surrounding edge cities. This often results in what sociologist describe as privatization-- the tendency to isolate one's personal life from ones work or public life. Regarding religion, privatization often results in a religion that fails to respond to the crucial issues facing public life. This phenomena is more more popularly referred to as an application gap between Sunday and Monday.
3. Global Cruciality:
The church of the 21st century will increasingly become a church that reaches diverse nationalities and ethnic groups. The census bureau has predicted that 79% of all population growth in the U.S. will be in ethnic minority groups. A church eager to grow and have an impact in culture will therefore be eager to reach such ethnic groups with the gospel of Christ. Since the center-city is the place where multiple nationalities and ethnic groups first come together, a center-city/regional model for church planting has the best opportunity of reaching diverse nationalities and ethnic groups.
This is important in at least three ways: First, As John Murray has noted, "Was it not the commission of the Savior on the eve of His departure from this world, "Go ye therefore and disciple all the nations"(Mt.28:19)? (See John Murray, "The Church and Mission", The Presbyterian Journal February 2, 1977.) We therefore consider it a crucial element of our heavenly mandate to reach the nations that have gathered to New Haven with the Christian gospel. Second, most first generation immigrants will come to the city and only in subsequent generations begin to move out. A church is in a much better position to plant ethnically suitable satalite ministries respecting certain obvious language or cultural barriers from within the center city than from outside the city. Statistics demonstrate that many of these first generations churches will eventually migrate into surrounding areas providing opportunities for even more church plants. Third, New Haven boasts of a major, international education center with the existence of Yale in the heart of downtown. This provides us with the opportunity for reaching and training the future international leadership of our multi-ethnic global community. Yet in order to have a significant campus influence and outreach, it is almost imperative that we meet within walking distance of students and resident faculty, many who simply don't own cars.
4. Mutli-Cultural Cruciality:
The scripture teaches that the present church is like a Bride being made ready for Christ in heaven. Therefore, our present "school of discipleship" would seek to most resemble the heavenly church in its various aspects. Among other things, the heavenly church will be characterized by its diversity in terms of human categories. Christ's bride will consist of a people that were once both urban and suburban types; upper, middle and lower income groups; multiple ethnic groups; etc. In short, the heavenly church will transcend all worldly categories and distinctions because of its common bond of fellowship in Christ.
This is why one the most distinctive doctrines of the new covenant is that it would form an assembly of all nations, recognizing "no distinctions."(See for example: Acts15:8-9; Romans 3:22-25, 10:12; James 2:2-4) And a center-city/regional approach to church planting provides us with the best opportunity for breaking down many of the human biases that present barriers to our becoming more heavenly in our present experience. Such a strategy for church planting provides us with a profound vision for accomplishing this new covenant distinctive in such a way that the world will take notice and be more assured of the reality of our faith in Christ. We want a strategy, then, that makes no distinctions or preferences. We seek to have a profound and eternal kind church, that if successful, will depend entirely upon the grace of God working in us, since no other human or social explanation could adequately explain our love one for another.
Conclusion:
Our strategy does not seek to minimize the significant anthropological distinctives, many of which present real "barriers" to immediate unity. (Such as some cultural barriers, linguistic barriers, etc) Where barriers do exist, we will target them with special strategies, but not so as to maintain that statas quo in anthropological disunity, but toward overcoming those barriers so as to foster greater appreciation of anthropological distinctions as they cooperate and unite together for the Glory of Christ! Quite simply, we glory in a church that as much as possible will look on earth as it will be in heaven!
