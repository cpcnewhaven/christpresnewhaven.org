Basic Methodology

Preston Graham, Jr.

I. A Review of First Principles From Which Interpretative Goals Are Derived:

1. Textual Inspiration: Meaning is located in the text itself- its historical and literary characteristics,-- not in the author or the reader of the text.

2. The Dual Nature of the Bible: "The Word of God in the words of men"

a. As the Word of God:

1) Meaning is singular representing the one Mind of God
2) Because the Bible is "God's Word" it has eternal significance.

b. As the word of men:

1) Every text represents a historical and linguistic particularity that should be treated seriously in interpretation.

3. The Role of The Holy Spirit

a. The Holy Scripture IS the special revelation of God the Holy Spirit and corresponds to the singularmeaning of the text as it has been codified into the text itself.
b. The reader is illuminated by reading the Scritpure when the Holy Spirit works in the readers affections so as to make him/her willing to hear God's word as applied to him/herself-- and this corresponds to the significance of the text to the reader.

4. The Role of the Church:

Meaning and Significance has been entrusted to the corporate assembly of God so as to necessitate an ongoing conversation between the individual interpreter and the corporate interpretation which transcends individual bias, national and ethnic biases, generational biases and historical biases.

II. Our Four Main Goals:
Exegesis: The careful and systematic process toward the discovery of the original , intended meaning of a Bible passage so as to preserve the Lordship of God as exercised through scripture.

Exegetical Reflection: The deliberate attempt to compare individual exegesis to the history of interpretation as it has been applied to the particular text.

Theological Reflection: The deliberate attempt to place the discovered meaning into a confessional context so as to interact with a respectable corporate tradition.

Hermeneutics: Carefully drawing good and necessary inferences from the original, intended meaning of a Bible passage so as to discover its significance for the reader(s)


III. A Basic Process:
(The art, not science, of interpretation in that all steps will act upon the other so as to frustrate any attempt at a strict "flow chart" in interpretation)

A. Pray-- Our affections will largely help or hinder our interpretation in so far as we are willing to submit to God. Only God can create in us such a disposition that is genuinely willing to hear God's voice as He speaks through His Word. "Create in me a clean heart O God... !

B. Have access to Three Translations-- You will want to recognize that you are at a significant disadvantage if you cannot work in the original languages since you will be attempting to study what amounts to a non-inspired text. You will also want to understand that there are different theories applied to translations. (Literal, dynamic equivalence, free, etc.) Furthermore, there are two major sources for the Biblical text itself. (Textus Receptus and Eclectic) Whereas you will not be able to escape these extra-Biblical realities, you can at least minimize these realities if you work with the following texts:

  • So as to have access to the Textus Receptus text, secure a New King James. (The text of the New Geneva Bible)
  • So as to have access to a more literal translation based on an ecletic text, you will want to have access to a New American Standard Bible.
  • So as to have access to a dynamic equivalence translation based on an eclectic text, you will want to have an Revised Standard (or New Revised Standard) or New International Version) Whereas I would make this my primary text, you will want to be very careful since more decision are being made by the translators, sometimes obscuring the original. (For example, why did the NIV impose a theology of "flesh" such as in their translation of Rom. 8, or why did they obscure the very significant fact that Paul choose to use one word "boast" three times in Romans 5:1-5, 11 as earlier used in Rom. 3:27, etc.? )

C. Pick Your Passage-- The fundamental unit of all language is not the word, not even the sentence, but the self-contained unit (paragraph or series of related paragraphs with a discernible point) Look for the beginning and end of an argument, narrative or poetic piece of scripture. For instance, this "self-contained unit" may consist of a single sentence in the case of a proverb or perhaps several chapters of Scripture in the case of an historical narrative. (as in Genesis for instance)

D. Do a General Study of the Book as a whole--This is very important-- and is why I prefer expositional series so as to not exempt this very important step in exegetical method-- a step that takes quite some time so as to be hard to do every week or so... You will want to answer questions like:

  • Who wrote the book?
  • What where the historical circumstances? I.e. when, where, how, why, etc.
  • What were the political factors?
  • What was the culture of that day?
  • To whom was the book written?
  • Was there a controversy that is being addressed?
  • What is the general theological themes emphasized in this book?
  • Look for key words often repeated and discern their "technical" use by the author.

As a rule, to understand the book as a whole, you will want to:

  • Read the Book several times, getting the "big picture" even as you attempt to discern a general outline
  • List the words most frequently used and do both thematic theological studies and word studies.
  • You Should Consult:
    a. The Introduction in a Good Commentary
    b. A Bible Encyclopedia (G.W. Bromiley, ed. The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia, Eerdmans, 1979)
    c. A Biblical Survey/Introduction
    - New Testament: A Survey of the New Testament, Gundry
    - Old Testament: Introduction to the Old Testament, Harrison

E. Discern and Understand the passage in its Redemptive Historical Context-- This presupposes you know something about the various Redemptive covenants and their relationship to each other. (A whole lesson will be devoted to this later) You will want to consult some good biblical theologians (Calvin, G. Vos, M. Kline, A. Hoekema,H. Ridderbos, T. David Gordon, Huggenburger, Beale, etc.)

F. Literary Analysis--

  • Given your general outline of the book, where is your passage?
  • What is the mainpoint before and after your passage?
  • How does the author make the transition to your passage? Notice the key transitional words: - therefore, moreover, but, otherwise, since, etc.

G. Sentence Flow-- Again, you will be at a disadvantage here, since the translators will have already made decisions, which is why you will want to compare your dynamic with your literal translations. But generally, you want to notice the relationships between clauses (not verses, or sentences, since these may be artificial anyway) Your goal is to understand the"logic" inherent to the passage. Where is clause going in relation to the others, etc.

  • Try to distinguish between subordinate and main clauses. (Notice particularly the verbs-- main verbs vs. supporting verbs)
  • Try to distinguish the various relationships between clauses in your passage. (see attached)
  • Two methods: (See attached:
    Discourse Analysis: Philippians 2:19-30
    Sentence Flow: 1 Peter 1:3-6

H. Word Studies-- You will want to make sure you understand the significant words in your passage. Don't assume you know the meaning, since again, you are working with a translation which is most likely at an eighth grade level. Also be careful NOT to fall pray to the "root word fallacy." (see below) Therefore, you will want to consult a good commentary here. You may also do your own, provisional word study in the following manner.

  • Distinguish the Greek word behind your English translation
    a. Consult a Young's Analytical Concordance, look up the English word and find the Greek or Hebrew word that corresponds to your particular verse.
    b. Using your concordance, look at the various ways your word is used in the Bible. This is called a "range of meaning." (Imagine a X were your word shows up in other passages and fill in what the context suggests it means)
    c. You will want to be particularly careful to notice how the word is used by your author.
    d. Consult a good "lexicon" (dictionary) if you can discern the Greek or Hebrew word in it.

  • Be careful here:

    a. Don't over analyze a word
    b. Don't use etymology studies.
    c. Words mean one thing at one time, the bible isn't "mystical" so as to play games with words... Like all communication, the Bible intended to say something, not anything!

I. Biblical Context-- By "Biblical" I mean how does your passage, specific clauses or words, compare to other passages in the Bible. Are there verbal similiarities (Quotations, special uses of words). You will want to further ask:

1. Is your passage derived from another passage in the Bible?
Sometimes your bible will actually help you some by noting in the margins cross-references.

2. If you locate an cross-reference that your author has intentionally used, you will want to go back and understand the OT passage in its original context and meaning. Often times, the author will intend to import the whole contextual meaning of the previously revealed text merely with the use of an abbreviated phrase taken from that passage.
Often times, you will discover that your passage assumes knowledge of previous revelation-- the prophets will assume familiarity with the Mosaic law for instance (especially Deut.), NT writers will assume familiarity with both prophets and Moses. But rarely will they directly quote from previous books. You will there need to see if key phrases or even words are used in other portions of the bible.

3. How does your passage compare with the cross-references? Are words used in similar ways? If there is variance, what does it accentuate so as to provide a theological emphasis?

J. Draft a Mainpoint-- Attempt to summarize the plain meaning of your passage, taking into consideration all the above. IF possible write it down in one sentence. If you are a reader of Puritan sermons, this will correspond to what they labeled, "doctrine."

K. Confessional or Theological Context--Remember that you have been looking at the "trees" so to speak, you will want to now compare your particular "tree" to the "forest." Theology, as such, is attempting to know what the Bible as a whole teaches concerning various important themes/topics. You will want to compare your "mainpoint" and its implications to the whole of scritpure. So as to do this, you will want to compare your conclusions to traditional interpretations as derived from Church history. For instance, if your passage pertains to the whole idea of "sin" , a good theological tradition will summarize the idea of sin, especially as this has been understood by centuries of corporate interpretations by people authorized and qualified to interpret scripture.

1. Determine the major theological topic that is being addressed by your passage?
2. Compare this to a faithful Christian tradition of your choice.
Examples: Westminister Confession of Faith, Heidelburg Catechism, The 1869 London Baptist Confession, Belgic Confession, The Thirty-Nine Articles
Notice that I suggested corporate creeds and confessions representing corporate interpretation of scripture. This is not to despair readings in theology by selected and reputable authors.

L. Significance-- You will want to carefully limit your application to only that which naturally and genuinely may be derived from the mainpoint. In some cases, your text may have "secondary" points, but they will related to the mainpoint in an obvious way. Your "application" then ought to be governed by the mainpoint. Also remember that many passages will not have a "to do" application. It may be informative in so far as you better understand the nature of your salvation such that you will be more thankful, for instance. Some questions you may want to ask are, although not always intended to be answered by your text:

1. What does the mainpoint infer about your relationship to God?
2. What duty does this passage teach in your relationship to God, to people?
3. How might I change my understanding of God, His ways, redemption, etc. so as to "grow in spiritual understanding?
4. How does this passage challenge assumptions in my culture?
etc.

Conclusion: Again, I want to stress the importance of humility! You are not the first person to study your passage. Moreover, the advantage to corporate over against private interpretations is that your own social, personal and historical biases are more likely to be exposed for what they are so as not to mislead you in your conclusions. Just as a good sermon would want to work hard at both exegetical and confessional study, so you are best served to do the same. Moreover, nothing will assist you more than to find a church whose pupit is committed to exegetical preaching whereby proper methods in Biblical interpretation is being modeled by someone trained in the discipline, and hopefully under accountability to some confessional standard .
