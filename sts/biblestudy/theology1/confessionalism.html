Creeds and Confessionalism
Study Guide

1. What is a Creed?

A "creed" is any summary and/or clarification of a certain belief. The word "Creed" is derived from the latin word "belief." Therefore, a Christian creed is an interpretation of the general teachings of the Old and New Testament Scriptures categorized into general themes and topics of relevance to the Christian faith.

2. What is Confessionalism?
Refers to the practice of subscribing to a given creed used as the standard and authoritative interpretation of Scripture by a given corporate assembly (or church). The Creed is then used to determine mature faith as required of its officers and in the nurture and discipline of its members. It is NOT used a terms of communion, at least not in its totality. Nor does subscription mean that every point of the confession must be believed in--even as prerequisites to being ordained into an office. Rather at the discretion of the relevant governing body of the church (the assembly with original jurisdiction) some teachings may be "scrupled" yet so as not to violate the integrity of the confession as a system of faith.

3. What is the relation of Creeds to Scripture?
WCF xxxi.3: All synods or councils, since the apostles' times, whether general or particular, may err; and many have erred. Therefore they are not to be made the rule of faith, or practice; but to be used as a help in both.

WCF i.10: The supreme judge by which all controversies of religion are to be determined, and all decrees of councils, opinions of ancient writers, doctrines of men, and private spirits, are to be examined, and in whose sentence we are to rest, can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the scripture.

Creeds, then, are never believed in themselves or for themselves, but insofar as they accurately portray the teaching of scripture. In our ministers' ordination vows, for instance, ministers are asked:
"1. Do you believe the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments, as originally given, to be the inerrant Word of God, the only infallible rule of faith and practice?
2. Do you sincerely receive and adopt the Confession of Faith and the Catechisms of this Church, as containing the system of doctrine taught in the Holy Scriptures?
3. Do you approve of the form of government and discipline of the Presbyterian Church in America, in conformity with the general principles of Biblical polity?"

4. What is the relation of Creeds to the purpose of Scripture?
It is the message of the Bible which saves; not the words. The Bible is not magic; it is a true message. Its effect comes from understanding and believing what it teaches.
1. 1 Cor.1.18: "For the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved, it is the power of God." This "word of the cross" is not scripture, because the NT scripture has not yet been written at the time Paul writes this. The "word of the cross" is the message that Jesus died for sinners and was raised for their justification. People who believe that are saved. By contrast, if a person reads the Bible but does not understand it to teach that Jesus died for sinners and was raised for their justification, such an individual is not saved.
2. 1 Cor. 14.2,3: "For one who speaks in a tongue speaks not to men but to God; for no one understands him, but he utters mysteries in the Spirit. On the other hand, he who prophesies speaks to men for their upbuilding and encouragement and consolation." Here Paul makes it clear that edification cannot take place apart from understanding.
3. 2 Pet.3.15b-16: "So also our beloved brother Paul wrote to you according to the wisdom given him, speaking of this as he does in all his letters. There are some things in them hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other scriptures." Here we see that reading the scriptures without understanding what they teach actually leads to destruction. The "unstable" could be destroyed by reading the scriptures, if they are not helped to understand them correctly.

5. How does the use of creeds correspond to the mission of the church?
The general mandate of Scripture concerning the church's ministry of education is given in Matthew 28:18-20. After commanding the Church to "make disciples," Christ further clarifies that this commission includes teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you." From this commission and elsewhere in scripture, the mandate has at least 3 characteristics.
a.Apostolic: (note that the commission was given exclusively to the apostles who as given the exclusive authority to lay the foundation for the New Covenant assembly. See also Mt. 16)
Acts 2:42 "they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and prayers."
Eph.2:19-20, ...you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ himself being the cornerstone."
2 Timothy 1:13, Follow the pattern of the sound words which you have heard from me, in the faith and love which are in Christ Jesus.
2 Thess.2:15, So then, brothers and sisters, stand firm and hold fast to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by our letter.
1 Tim. 4:6, If you put these instructions before the brothers and sisters, you will be a good servant of Christ Jesus, nourished on the words of faith and of the sound teaching that you have followed.
1 Tim. 6:3, Whoever teaches otherwise and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that is in accordance with godliness...
2 Tim.4:3, For the time is coming when people will not put up with sound doctrine, but having itching ears, they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own desires.
b. Comprehensive:(note "all" things...)
Mt. 28:20, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you...
Acts 20:26-27, Therefore I declare to you this day that I am not responsible for the blood of any of you, for I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God.
c. Ecclesiastical:
(Note that this commission assumes 1) "teaching" 2) a government ["teach them to obey] and 3) sacraments ["baptism"]. In other words, the great commission presupposes the "church" as is clearly stated elsewhere in scripture--a church by definition consist of three marks-- apostolic doctrine, apostolic government and apostolic sacraments corresponding to the three offices of Christ [prophet, priest and king] as mediated in this present age by his Holy Spirit through the "gifts to men" Eph.4)
1 Timothy 3:15, the household of God... the church of the living God, the pillar and bulwark of the truth.
Eph. 1:22, And he has put all things under his feet and has made him the head of all things for the church, which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all.

The church, then, is to be the guardian and witness to the truth. The use of creeds are an essential aspect of this witness whereby:
a. Ministers in a creedal church are accountable to a publicly-adopted standard.
b. The truth that IS taught is more likely to be God's truth.
All preachers are fallible; all are capable of exercising poor judgment, and all have limitations of intellect. Why is it that the preachers in confessional churches are less likely to make mistakes than those in other churches? Because they have been disciplined by a confession; required to express opinions that have interacted with others.
c. Christian unity may be fostered as in "one faith... "(Eph 4)
d. False teaching may be held to account so as to protect God's people from being tossed to and fro by every wind of doctrine. (Eph.4)

9. What are some of the profitable uses of Creeds in the Church?
A.A. Hodge describes how the use of creeds in all ages of the church were useful in the following four ways:
1. To mark, preserve, and disseminate the attainments made in the knowledge of Christian truth by any branch of the church in any grand crisis of its development.
2. To discriminate the truth from the glosses of false teachers, and accurately to define it in its integrity and due proportions.
3. To act as the bond of ecclesiastical fellowship among those so nearly agreed as to be able to labor together in harmony.

8. What are some of the typical objections to the use of Creeds and our response?
1. Creeds are of human invention
However, an English translation of the Bible is itself a human work, not done by inspiration, and capable of error. Commentaries are human works, yet many anti-creedalists read (and write!) commentaries. A sermon is a human work, yet many anti-creedalists listen to them and/or preach them, recognizing that the Bible authorizes some to teach others and to proclaim God's Word.
2. Creeds violate liberty of conscience
a. No one is required, by pain of law, to join a particular communion. If one disagrees with a church's creed, one may join another church, with which one agrees.
b. If merely being exhorted or encouraged to interpret the Bible a particular way is an infringement of conscience, then a sermon is also a violation of liberty of conscience.
c. As stated by the creed itself, (Chapter 20) the creed ought not be used to violate liberty of conscience, nor to separate from fellowship those who are otherwise of a credible profession of faith. As part of our "creed", we believe that our communion ought to be open to as many as will be in heaven with us. Whereas the creed represents a "mature" reading of scripture, it does not represent the "terms of communion." Subscription to it is only required of those who are to be leaders in the church. Otherwise, subscription as to membership is only in so far as is necessary for a credible profession of faith
3. Creeds stifle inquiry.
This is manifestly not the case. The most productive and creative theologians who have ever lived have been members of confessional churches. Further, particular confessions have been modified as the result of such study.
4. Creeds hinder Christian unity.
To some degree, this third objection is an objection against denominationalism on the grounds that it fosters disunity. It is said that denominationalism (subscription to creeds) keeps the church from experiencing the kind of unity that would be possible if we would simply put aside our differences and just experience God and his love. The fundamental error implicit to this objection is a definition for unity that is foreign to the Bible. It assumes a definition of unity that stresses a particular organizational structure or method. This "organizational" unity is defined in terms of sharing a common "method" or "technique" which is often called a philosophy of ministry. This unity is not the unity of the Scripture.
Phil. 1:27, "stand fast in one spirit with one mind"
1 Cor.1:10, "speak the same thing and be on one accord of one mind"
Philip. 2:2, Make my joy complete, be of the same mind, having the same love, being in full accord and of one mind.
Eph. 4:4, "There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism.
Eph.4:13, "until all of us come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the son of God.
5. Creeds make to big of a deal over "non-essential" doctrines?
a. As previously noted, it ought to be the goal of any church to have a creed that represents the comprehensive teaching of Christ as revealed in the apostolic faith. It is a fundamental violation to the great commission to reduce our "belief" to merely "some things whatsoever" that Christ has taught. We in effect play God when we begin to determine what portion of His word is important and what portions are not.
b. As also noted, having a comprehensive creed ought not to be confused with terms of communion. It is true that only that part of our "creed" that is essential to a credible profession of faith ought to be applied in admitting people into membership of a given church. This is not to say, however, that the other teachings of Scripture are not important, this would be to reduce the church to remaining as a child in spiritual wisdom and understanding.
c. An "essentials" argument tends to represent a modern "anti-intellectualism" that is not representative of the Biblical understanding of Christian growth. Scripture makes it clear that to increase in faith is necessarily related to an increase in spiritual knowledge. How else can faith increase except in response to the truth of God and his promises?
Rom. 12:1 I appeal to you therefore, brothers and sisters, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship. 2 Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your minds, so that you may discern what is the will of God--what is good and acceptable and perfect.
2Cor. 10:4 for the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power to destroy strongholds. We destroy arguments 5 and every proud obstacle raised up against the knowledge of God, and we take every thought captive to obey Christ.
Phil. 1:9 And this is my prayer, that your love may overflow more and more with knowledge and full insight
Col. 1:9 For this reason, since the day we heard it, we have not ceased praying for you and asking that you may be filled with the knowledge of God's will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding,

4. Creeds fail to achieve their desired goals.
It is true that confessional churches are not yet perfect in doctrine or life. Yet, the fair question to raise is whether confessional churches are ordinarily more or less sound than non-confessional churches; not whether they are perfect.

Addendum:
The Westminster Confession of Faith and the Larger and Shorter Catechisms
by T. David Gordon

A. The Work of the Westminster Assembly.
Called by the "Long Parliament," the assembly had no ecclesiastical status, and only advisory status to the Parliament ("nor shall they in this Assembly assume to exercise any jurisdiction, power, or authority ecclesiastical whatsoever, or any other power than is herein particularly expressed"). It met from July 1, 1646 to February 22, 1649. 151 men were invited to the assembly, including 121 ministers, ten lords and twenty commoners. On average, about sixty were in attendance at any given time. Six of these 151 were from Scotland (Robert Baillie, George Gillespie, Alexander Hamilton, Samuel Rutherford, Adoniram Byfield, and Henry Roborough), and these commissioners wielded an influence substantially disproportionate to their numbers. The Assembly met 1163 times, normally beginning at 9 am and concluding by 1 or 2 pm.
By an ordinance enacted by the Long Parliament on June 12, 1643, the Assembly was called because of the recognition that the Reformation in England had not yet adequately addressed errors in church government and worship ("as yet, many things remain in the liturgy, discipline, and government of the Church, which do necessarily require a further and more perfect reformation than as yet hath been attainedthat the present Church-government by arch-bishops, their chancellors, commissars, deans, and other ecclesiastical officers depending upon the hierarchy, is evil, and justly offensive and burdensome to the kingdom, a great impediment to reformation and growth of religion, and very prejudicial to the state and government of this kingdom"). The Assembly's primary purpose then, was to correct errors in government and worship, though it was also charged with "the vindicating and clearing of the doctrine of the same (Church of England) from all false aspersions and misconstructions." Its first work was the Form of Government and the Directory of Worship, then it wrote the Confession of Faith, and finally, the Catechisms.
Every member of the Assembly took the following vow: "I do seriously promise and vow, in the presence of Almighty God, That in this Assembly, whereof I am a member, I will maintain nothing in point of doctrine, but what I believe to be most agreeable to the word of God; nor in point of discipline, but what may make most for God's glory, and the peace and good of this church."

B. The Influence of the Westminster Assembly.
In England, the work of the Assembly had only a short-lived official influence. By 1660, scarcely a decade after the completion of the Assembly's work, Charles II destroyed the Commonwealth which had been established by Cromwell, and restored Anglicanism as the state Church.
In Scotland, the Assembly's work was adopted in several acts of the Scottish Parliament sitting at Edinburgh in 1647, 1649, and 1660.
In America, the work was adopted with minor modifications in 1729 and 1789, with the establishing of the first American presbytery and General Assembly, and it has also been employed by many presbyterian bodies since.
With modifications, it was adopted by Baptists in London in 1677 and in Philadelphia in 1742. Spurgeon withdrew from the Baptist Union of England and Ireland when, in 1888, they repudiated the Confession. It was adopted by English Congregationalists with minor variations in 1658 in the Savoy Declaration, and by various American Congregationalists in 1648 (Cambridge) and 1680 (Boston) and 1708 (Saybrook, Connecticut).It ought further to be noted that the Shorter Catechism was printed in New Haven as early as 1786 representing the confessional backbone of Puritan New England.
The influence of the Assembly has been broad and deep in the English-speaking world. John Leith says that "the number of children who received their religious instruction from the Shorter Catechism must be estimated in the millions (p. 12)." This is all the more remarkable since there was little effort expended to make it easy. Benjamin Breckinridge Warfield's testimony is clear on this point: "Its framers were less careful to make it easy than to make it good. As one of them, Lazarus Seaman, explained, they sought to set down in it not the knowledge the child has, but the knowledge the child ought to have" (See Warfield's "Is the Shorter Catechism Worth While?", the first paragraph).
