Perspectives on the Work of Art

by Christopher Clay

(Given at the CPC "Christian Faith And Practice" Lecture Series, Spring, '99)

 

The following conversation took place about twenty-four hundred years ago in Athens:

"And so, Glaucon, when you happen to meet those who praise Homer and say that he’s the poet who educated Greece, that it’s worth taking up his works in order to learn how to manage and educate people, and that one should arrange one’s whole life in accordance with his teachings, you should welcome these people and treat them as friends, since they’re as good as they’re capable of being, and you should agree that Homer is the most poetic of the tragedians and the first among them. But you should also know that hymns to the gods and eulogies to good people are the only poetry we can admit into our city. If you admit the pleasure-giving Muse, whether in lyric or epic poetry, pleasure and pain will be kings in your city instead of law or the thing that everyone has always believed to be best, namely, reason.

"That’s absolutely true.

"Then let this be our defense — now that we’ve returned to the topic of poetry — that, in view of its nature, we had reason to banish it from the city earlier, for our argument compelled us to do so. But in case we are charged with a certain harshness and lack of sophistication, let’s also tell poetry that there is an ancient quarrel between it and philosophy.... Nonetheless, if the poetry that aims at pleasure and imitation has any argument to bring forward that proves it ought to have a place in a well-governed city, we at least would be glad to admit it, for we are well aware of the charm it exercises. But, be that as it may, to betray what one believes to be the truth is impious. What about you, Glaucon, don’t you feel the charm of the pleasure-giving Muse, especially when you study her through the eyes of Homer?

"Very much so.

"Therefore, isn’t it just that such poetry should return from exile when it has successfully defended itself, whether in lyric or in any other meter?

"Certainly.

"Then we’ll allow its defenders, who aren’t poets themselves but lovers of poetry, to speak in prose on its behalf and to show that it not only gives pleasure but is beneficial both to constitutions and to human life. Indeed, we’ll listen to them graciously, for we’d certainly profit if poetry were shown to be not only pleasant but also beneficial." (Plato, Republic, X.606E-607E, trans. by G. M. A. Grube)

How should a Christian, whose true citizenship is in the Kingdom of God and not in Plato’s Republic, respond to Socrates? Plato is famous, or rather infamous, for banning the poets from his ideal city because poetry can arouse emotions which subvert the control of reason through intense emotional affect. In our own century we are very well aware that in an authoritarian state art which harnesses the emotions poses a real threat to the regime and is inevitably censored or repressed. Another reason Plato banned the poets was because poetry — as the chief representative art form in addition to music and painting — merely created bad copies of the perfect Ideas or Forms that existed in Heaven. Reason or the intellect was the best means of access to these Forms which lay beyond the physical realm. Reason provided the foundation for metaphysics or theology, and art had nothing to add to our knowledge of the heavenly realm. At least, that is the standard view of Plato’s attitude to art. What is so interesting about the passage from the Republic cited above, however, is that Plato seems to complete his final indictment of art with a wistful admission of poetry’s lingering attractions, and even seems to hope that if the proper argument can be marshaled then poetry might be welcomed back from exile.

Like many great thinkers, then, Plato takes a stand and follows what he thinks is the truth, but is still haunted by doubt. Like many great thinkers, he admits a tension into his system. His valorization of reason is haunted by the riddle that is art. Great art indeed is a great mystery, though for this very reason, I will suggest, it should never be excluded from our lives. In this paper I shall attempt to respond to Socrates and speak in prose as a lover of poetry, arguing why I think art has a central role to play in our lives, in our culture, and in our state. I have chosen Socrates as a starting point because he is a great example of a man who is not a Christian but is richly endowed with the common-grace willingness to entertain a contrary opinion. In the passage cited here he seems to exhibit that wonderful Hellenistic grace of hospitality when he speaks of welcoming the lover of Homer into his midst. For me there is a very personal lesson here, because at a time in my life when I was a lover of other Homers and thought literature alone might suffice for ordering and living one’s life, I was in fact welcomed into the Kingdom of Christ. Socrates also embodies some of the predilections that are common in our culture today — a combination of respect for a vaguely articulated sense of the divine and a belief in the transcendent power of the intellect. As I mentioned earlier, the transcendent power of the intellect is the ability of reason to establish metaphysical or theological principles for living one’s life.

The title of this paper is "Perspectives on the Work of Art," which suggests that art may be viewed from a variety of perspectives and that there are no transcendental or omniscient perspectives able to see and understand everything about the nature of art. When I speak of art, I mean all the arts, from architecture and music to poetry and painting and myriad other arts. While I will suggest that the experience of mystery in the experience of art intimates something that is transcendent, I do not wish to imply that the artist or the critic’s perspective on art can be transcendent. In our own time, we are far too willing to make gurus out of critics and artists. Indeed, there is no final word on art — if there were you certainly wouldn’t be listening to me give it to you — and in discussing art there is no alpha and no omega although perhaps for this very reason much art is very preoccupied with beginnings and ends, with origins and eschatons. Each person’s perspective is partially rooted or restricted by matters of time and place, history and culture. The articulation of each individual perspective is like the light of the sun shining on the earth — at any one time some parts are illuminated while others remain obscure, left in the dark. Each interpretation reveals some things, and leaves others concealed. Later on I’ll return to these two issues of the origin and interpretation of art, but for now I simply want to suggest that for most people, the question, "What is art?" is one of those eternal, immutable, and yet ultimately unanswerable questions of life. Christians, I will suggest, ought to share this view. This paper is about the nature of art. Art is a question, and it is a question that can not be answered. My task is to articulate the question, "What is art?" The challenge is to see the question, and by returning briefly to Socrates and one of the original discussions of art that has so deeply influenced subsequent history, I wish to reawaken a sense of the question. What counts as a work of art, and what kind of art should we favor? Do we need only hymns to God? When I speak of the "work of art" I mean not only artworks but also, in the most literal sense, the work that art does. What does art do, or accomplish, and how does it do it?

In addressing all these questions, it is helpful to step back and inquire into the interrogative situation which prompts all these questions about art. Why do we ask in the first place? When we consider our framework for asking the questions, we go a long way towards answering them. This paper is derived from a talk given at Christ Presbyterian Church of New Haven as part of its "Christian Faith and Practice" series, in which we discuss the place of Christians in our world and in our culture by hearing one individual discuss his calling. I myself am a student of modern Japanese literature. (In speaking of Japan modern literature generally refers to the literature of the last one hundred twenty years or so.) Another way of describing what I do, however, is to say that I look at the phenomenon of modernity as experienced by the Japanese. I look at modernity and its interaction with art. We are all familiar with the word modernity though, which ought to suggest that what I do is inherently comparative and always seeks to relate itself to the experience and discussion of modernity in other cultures, and of particular interest to me is that of European modernity.

In our tradition of Reformed orthodoxy, we have a great appreciation for the sphere of common grace. This means that we highly esteem the contribution to humankind that emerges in the work of all people, whether Christian or non-Christian. One of the first and best articulations of the meaning of common grace can be found in Calvin’s Institutes:

"Because knowledge of the arts is bestowed indiscriminately upon pious and impious, it is rightly counted among natural gifts.

Whenever we come upon these matters in secular writers, let that admirable light of truth shining in them teach us that the mind of man, though fallen and perverted from its wholeness, is nevertheless clothed and ornamented with God’s excellent gifts. If we regard the Spirit of God as the sole fountain of truth, we shall neither reject the truth itself, nor despise it wherever it shall appear, unless we wish to dishonor the Spirit of God. For by holding the gifts of the spirit in slight esteem, we condemn and reproach the Spirit himself. What then? Shall we deny that the truth shone upon the ancient jurists who established civic order and discipline with such equity? Shall we say that the philosophers were blind in their fine observation and artful description of nature? Shall we say that those men were devoid of understanding who conceived the art of disputation and taught us to speak reasonably? Shall we say that they are insane who developed medicine, developing their labor to our benefit? What shall we say of the mathematical sciences? Shall we consider them the ravings of madmen? No, we cannot read the writings of the ancients on these subjects without great admiration. We marvel at them because we are compelled to recognize how preeminent they are. But shall we count anything praiseworthy or noble without recognizing at the same time that it comes from God? Let us be ashamed of such ingratitude, into which not even the pagan poets fell, for they confessed the gods had invented philosophy, laws, and all useful arts. Those men whom Scripture calls "natural men" were, indeed, sharp and penetrating in their investigation of inferior things. Let us, accordingly, learn by their example how many gifts the Lord left to human nature even after it was despoiled of its true good." (Calvin, Institutes, II.ii.14-15; trans. by Ford Lewis Battles)

Calvin makes it very clear that Christians can learn abundantly from the work of non-Christians, both in science and in art. But what is the truth in art? What contribution to truth is made by art, or what is the truth that happens in a work of art?

In turning to this question, it is important to apply Scriptural wisdom, and for this we can turn to another conversation that occurred in Athens about nineteen hundred years ago:

"Then Paul stood in the midst of the Areopagus and said, ‘Men of Athens, I perceive that in all things you are very religious; For as I was passing through and considering the objects of your worship, I even found an altar with this inscription: TO THE UNKNOWN GOD. Therefore, the One whom you worship without knowing, Him I proclaim to you: God, who made the world and everything in it, since He is Lord of heaven and earth, does not dwell in temples made with hands. Nor is he worshiped with men’s hands, as though he needed anything, since He gives to all life and breath and all things. And he has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings, So that they should seek the Lord, in the hope that they might grope for Him and find Him, though He is not far from each one of us; For in him we live and move and have our being, as also some of your own poets have said, "For we are also His offspring." Therefore, since we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Divine Nature is like gold or silver or stone, something shaped by art and man’s devising. Truly, these times of ignorance God overlooked, but now commands all men everywhere to repent, because He has appointed a day on which He will judge the world in righteousness by a Man whom He has ordained. And He has given assurance of this to all by raising Him from the dead.’ And when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked while others said, ‘We will hear you again on this matter.’ So Paul departed from among them. However, some joined him and believed, among them Dionysius the Areopagite, a woman named Damaris, and others with them." (Acts 17.22-34; NKJV)

This passage of Acts is truly remarkable, and has taught me a great deal about art and humanity. For one thing, Paul assumes that all people are religious. Either you’re worshiping the true God, or you’re worshiping an idol. The Bible never recognizes anything like the supposedly comfortable atheism of our modern world whereby, the thinking goes, some people just don’t believe in religion. On the contrary, Paul insists, everyone must believe in something as a means of constructing their world-view and determining what to do with their lives. Everyone proceeds in life with some kind of belief or faith that they are on the right path in life. And the reason people must have faith for all this is because there are no guarantees in life that anyone’s path will be a successful means of reaching whatever is their end goal. Disaster or failure are always lurking around the corner; no one is ever in total control though once again it is one of the big lies of modernity to pretend that this is not the case.

One response to this situation of life where there are no guarantees that things will turn out fine is to feel a sense of alienation. Oftentimes the feeling here is as if we have been thrown into our bodies and tossed into a hostile natural world, and to hope for deliverance from a divine being. This was partly the view of Plato, and was definitely the view of Paul’s day. It is the world view or idolatry of Gnosticism. Sometimes Gnosticism wants to hope for this deliverance by literally seeing the divine being in the natural world around us and worshiping that, and this might be called monism. And sometimes Gnosticism wants to say that we’ll be delivered from this terrible life by a transcendent deity.

In our own day various forms of Gnosticism have returned with reckless abandon. One example is the Heaven’s Gate cult which hated the human body, called it a vehicle, and thought that an alien spaceship of higher beings was going to rescue them and assimilate them into the next level of existence. It sounds like Star Trek and this view is warped, but Christians are often all too ready to castigate such world views. Gnosticism is a bit of hobby-horse among Christians nowadays, but what is required here is the equanimity and understanding of Paul and Calvin. In preaching to the Gentiles at the Areopagus, Paul observes the cultural practices of the Athenians and notes that they are striving to reach for God but that they do not know God. In our day the situation is no different even if there are no statues dedicated to the unknown god. Every time people demean their bodies through reckless sex or drug use, people are searching for something like the unknown God, that thing which might rescue or complete them. While Paul points out that the idea of God dwelling in rocks or statues is utterly contrary to the character of the omnipotent creator of the universe, he is very careful to note that there is truth or the beginnings of a right instinct in the cultural products of the Athenians. Here is where a high view of common grace is so essential to any student of culture. Paul notes that the Athenians’ poetry expresses a yearning or searching for God, and in a sense condones the poet’s statement that in God "we live, and move, and have our being." Because of common grace, Paul has something to work with or appeal to in preaching the gospel and making it relevant to the Athenian cultural context of his day. And this in no way delimits the sovereignty of God to work in the hearts of a few and change their lives. As the narrative rather matter-of-factly reports, some mocked Paul, others were intrigued, and some became believers. Paul was respectful in the way we all must be in witnessing for the gospel, and his starting point was the particular way in which Athenian culture expressed its religious yearnings through art. For me, the lesson here is very personal, because I was one who was perhaps content to worship the beauty of art and be satisfied with art as the reaching out for transcendence. Even then, though, it was never enough for me. The story of my life does not end with art and neither does Paul’s speech; the Apostle concludes with the prophetic call for repentance, a recognition that one’s life is a reaching out for God, a groping for the Lord of all life and beauty and truth. Paul’s message is to run to Christ, and it is interesting that even if the text of Acts is an abbreviated version of Paul’s speech, the dramatic drive of these words can hardly be understated. God isn’t merely the ground of our being as some like to say, but He commands us to repent now and put our faith in Christ. In the Authorized (King James) Version of Acts, the entire two-verse message of repentance is translated as one long sentence, which serves to heighten the dramatic intensity of the speech as it concludes with the final assurance of the faith that is given to us by the historical fact of the resurrection. Consider the passage again in the Authorized Version: "And the times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men everywhere to repent: Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." (Acts 17.30-31, KJV) Paul’s language here runs to Christ. I myself have only a passing familiarity with Paul’s Greek, but I do know that his sentences are long and fast and furious, brilliant and intense. Open any of Paul’s epistles and listen to the dramatic tension of his language. Open up any of Paul’s epistles and count how many sentences end with "Jesus Christ," or "Christ Jesus." Listen to these familiar lines from Romans and hear the sustained dramatic tension whose consummation is Christ:

"Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword? As is it written, For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter. Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 8.35-39; KJV)

Paul’s sentences literally run to Christ. This shouldn’t surprise us, it is the greatest story ever told. And part of the impact of the letter is the style: Run to Christ, nothing will keep us from Him.

A high view of common grace, then, is essential to appreciating the truth in art, which is often to give expression to the yearning for transcendence that is so characteristic of the human condition. The understanding that all people are religious is an indispensable tool for analyzing a culture’s artwork. One origin of the work of art is the longing for transcendence, the desire to exercise or participate in the mystery of aesthetic form and beauty and to see what that might have to teach us about ultimate reality. And as we saw in the case of Paul and the Athenians, such exercises or works of art usually mix truth and error. What I do in my own work is discuss cultural products such as essays, novels, and films. In each case, I try to discern the world-view that is motivating that novel or essay or film, and ask how that world-view is related to the style of the work, what part of the work is truth and what parts should be exposed as idols or arbitrary constructions. In general, I try to argue for the Christian insistence that all modes of living are based on faith in something, and I should add that our present-day cultural context of "postmodernity" is more receptive to this argument than earlier cultural moments were. In any case, I hope to help lay something of a groundwork for people’s confrontation with the inescapable truth that even they have faith in something and are looking for something.

Every work of art, I suggest, is an existential, narrative positing about the future or about ultimate reality. Let me explain what I mean by that. If every person, as Paul and the Bible insist, lives his life based on some kind of faith, then every person has a faith or hope for how their life will turn out. We see our lives as a story or narrative, and have a certain faith or hope about how it will end up. We also trust and hope that our present mode of existence — understood as the choices we make — is the right one. By existential I mean that the mystery and the beauty of art are related to the inability of people to give clear and perfectly logical reasons for why they live the way they do. Unlike Socrates, I think it is impossible to derive a logical reason for every decision you make about how you live your life. I should point out, though, that Socrates retreats or compromises a bit by positing the gods and the Forms, but how he knows either of these exist he never really does a good job of explaining. My point is that too high a view of reason destroys the place for art because art participates in mystery, which is so antithetical to reason. Faith in some morality or rule or truth then, is also based on a certain mystery, and it is also a faith regarding how your life with end up. This faith, as I have said, has a narrative element to it because of the concern over how life will turn out. Every faith consists in a story about the future, and that is why so many faith traditions tell stories. Every faith is eschatological, it projects itself forward, and so to express that faith in art is to link it with the mystery of aesthetic beauty. In the work of art, two mysteries often coexist: one is the existential faith about living your life, the other is the exercise of aesthetic form, harmony, or beauty. These two will coexist in most art forms. Let me borrow an example from architecture, in which the interpretive moves are made with reference, again, to the concept of narrative.

Consider the Marquand Chapel at the Yale Divinity School, where our church worships on Sunday mornings. Walking into Marquand Chapel, your vision is immediately subjected to a narrative of sorts. From the back of the sanctuary your eyes notice the long, rectangular form of the room, and your sight is guided horizontally forward to the end of the room by noticing the patterned placement of pews which stop at the front, just like the columns going down the two aisles also stop at the front of the room. Your eyes, then, are drawn to the front and center of the Chapel, and your line of vision then moves on a continual vertical line of oscillation between the domed ceiling and the communion table. The domed ceiling has small windows around the perimeter and lets in light from the outside, and a large lantern is suspended from the middle of the ceiling and draws your eyes back down to the communion table. The natural light from outside the domed ceiling and the lantern represent the coming of transcendence to the focal point of the communion table. Please don’t misunderstand me to be saying that we confuse the architecture with the actual transcendence. What I am saying is that there simply couldn’t be a more powerful narrative metaphor for focusing our attention on the communion table as a place where something transcendent occurs, a place where we experience the real, authentic, living presence of God. My point is that the best art that expresses our yearning for transcendence is completed by the special event of the Christian service where we meet with God. Let me also add that if you think a sermon is just a lecture or if you think that the Lord’s Supper is just a commemorative event — nothing special is going on, God’s presence is not being communicated to us — then art and its metaphorical power are merely decorations appended to such a commemoration. My point is actually very serious: a rationalism which leaves no place for the mystery of transcendence spells the death of art and leaves no real place for God. Likewise, an equal and opposite total mysticism which confuses everything that is beautiful with God starts worshiping the creation and not the Creator. But if you have a theology of presence, then the Marquand Chapel is a powerful narrative metaphor for transcendence that prepares us for the authentic presence of God, which is communicated to us every Sunday in the preaching of the Word and the administration of the Sacraments. The great yearnings expressed in art are only fully answered by the presence of God. Surely, Reformed Christians have the highest appreciation for the arts because we neither belittle it as do the rationalists nor idolize as do the mysticists. We need more art that lets us dream of utopia, and we need more Christians to witness to the transforming effect of living for Christ.

One problem in interpreting art is in interpreting instrumental music or more abstract visual art, which is usually an exercise in matters of form. I’m afraid I don’t have enough time to address this problem adequately, but in passing I’d like to point out that many people have pointed to Beethoven as the one artist who did more to express the human emotional condition than perhaps any other artist, even more than perhaps Michelangelo or Shakespeare. All such judgments are debatable, but I want only to point out that such a high estimation of a musician is conveyed once again using the metaphors of speech and communication: Beethoven had something to express or communicate, and he did so using the language of music. The problem in interpreting or judging abstract art is of knowing the grammar of the language, and oftentimes abstract art rather willfully wants to confront us with a statement and not give us the grammar to decipher it.

Returning to my main argument, then, I have been suggesting that the work of art is an existential narrative. In a complacent and wealthy culture like our own, however, it’s very often difficult for people to believe this. But in difficult times, it sometimes is painfully obvious. I’m now going to say something about the character of modernity in very broad brush strokes as a way of saying a bit more about what concerns me in the study of modern culture and of what some of the issues are. As a student of modernity, I am drawn to the crisis years or cataclysm of modernity represented by the decades leading up to the second world war. In this area the experience of Japan has many affinities with other western nations who felt a keen sense of reckoning in the 1920s and ‘30s. People of this time felt as if the ideals of progress and decency that had previously so motivated and inspired people had suddenly come crashing down as a result of the first world war. Today it is shocking to us that people could call a war like the first world war "the war to end all wars" but that was in fact the case, and people who honestly felt that way were bound for a fall. Of course, disaffection had been gathering steam in the nineteenth century ever since countries began to industrialize, and out of this disaffection came the two great prophets of alienation — Karl Marx and Sigmund Freud. Marx said we’re alienated from one another because the capitalist system manipulates and exploits workers like machines without any regard for their welfare, and Freud said that we’re alienated from ourselves because we’re suppressing instincts and suppressing traumas we suffered as children. We shouldn’t just throw out Marx and Freud indiscriminately, however, because there are insights they have to make which are truthful and interesting to the Christian, such as a consideration of how capitalism works and what it does to people and how it needs to be regulated. Christians, too, point to a certain sense of homelessness or alienation in the world, recognizing that the present reality of this world does not represent the best situation or home for ourselves. Our citizenship is in Heaven, as the author of Hebrews reminds us:

"By faith Abraham, when he was called to go out into a place which he should after receive for an inheritance, obeyed; and he went out, not knowing wither he went. By faith he sojourned in the land of promise: For he looked for a city which hath foundations, whose builder and maker is God. Through faith also Sara herself received strength to conceive seed, and was delivered of a child when she was past age, because she judged him faithful who had promised. Therefore sprang there even of one, and him as good as dead, so many as the stars of the sky in multitude, and as the sand which is by the seashore innumerable. These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. (Hebrews 11. 8-14; KJV)

The problem for people in the late nineteenth century was that in this climate of alienation it seemed as if a culture of reason or nihilism had defeated the culture of Christianity, and this led to some violent swings of attitude among Europeans and Japanese and also Americans.

The advent of nihilism in modernity is really perhaps unprecedented in the history of the world, and represents one of the defining features of late modernity, by which I mean from the late nineteenth century into our own time. In many respects, though, it is the end-point of a culture of rationalism and reason. There are clear precedents of a fully articulated nihilism in some of Shakespeare’s characters, but the landscape of late modernity seemed to make nihilism inescapable or omnipresent. A culture that increasingly sees nature and the universe as entirely open to the inquiring intellect of humankind will steadily remove the old fortresses of mystery which people used to invoke in order to explain how the universe worked. An example is evolution. I am not offering any comment upon the truth or falsehood of evolution, but I would like to point out that the advent and acceptance of evolutionary theory in its original Darwinian model meant for many people that there was increasingly less mystery in the world and that what used to be a source of pleasurable or comforting speculation could be described quite readily according to certain models, and the value of that model was not very comforting because it said that all animals are nasty, and people are nasty animals. Thomas Hobbes’ famous observation that life is "nasty, brutish, and short" now seemed unavoidable. And this situation, seen as the progress of reason, made the world increasingly devoid of any meaning or value. Nihilism is the inability to see any meaning or value in anything: the universe has no God or center which guarantees the value of the creation, the universe itself is entirely indifferent to us. I think the emergence of this attitude in the late nineteenth century signals a real sea-change in the history of thought. It is as if we are all now Hamlets, and it should come as no surprise that is was in the late nineteenth century that Shakespeare’s play was "discovered" as the greatest piece of English literature. Whereas to the heretical or gnostic mind we are thrown into an antagonistic, anti-human nature, the nihilist says we are tossed into an indifferent universe. This latter case represents the real abyss, because to the Gnostic if nature is antagonistic then the path in life is clearer, but if for the nihilist the world is utterly indifferent, then no direction for life can be elicited from nature. This is the abyss of freedom so common in existentialist plays like Jean-Paul Sarte’s "No Exit." The gnostic world view, as fantastic as it sometimes was, made a bit more sense, because the self seen as pitted against a demonic nature is a bit more consistent.

As people like Heidegger tried to suggest, however, the position of nihilism raises a certain paradox: somehow an indifferent universe let us arise in its midst, and we do care about how we live and whether we live. Somehow an unconscious universe gave rise to our conscious nature, and shouldn’t the universe therefore be qualified by this fact? The strange rupture between man and reality at the bottom of nihilism condemns man to stare at himself in isolated selfhood. Isolated from the universe, and isolated from others because there he is totally free and unable to choose how to live. Very often, however, the modern nihilistic self likes to trade itself in for a monistic view that constructs the self as the same as nature, as being delivered from nature by nature. In America, many strange perversions of Eastern religions feed the need for the nihilistic self to convert to a monistic self. This view, however, abolishes the humanity of man, our peculiar place in the world as self-conscious beings, beings who ask why we’re here and who long for a transcendence that does not erase our humanity but rather completes it.

As Hans Jonas observed, the modern mind hovers between the Scylla of nihilism and the Charybdis of Gnosticism or monism. How should Christian artists and critics, then, respond to this situation? Earlier I cited a passage from Hebrews which affirmed that we are strangers and pilgrims on this earth, but this is really only half the story. The Scriptures also affirm that the creation is God’s and we belong to Him, so that we are at home in it. The gospel is about freedom from the bondage of sin and the desiring of sin. The gospel is not a straitjacket of moralisms because it is about the renewal and redemption of the world. We have been set free by God to be what He made us and intended us to be: sons and daughters of God who delight in Him and in His creation, who battle against sin, hunger after righteousness, make peace, help the oppressed, raise children, share the joy and beauty of the creation, learn about the creation, steward the creation and teach all this to others. In short, we have been set free to be creatively working for the redemption of the earth, and artistic creativity is just one such creative act of the free Christian.

So can there be Christian art, bearing its witness in the world alongside other art? Indeed Christian artists and critics should bear witness, but what does this mean? It certainly doesn’t mean only painting or writing or sculpting biblical themes. Christian art is very simply this: good art. Christian art is good art as understood by Christians. Christianity is about the renewal of life, and it is therefore about the renewal of art. Earlier I outlined some of tendencies of the modern mind towards nihilism or Gnosticism. Where these views are expressed or championed, Christians must offer a comprehensive vision of a renewed life. Art that is obsessed with graphic violence and sex is not good art because these preoccupations betray the need of a nihilistic culture for ever more shocking "virtual realities" to jolt itself out of the complacent indifference of a nihilism that can’t see any real value in the basic things of life, and doesn’t have a transforming and comprehensive view of the world. It is for Christians to express what is meant by life and humanity, by vitality and a sense of beauty. But please do not misunderstand me to be suggesting that art is a kind of Christian propaganda; while art is a free act of human creativity, art need not especially seek to justify itself. There is no real dichotomy between the "fine" arts and the craft arts — that is a false dichotomy invented in the eighteenth century when the upper bourgeoisie tried to appropriate the arts as a mark of self-cultivation. Art in the service of this end became merely decoration. There is only good art and bad art, though as I have intimated there may be limits to how much certain art forms are able to communicate.

There are also norms for art, but they are no different than the norms for Christian life. Occasionally art may analyze an unfortunate plight or a sordid world or lifestyle — like "Hamlet" or the movie "Boogie Nights" — but that should be to make a point about the futility to be found there. There is an art that we favor in the same way that there is a lifestyle that we favor. The attitude involved in being essentially human, in possessing the humanity which Christ came to restore and complete, is summed up by Paul in the letter to the Philippians. Paul exhorts the church at Philippi to consider whatever ever things are "true, honest, just, pure, lovely, gracious, excellent, praiseworthy, and virtuous." These are the norms for life, and they are the norms for art as Christians seek to speak prophetically to the culture around them.

There are myriad more perspectives and expectations for the work of art. The perspectives on the work of art are as richly variegated as the common graces of this life, and it would be foolish hubris of me to simply continue by trying to catalogue them. I will close by suggesting that while there are occasions, particularly in literature, when Christians might give a more explicitly Christian testimony about their lives if they’re so inclined, for the most part there really isn’t an especially Christian art. T. S. Eliot, the greatest Christian poet of the century, was also simply one of the greatest poets of the century. (Rivaled only by Rilke?) But T. S. Eliot, when he wrote his poetry, testified less to the theological distinctives of the faith than to how those distinctives frame the great Christian exploration of the Creation that will be consummated by the final redemption of the earth when we are reunited with the triune God. In the poem "Little Gidding," the title of which refers to a prayer colony in northern England, he put it this way:

With the drawing of this Love and the voice of this Calling

We shall not cease from exploration

And the end of all our exploring

Will be to arrive where we started

And know the place for the first time.

Through the unknown, remembered gate

When the last of earth left to discover

Is that which was the beginning:

At the source of the longest river

The voice of the hidden waterfall

And the children in the apple-tree

Not known, because not looked for

But heard, half-heard in the stillness

Between two waves of the sea.

Quick now, here, now, always—

A condition of complete simplicity

(Costing not less than everything)

And all shall be well and

All manner of thing shall be well

When the tongues of flame are in-folded

Into the crowned knot of fire

And the fire and the rose are one. (T. S. Eliot, "Little Gidding," Four Quartets)

 

Christian art testifies to our delight in the free exploration of the Creation. The brief arresting epiphanies of the beauty of the Creation — "half-heard in the stillness / Between two waves of the sea" — testify to the actual incompleteness of the Creation which will only be completed at the final consummation of the Kingdom when for those who are in Christ "all shall be well." In the startling rhythms of these concluding lines, Eliot somehow manages to combine the excitement of the Christian journey with a restful confidence and anticipation of the final consummation of history. The synthesis is extraordinary and to my knowledge has never been equaled. This is art its finest because it is the Christian view of the world given one of its most exquisite articulations: an excited, delighted exploration of the world that simultaneously rests in Christ. This is not a work of art which seeks to validate Christianity, it is rather the work of a Christian which validates art.

One of the characters in C. S. Lewis’ Till We Have Faces remarks, "The sweetest thing in all my life has been the longing to find the place where are all the beauty comes from." To this we add that it is MOST intellectual to be a Christian, it is MOST artistic to be a Christian, and it is MOST human to be a Christian because to do anything other than fix your eyes upon God is to close off that part of your humanity and that part of your soul which longs for authentic transcendence, for your soul’s real completion and renewal. Only God can complete your humanity by making you free to follow him, to live for him, to explore his creation, and to rest in him, because as Paul wrote to the Church at Phillippi, "our citizenship is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ." (Phil. 3.20; KJV)
