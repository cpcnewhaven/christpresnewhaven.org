Are you like me in that you have fantasized about living in the past? Even one trip to the 19th century setting of Sturbridge Village effects me so as to wish for days long gone. What is it? Perhaps it is that I have this thing for animals, or that I like to put my hands in dirt. ( I doubt it!) Of course, when I consider many of their hardships, my fantasy quickly subsides. No doubt, I would readily accept the complexity of modern life with penicillin, than say the simplicity of pre-modern life without it. Yet I am all the same intrigued by the simplicity of pre-modern life-- especially as it seems to be more conducive to many of my Christian values.

One of the values that modernity seems to work against is the sacredness of life-- all of life as an integrated whole rather than a splintered and disjointed complexity of work, family and church. This is it-- the thing that most attracts me to a pre-modern life is how it seems as though people then lived but one life and not many lives. Rather than having one life with family, another life with work and yet another life with church, it seems as thought the three spheres where more integrated so as to overlap and even be more conducive to the benefit of the other. Then, our work was with our family-- as each member played an important role in the family economy. It would have seemed odd to "plan" time with my family. Now, I "leave" my home to go "to" work, even if only five minutes away. I must make a huge effort even to know my kids, much less to know them meaningfully. Then, my work was in a village (albeit farming or township) with people that I worshipped with on Sundays. diaconal care, as it where, was the village co-op so as to integrate family, jobs and church. Now, my church relationships are largely splintered from my work relationships, etc.

As I think about all this, I suppose it presents a real challenge to Christian discipleship. As a Christian I realize that my primary calling is to follow Christ so as to live holy and good lives in all spheres of life. Moreover, just as all three spheres of job, home and church are aspects of God's calling, these ought then to be integrated as under the common lordship of Christ and therefore must be viewed as in their relation to each other. What all this means practically, is that I should work against the fragmentation of life as it where. And since our modern economy doesn't help us much here, we will need to make a special effort to do this in at least two ways. I offer these suggestions if per chance you will be doing some "new year" planning. (And I hope that you will!)

1. We need to find balance-- We cannot make plans in one sphere without a consideration for the other spheres. Before we say "yes" or "no" to anything, we must consider our callings as related to our family, work and church as given our unique circumstances and gifts. Among other things, this will require that we talk with those significant persons in all three spheres before we make decisions related to any one sphere. We will need to discern our unique role as related to each of these areas of life and seek balance so as to plan time and resources for each.

2. We need to integrate better-- We should think about ways to make one sphere work for another sphere rather than against it. If you are a parent, how can you include your children in your work, or be included in their work. Perhaps you could participate in a school project with your kids, or sign up to read a book in their classroom, or join them for lunch... Look for creative ways for your children and spouse to spend time with you at the office or take them along on a business trip if possible. Regarding your family and church, how might you do things together as a family so as to serve the church? What kinds of things can we do as a church to be more involved in your family? And toward integrating work and church, what are some ways that the church might be more involved in your work and/or the people at work-- maybe a discovery group, once a month prayer meeting at work, perhaps being more bold to ask your work friends to church dessert parties or Saturday morning breakfasts, special church sponsored seminars on topics related to your workplace, etc.

 All this to say in a "pastoral" way-- "have a happy (which in Hebrew meant blessed) new year!"
