Arguments for Infant Baptism Summarized
by Preston Graham, Jr.

Introduction: On The Burden of Proof
In most every theological discussion this side of heaven, where perfect knowledge is not yet attainable, we "do our best" to determine what most accords with the teaching of scripture until proven otherwise. In other words, our goal is often not to "prove" a doctrine in some kind of absolute sense, but to establish the "burden of proof" whereby we may act and believe 100% even though we may have an intellectual confidence of say 60%. This is not an "intellectual" cop-out for the simple fact that it is most "intellectual" to admit of our finite reasoning about things which originates from an infinite mind. Simply put, there IS a reasonableness to mystery such that we are content to settle for a burden of proof on most things in theology. Therefore, we may act and believe 100% on the doctrine of the Trinity although we remain curious about many aspects about the Trinity that are mysterious to us.

The following "reasons" for infant baptism will not satisfy you if you are looking for absolute, intellectual certainty (nor would the reasons that are presented to justify anti-infant baptism for that matter) Rather, the "reasons" for infant baptism are presented so as to satisfy a "burden of proof"-- or, to ask the question, "what makes most sense given the Biblical data?" If at the end of the exercise, you are left with an intellectual certainty of say even 60%, you are then permitted to act and believe in infant baptism with 100% conviction. If you presently hold to an anti-infant baptism position, you will discover that as is the case with most theological shifts, beginning with perhaps your conversion itself, the shift will "feel" like a leap because it was made upon 60% intellectual confidence and you will have the experience of the remaining 40% uncertainty together with the simple reality that you have not yet been able to experience the reality of your new position. In time, you will "feel" more and more "comfortable" with your new views-- especially as you have the opportunity to ride them for awhile. (Kind-of like riding a horse that you have never ridden, while you may feel good about your riding skills, you are not yet sure what the horse may do)

One last note-- in the case of infant baptism, one possible reason why the view was more readily acceptable historically than in modern history is the modern transition from federal communalism to individualism as a way of life. For instance, although enlightenment individualism was beginning to shape more and more of early, revolutionary America, already the federal aspects of communal privileges had established the terms for citizenship in various levels of social contracts, etc. Therefore, in pre-enlightenment life, it was common-place for societies to be compacted upon the federal principle of representation whereby individuals had access to various social privileges simply for the fact that they were represented by some authorized person. (Ex. Children having as their "birth-right" the privileges of American citizenship based upon their parents status. Or in pre-modern "patriarchal" societies where all family members were given privileges based upon the fathers standing in society, etc.) That this was the case in Biblical times is also clear. Therefore, do not be surprised if the doctrine of infant baptism "feels" strange to you simply because it reflects this "federal" principle even as you live a social reality of an increasingly "individualistic" ideology.

The establishment of a burden of proof in favor of infant baptism:
As noted above, the "yes" or "no" concerning infant baptism will be resolved by the burden of proof. It is true that we don't have an explicit command to baptize infants in the New Covenant. It is also true that we don't have an explicit command NOT to baptize infants in the New covenant. The question of burden is then to determine which "silence" ought to be given priority. Our argument will be that both in view of several key passages and in view of the redemptive context of the New Covenant, the "silence" ought to be viewed as giving priority to the continuation of an older principle-- which is to understand that baptism functions like circumcision, as an entrance sign into to visible church (rather than as a personal" profession of faith") and ought to be administered to both believing adults and their children.

A Point of Clarification:
The "function" of infant baptism-- Is it to signify entrance into a visible community of faith community or is it evidence of personal faith?
1. A Brief statement of the anti-infant baptism view-- or "evidence of personal faith"

Since most people holding to the anti-infant baptism view will agree that their children are in some sense "members" of the ecclesiastical community so as to enjoy its care and oversight, the sacrament of baptism is then left to function as an evidence of personal faith (acting then as a profession of faith) indicative of personal conversion by the Holy Spirit, although not necessarily since there can exist "false conversions." (The only other option would be to attribute regenerative qualities to baptism itself with the Roman Catholics, which most Baptist would never do) The anti-infant baptism position will often refer to such passages as stated here:

Mark 1:8 I have baptized you with water; but he will baptize you with the Holy Spirit."
Acts 2:38 Peter said to them, "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ so that your sins may be forgiven; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.
Acts 2:41 So those who welcomed his message were baptized, and that day about three thousand persons were added.
Acts 8:15 The two went down and prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Spirit 16 (for as yet the Spirit had not come upon any of them; they had only been baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus).
Rom. 6:4 Therefore we have been buried with him by baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life.


2. Our View Clarified: or entrance into a visible community

We agree that one of the characteristics of the New Covenant is the promise of the Holy Spirit to all who believe. We also agree that as a "sign" one of the NC blessings signified by Baptism is the promise of the Holy Spirit and the corresponding "new life." What we believe, however, is that all this represents the message and purpose of the New Covenant community itself, rather than necessarily the experience of an individual. Therefore, to enter the New Covenant community is to gain entrance into that context where the New Covenant benefits will be preached and are made available upon reception by faith. The sign of baptism represents, then, the hallmark message of the new covenant community itself and is given to all who are in some relation to that community, which everyone seems to agree consists of all believers and their children. (Lest you believe children are outside the church which would by inference mean outside the church's care and oversight.) Therefore, we believe that the sign of baptism indicates entrance into the New Covenant community-- entrance which can either be the result of having a personal conversion into the Christian faith, or by God's sovereign will gaining entrance through birth into a family whose parents are members of the New Covenant community. It should be clear, then, that our view of baptism does not necessarily pre-judge a persons status with God by virtue of the indwelling Spirit. This, we all agree, is something which only God can do through regeneration as evidenced by personal faith and repentance. But we don't agree with the anti-infant view that the administration of the sacrament of baptism ought necessarily to correspond to when a person professes faith, and for the following reasons.



Why the Anti-Infant Baptism View Ought To Suffer The Burden of Proof: (Or why infant baptism is most agreeable to scripture.)

1. Under the Anti-infant baptism view, we would be left without an "entrance" sign into the Covenant community since circumcision is no longer required under the New Covenant.

If circumcision had remained, and baptism was merely added in the NC, it could be argued that baptism served a different function from circumcision so as to warrant a different administrative principle. But to the contrary, baptism did replace circumcision according to the NC authors such that it would appear to also function in a manner which is similar. The very fact that circumcision is no longer viewed as necessary, and baptism is, indicates that baptism has replaced circumcision-- Notwithstanding the clear associations that are in fact made between baptism and circumcision by the NC writers. For instance:

Galations 2:3:
But even Titus, who was with me, was not compelled to be circumcised ,though he was a Greek.

Romans4:11
He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised. The purpose was to make him the ancestor of all who believe without being circumcised and who thus have righteousness reckoned to them, 12 and likewise the ancestor of the circumcised who are not only circumcised but who also follow the example of the faith that our ancestor Abraham had before he was circumcised.

Col. 2:11-13
In him also you were circumcised with a spiritual circumcision, by putting off the body of the flesh in the circumcision of Christ; when you were buried with him in baptism, you were also raised with him through faith in the power of God, who raised him from the dead. And when you were dead in trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made you alive together with him, when he forgave us all our trespasses,


2. The Problem of New Covenant Continuity with the Abrahamic Covenant for the Anti-infant baptism view:

The New Covenant Community is presented to be in continuity with the Abrahamic Community, such that to change the constituency of the visible covenant community would need direct confirmation, which we do not have so as to exclude children. Moreover, in that baptism replaces circumcision-- this as known by direct confirmation, it would again be odd to change the administration of the entrance sign as other than how the entrance sign was administered under Abraham. Notice carefully the implications from the following scriptures:

Gen.17:10-12
And finally, circumcision in its day was applied not only to those who professed faith, but also to their children. Note, no one denies that baptism ought to be administered upon conversion into the Christian faith if they have never been baptised.

Galatians 3:8
Paul asserts that "the Gospel was preached before unto Abraham" meaning that the New covenant is essentially the same as the covenant with Abraham.

Romans 4:11
We also know that circumcision in its day signified and sealed the same realities which baptism signifies in its day: justification by faith and all the other benefits of redemption.

Colossians 2:11-12
This makes it clear that baptism not only signifies the same meaning, but has replaced circumcision as the initiatory rite into the visible church.

Galatians 3:27-28
As stated by David Gordon, "since the New Covenant unlike the Old, includes women, slaves and Gentiles without any additional rites or cleansings, it would be surprising for it to exclude those included in the Old Covenant (children of believers) and would be even more surprising if it excluded them without any mention of the exclusion."

Therefore, the New Covenant church originated from and remains in continuity with the Abrahamic Covenant such that the general constituents of the Old and New Covenant Church would be the same, unless we are told otherwise in scripture.

Acts 3:25
You are the descendants of the prophets and of the covenant that God gave to your ancestors, saying to Abraham, 'And in your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed.'

Gal. 3:6
Just as Abraham" believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness," 7 so, you see, those who believe are the descendants of Abraham. 8 And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, declared the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, "All the Gentiles shall be blessed in you." 9 For this reason, those who believe are blessed with Abraham who believed.


3. The Problem of Acts 2:38 for Anti-Infant Baptism Point of View:

There is the curious "future tense" that is used concerning the reception of the Holy Spirit in relation to "repent and be baptized..." The anti-infant baptism doctrine would seem to be more compatible with this verse if "baptism" was after, not before, the gift of the Holy Spirit. This is especially curious in light of the "promise" that is next stated in verse 39, "For the promise is for you, for your children, and for all who are far away, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to him." Our view states that this promise is for those whom "the Lord calls" Our view is comfortable with this "future tense" in that the promise of Acts 2:38 pertains to entrance into the New Covenant community and not necessarily a personal experience. It is certainly true that the only way for an adult person to enter would be to convert from one religion (as a Gentile or Jew) to the Christian religion-- this by the simply historical context where this was the very beginning of the New Covenant community such that every adult would be "converting" so to speak. But the language suggests that the promise was about the forming of new community (over against old Israel) whereby one of the benefits was the promise of the Holy Spirit. As such, Peter was comfortable with his "future tense" and his including even children as possible participants of this new community! Therefore, we would conclude that Acts 2:38-39 is more compatible with the view that baptism functioned to enter a person into the New Covenant community rather than necessarily indicating personal faith.


4. The Problem of Household Baptisms for the anti-infant baptism view:

What is troubling for the anti-infant position is NOT that household baptism's exist, since we know nothing about the age of those in the households. What IS troubling is the very fact that we DON'T know anything about the faith of those baptised except the head of the household. Now if the federal principle of representation was used to determine who received baptism, this absence of information would make sense. But if it was important to the New Covenant writers that each person baptised should have a personal profession of faith, it seems odd that no mention of it would be made. Likewise, it is troubling to think that not one of these households had young children. Therefore, the troubling absence of description in "household baptisms" concerning the "faith" of dependents given the anti-paedobaptistic views ought to at least cause us to question whether or not the same principle for administration that was used in all previous covenants wasn't in fact being used in these household baptisms.

1Cor. 1:14
I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius, 15 so that no one can say that you were baptized in my name. 16 (I did baptize also the household of Stephanas; beyond that, I do not know whether I baptized anyone else.) 17 For Christ did not send me to baptize but to proclaim the gospel, and not with eloquent wisdom, so that the cross of Christ might not be emptied of its power.

Acts 16:14-15, 25-34; 1 Cor.1:16
In each case, the text seems to emphasize the faith of the representative head of the home rather than the individual members of his household. The point is that while the text does not specify children or the faith of each member, the text is certainly not nervous about not mentioning either. The repentance and faith of the head of the household is clearly in view such that the whole household is baptized. For those who want to exclude those who by virtue of age cannot profess faith in Christ, it seems odd that the text seems to explicitly emphasize the whole family by virtue of representation rather than a profession of faith by each individual member of the household.


5. A Strange Duplication in Function Regarding the Lord's Supper and Baptism according to the anti-infant baptism view.

Under the anti-infant baptism view, a person is baptized under the same terms as they are admitted to the Lord's table. Again, in so far as there are only two sacraments, this view is absent an "entrance" sign since "communion with God" through a credible profession of faith is represented by both baptism and the Lord's table. A Biblical understanding of conversion would lend itself to the kind of instructions that Paul gives in 1 Cor.11:28, "examine yourselves..." Yet this kind of instruction is curiously absent concerning the administration of baptism, especially in contexts like the epistles where you would expect it since Acts is often more descriptive in form than instructional. It seems clear that baptism, like circumcision functions to enter a person into the visible community, but does not represent a persons own personal experience as say the Lord's supper does-- which explains the void of baptismal instruction likened unto 1 Cor.11:28 for the Lord's supper.



6. Problem for anti-infant baptism view regarding the meaning of baptism itself-- signifying the same thing as what circumcision did in its time-- anticipating a similar administration and function unless notified directly to the contrary.

a. Both representative of God's Covenant whereby Justification is through faith:
Often the anti-infant baptism view uses the argument that New Covenant blessings are by faith whereas circumcision represented a work such that baptism ought to accompany faith, etc. Unfortunately, this argument responds to an error prevalent in Paul's day rather than to the original and biblical understanding of circumcision. Clearly Paul argues, that circumcision, like baptism, was used on a people who were justified by faith through grace and not by works. Yet circumcision was administered to the dependents of those who had professed faith just as we would argue that baptism ought to be administered to children of believing parents as well. Notice, then, Paul's argument:

Rom. 4:11 He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised. The purpose was to make him the ancestor of all who believe without being circumcised and who thus have righteousness reckoned to them, 12 and likewise the ancestor of the circumcised who are not only circumcised but who also follow the example of the faith that our ancestor Abraham had before he was circumcised.

b. Both Symbolize Passing through Judgment into Salvation:
The symbols of "water" and "cutting" are intentionally used to signify a passing through the judgment of God. First, it will be shown through New Testament passages that the "water" signifies a passing through a divine judgment ordeal into either condemnation or salvation. This signification by the New Testament writers will be shown to be derived from the Old Testament significance of water.

New Testament Passages:
1. Matthew 3:11-12
John's ministry of water baptism is described to be symbolically representative of Christ's ministry of baptizing people with the Holy Spirit and fire. That is to say that the water is symbolic of judgment into salvation or condemnation. It ought to be noted, however, that John's application of baptism was considered unique. Moreover, it was never treated as a New Covenant baptism by the very fact that those having been baptized by John were required by the Apostles to be Baptized after the New Covenant promise of the indwelling Spirit. Therefore, while the significance of water is notable, we ought not to conclude anything from John about the application of baptism, if such issues relevant to application were even intended. All the same, "water" as a sign was to symbolize the coming of judgment.

2. 2 Peter 3:5-7 and 1 Peter 3:18-22
Both relate the idea of judgment by water as observed in Genesis flood to the day of judgment. In 2 Peter, the same waters which destroyed the wicked in Noah's day are shown to typify a future destruction by fire reserved for "ungodly men.
Yet in 1 Peter 3:21, Peter states that "this water (referring to the flood) symbolizes baptism that now saves you ...by the resurrection of Jesus Christ."
From one point of view, the water depicts judgment unto destruction. Yet from the vantage point of the elect, the same water depicts judgment unto salvation. And this is referred to as a "baptism" by Peter.

3. 1 Cor 10:1-2
In a similar fashion, the exodus through the Red Sea is spoken of by Paul in 1 Cor. 10:1-2 as a baptism in water. Paul writes, "our fathers...all passed through the sea, and ... were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea." For those who were represented by Moses in covenant with God, the waters represented salvation. Yet for those who were not represented by Moses but the Pharaoh, the waters of baptism represented curse and destruction.

Old Testament Passages:
1. Isaiah 43:1--4, Prophetic use of "water imagery" regarding passing through judgment into safety.
This passage speaks of god's redemption in terms of passing through waters with God into salvation. It prepares for the New Covenant use of water in baptism as already noted in 1 and 2 Peter.

2. Genesis 9:11
In Genesis 9:11 the Genesis flood is referred to as a "cutting off of the flesh" curse of the covenant. This is the same word "berith" used to depict God's covenant as signified in the OT by the cutting ceremony of circumcision. Clearly, the water is here being related to circumcision as described in Genesis 15, 17 and 22 where the practice of circumcision is intended to signify the judicial authority of God's sword.

3. Genesis 3:24
The sword imagery is first depicted in Genesis 3:24 where the judgment sword was given to the cherubim after Adam and Eve's expulsion from the sanctuary of the garden. Meredith Kline states, "In the hour that God drove man into exile it was indicated that any future return to God's dwelling place and the tree of life must involve a passage through the flaming sword of God's judgment." (Meridith Kline, Kingdom Prologue, p.85)

This image is later placed upon the great curtains which guarded the Holy of Holies from intrusion. However, on the day of Atonement the blood from a sacrificial substitute was sprinkled upon the cover of the ark as representing the passing through God's judgment into his Holy presence by means of a representative substitute. Therefore, participation in the rite of circumcision as depicted in Genesis 15, 17, and 22 represented a confession to be under the judicial authority of God. To be circumcised was ceremonially representative of gaining an entrance into that covenant community that had access in a specially revealed way to safe passage through the flaming swords of judgment into God's blessed presence.

Colossians 2:11-12, New Testament Verification of dual OT sign:
The New Testament evidence for relating baptism to circumcision is supported by Colossians 2:11,12 where circumcision is used as an analogy for baptism. In general, Paul is asserting that circumcision in its day signified the same realities which baptism signifies in the present day. In essence, the "water" of baptism signifies the same reality as the "knife" in circumcision.

Therefore, as depicted in the ministry of John the Baptist, water baptism as a sign represents the coming of a great judgment ordeal bringing salvation on the one hand and destruction on the other. For this reason, "baptism does not prejudge the ultimate issue of the individual's destiny one way or the other. It place him under the authority of the Lord for judgment and tells him that as a sinner, he must pass through the curse, yet it also calls him to a safe passage through the curse waters of the ordeal." (Kline, By Oath Consigned, p.82) Whereas circumcision represents a "cutting" ceremony and baptism represents a "drowning" ceremony, both represent the reality of God's judgment. In so far as a person goes through the judgment of God under the redemptive covenant, they pass through judgment into salvation.

Both these similarities in meaning at least point to the strong possibility that circumcision and baptism ought also to be similar in function. Again, this seems to put the burden of proof on those who would say otherwise.


7. The New Covenant treatment of Children in a general way as inclusive of the covenant community and its administration of the means of grace.

Genesis 17,
The promise is given to Abraham and all his seed. God's covenant with Abraham was none other than with a chosen representative of a large group of people. So too, in the covenant with Abraham, all of his descendants were born into the visible covenant community, that community which would provide access to passing through God's judgement into blessing. This was by virtue of their being represented by Abraham.

Luke 18:16,
When infants are brought to Jesus, he responds, "of such is the Kingdom of heaven". The children themselves are in view here, not simply those who are somehow "like" them as supported by the use of "such" in Acts 22:22. John Calvin comments that "if it is right for infants to be brought to Christ, why not also to be received into baptism, the symbol of our communion and fellowship with Christ? The placing of hands upon another was to bring them under a jurisdiction represented by the hands, in this case, the jurisdiction of the Messianic grace.

1 Corinthians 7:14,
Even if a child has as his/her parent just one believing parent, the children are considered religiously "holy." (The adjective is routinely employed throughout the remainder of the New Testament to designate believers or those who are the "saints" in reference to the visible church.)


Conclusion:
We find it difficult to believe that given the anti-paedobaptism position, the new covenant represents an all together different sociology with respect to the community of faith that was practiced under all preceding covenant administrations. Given the historic constituency of the covenant community as consisting of the children of believers, we would expect explicit commands against infant baptism if the terms of entrance where changed with the sign itself. This is to say that we find it easier to believe in infant baptism than anti-infant baptism so as to place a burden of proof upon our Baptist brothers and sisters to "prove" from scripture the contrary.

All the same, we recognize that those who differ with us concern this view do not necessarily differ concerning the fundamental teachings of the New Covenant-- we all agree that baptism IS the New Covenant sign and ought to be administered as a sacrament of the Church. At least historically, we agree that the sacrament of baptism ought to be administered by a visible church as representing an entrance into the church and the mediated presence of Christ through an authorized ministry of preaching, administration of sacraments and government. We therefore welcome our Baptist brothers and sisters to share with us in our Lord's Table while in this present age, since this is the ultimate and highest evidence of our genuine Christian affection for one another. And we take great pleasure that we will one day share in the immediate presence of Christ in heaven, where one of us will discover in the other a blush for our mistaken views on earth.
