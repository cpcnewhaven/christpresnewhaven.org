The Utility of Creeds and Catechisms in Christ Presbyterian Church
and "The Place of Theology in the Church"
Preston D. Graham, Jr.

Introduction:

Should the church use creeds and catechisms in its worship and education? Does the use of creeds contradict the "sola scriptura" (Scripture alone) motto of our tradition? Don't creeds lead to the kind of "dead orthodoxy" which has resulted in a dead end for many of the mainline denominations? And doesn't the use of creeds bias a person in their own study of scripture? These are some of the questions often asked regarding the use of creeds and catechisms. It might be noted that behind each of these questions are some very positive and noble concerns. Whether it be a concern for scriptural authority, vital and lively Christianity or freedom of conscience, I hope to demonstrate that these concerns are best protected and promoted with the use of creeds and catechisms, not without them. This paper, then, is a brief demonstration as to why the use of creeds and catechisms in the educational ministry of the church has always been one of the best ways to promote a true and comprehensive knowledge of God together with the corresponding privileges and duties of Biblical religion. An Addendum has been added to introduce the Westminster Confession of Faith and Catechisms-- the creed and catechisms that is subscribed to by our denomination, the Presbyterian Church in America.

For the purpose of definition, a "creed" is any summary and/or clarification of a certain belief. The word "Creed" is derived from the latin word "belief." Therefore, a Christian creed is an interpretation of the general teachings of the Old and New Testament Scriptures categorized into general themes and topics of relevance to the Christian faith. A "catechism, " is when a creed is broken down into a question and answer format for the purpose of further commentary or further reduction into a more simple and easy to memorize format. For instance, the "Westminster Confession of Faith" is a Creed whereas the Larger and Shorter Catechisms are the Confession of Faith put into a question and answer structure. The Larger Catechism then is a more detailed commentary and summation of the Confession of Faith whereas the Shorter catechism is a simplified summation of the Confession of Faith.

I. The Social Context of Modern Evangelicalism

Kenneth Woodward in an article entitled, "Dead End for the mainline" (Newsweek, August 9:1993), notes that "for twenty five years, liberal Protestantism's seven sister denominations have watched their collective membership decline... From every angle, mainline Protestantism is gripped by crisis: of identity and loyalty, membership and money, leadership and organization, culture and belief." For many evangelicals (ironically those who continue to believe in many of the classic doctrines of historic Christianity such as rebirth, the authority of scripture, etc.) , the reason for decline in mainline denominations is "denominationalism" itself. What is meant by "denominationalism" is basically the organization or "naming" of a given church around certain traditional "beliefs" or "creeds."

A distain for denominationalism is clearly articulated by one of the evangelical pastors interviewed by Woodward. "People today aren't interested in traditional doctrines like justification, sanctification and redemption." Rather, Woodward observes, "Steven Spielburg like Sunday school gimmicks to the generic Amy Grant music at worship services where everything is designed to meet the needs of his nondenominational babyboomers." "The aim", observed Woodward, "of this determinedly anti-denominational network is to lure baby boomers back to church by welcoming all comers regardless of their beliefs and appealing to their lack of theological convictions." (All emphasis mine)

Notice that it is not so much that the Bible has been rejected explicitly. Quite the contrary, these churches will often say something like "no creed but the Bible." Yet it is what the Bible is being used to say that is so novel. Rather than seeing a "message" in the Bible, what has become increasingly trendy has been to observe a "method" or a "psychology" in the Bible. (Regardless if one was ever intended I might add) Of course it is one thing for a church to reach out to people who don't believe , but it is yet another thing altogether to "appeal to their lack of theological convictions."

What then do these modern churches appeal to or organize themselves around if not theological beliefs or a theological "message." One alternative is what Robert Wuthnow has described in his recent book The Restructuring of American Religion. Wuthnow observes that it has become increasingly more likely for a church's identity and corresponding "structuring" to be in response to a given political or special interest agenda than to a certain set of theological "beliefs"-- or what may be described as the politicization of the church. Yet there is also another kind of restructuring going on according to David Wells in his book No Place for Truth , or Whatever Happened to Evangelical Theology. Like Wuthnow, he observes a restructuring of American religion. For Wells however, this restructuring goes beyond the political, it also involves a therapeutic, democratic and technologic element. As was observed by Woodward, what is common to the restructuring of American religion is the increasingly avant garde "marginalization of truth and theology" in deference to more "modern" categories. Again, it is not so much that evangelicals cease to believe anything theological, it is rather that belief is no longer central to an identity and program!

What has become "center stage" in many evangelical churches according to Well's is "Self-Piety", "Everyperson" and "Technique." Each of these three trends have a decidedly "anti-creedal" impetus underneath it in so far as they are each anti-confessional. The result is a restructured church around three very modern social trends. By self-piety, he means the shift from objective truth to inner experience as the focus of religion. It is what Wells rather tongue and cheek further describes as a movement from Descarte's "I think therefore I am", to the Freudian, "I feel therefore I am." In other words, " the experience of God has displaced the truth of God as more fundamental." The question that must be asked is to what degree is this trend the result of an honest inquiry into the Bible from which our spirituality is derived, and how much of it is simply a mimicking of modern sociology. If Philip Rieff is correct in his The Triumph of the Therapeutic, Uses of Faith after Freud, this psychological church is simply the socialization of Freud's "psychological man" in modern culture. The question that this raises is whether or not the Bible ever intended experience to be fundamental to the Christian faith. And how would all this impact our desire to use creeds and catechisms in doing objective theology from the Bible.

In the "rise of everyperson" Wells means "the person who is the product of the American experience of democracy, the person for whom democracy is not simply a political system but an entire worldview and for whom, therefore, culture and truth belong to the people."(p. 189) As a result, the custodian of truth as moved from the church as represented in its learned representative leadership to "within the jaws of popular consensus." Daniel Boorstin describes this trend to be such that "democracy made society into a mirror where people saw the way things were and made that the measure of the way things ought to be." It is what the noted historian Nathan Hatch has described as The democratization of American Christianity. The predominate symptom of the rise of everyperson according to Wells is the "middling standard." That is, with the shift of objective theology to a theology by consensus, the truth that is taught has been reduced to whatever everyone is willing or able to believe. Quite significantly, the rise of "everyperson" has also been coupled with the emergence of a new populist medium for building consensus, namely the TV. As a result, the "cultural mantle has passed from the users of words to the makers of images" says Wells. Again, what needs to be asked is who does the scripture intend to interpret guard and proclaim the "deposit of faith"? Was popular concesus ever intended to be the guardian of truth? And how would the "rise of everyperson" impact our inclination to use creeds and catechisms as written by trained theologians and subscribed to by church courts?

Thirdly, this modern "marginalization of truth and theology" is depicted by the movement away from confessionalism (where belief is central to identity) and toward technique and strategies-- what is often described as a philosophy of ministry. Of course the question this raises pertains to the meaning of discipleship if not "to teach them all things that I have commanded you." Now what is most interesting about all this is the fact that it again suspiciously parrot's what is taking place in modern society in general. It is what Peter Berger et. al in The Homeless Mind has described as "bureaucratization." This is the modern trend that has more and more reduced life into methods and flow charts, simple five point strategies, etc. It is a world and worldview created by the modern "Technological Society" as described by Jacque Ellul. Again, it ought to be asked, how does all this line up to the Bible. What is central to Scripture in promoting the gospel, a method or a message? And what effect would this modern "bureaucratization" have upon our desire to use creeds and catechisms in the church and teaching our children at home."

These descriptions of the "margianalization of theology" suggest that the present reaction against "denominationalism" and the use of certain "creeds" (statements of beliefs) toward church identify and education might very well be more the product of modern sociology than any real interaction with the Bible. Perhaps then, it is modernity itself, and its therapeutic, technologic and democratic trends, that function as the primary player in a reaction against the use and utility of creeds rather than any serious study of scripture-- which leads us to the next section. Rather than being swept away by the avant garde trends of modernity, perhaps we must carefully rethink what the Bible teaches concerning the church's mission to "make disciples" and how the scripture teaches this will be accomplished.

II. A Biblical Perspective On Christian Education

The general mandate of Scripture concerning the church's ministry of education is given in Matthew 28:18-20. After commanding the Church to "make disciples," Christ further clarifies that this commission includes teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you." The question then is not should we be teaching Biblical truth, but rather "what is the content of "all things..." and what method is most suitable for teaching it? As T. David Gordon notes in "the Education Ministry of Christ Presbyterian Church" (from an unpuplished document from the session of Christ Presbyterian Church of Nashua, NH), "Novelty was not valued in the New Testament, but faithfulness to the authoritative, foundational instruction of the apostles was." (p.1) I am endepted to the above mentioned document for the general categories and some of the content of this section-- original quotes are noted.

A. The Content:

1. Apostolic Doctrine:

The substance of sound, faithful teaching was described in Scripture to be Apostolic. For instance, Acts 2:42 affirms that "they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and prayers." The following is a Biblical analysis of why this was so. The basic idea is that Christ mediated his authority through the teachings of the apostles. In other words, the teachings of the apostles were to be received as the teachings of Christ.
  • Mt. 16:18-19, ...upon this rock (the apostolic confession of Peter that Christ is the Messiah), I will build my church, and the powers of death will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of Heaven, and whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven."
Christ's Messianic authority was to be mediated through the foundation laid by the apostles. This foundation consisted of the Church's government, mission, worship, and authoritative teachings or doctrine. (Therefore, the term "apostolic doctrine.")
  • Eph.1:22, and he has put all things under his feet and has made him the head over all things for the church which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all.

  • Eph.2:19-20, ...you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ himself being the cornerstone."
Clearly Paul understood the apostle's work and teachings to be foundational. This "foundation" consists of the church's doctrine, government, mission and worship upon even a simple analysis of the things which the apostles taught.
  • 2 Timothy 1:13, Follow the pattern of the sound words which you have heard from me, in the faith and love which are in Christ Jesus.
  • 2 Thess.2:15, So then, brothers and sisters, stand firm and hold fast to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by our letter.
These passages clearly teach that what Paul and the apostles taught were meant to be followed as from Christ Himself. What are the "pattern of sound words" which Timoth would have heard from Paul? Do we limit these "sound words" only to what is now considered to be "essential" teachings? Or must we included such topics as the context of 2 Timothy directs to us?
Therefore, Apostolic doctrine could be defined as that doctrine given to us by Christ and under His authority by the Apostles. It should be noted that the teachings of the Apostles are not to the exclusion of the Old Testament or the Gospels, but rather the culmination of these teachings as they are fulfilled in the New Covenant context. While the Old Testament is still our Bible, the Apostle's teachings interpret it, like the teachings of Christ, under the New Covenant context. The Old Testament is indeed fulfilled and concluded in the Apostolic teaching.

One can therefore say that the content of our instruction ought to be consistent with the apostolic doctrine. This kind of teaching was referred to in scripture as "sound" or "faithful."
  • 1 Tim. 4:6, If you put these instructions before the brothers and sisters, you will be a good servant of Christ Jesus, nourished on the words of faith and of the sound teaching that you have followed.
  • 1 Tim. 6:3, Whoever teaches otherwise and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that is in accordance with godliness...
  • 2 Tim.4:3, For the time is coming when people will not put up with sound doctrine, but having itching ears, they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own desires.
2. Comprehensive: The whole counsel of God:

The instructional ministry of the church should also attempt to cover the full range of inspired truth, from Genesis to Revelation. It must not deliberately avoid any aspect of the truth which is contained in the Scriptures. Moreover, it must take great pains to know the complete teachings of Scripture and teach them to God's people. In other words, we ought to teach comprehensively rather than just the exclusive doctrines of our own "pet interests." As we shall see, this particular obligation of the church is extremely relevant to the use of creeds.
  • Mt. 28:20, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you...
  • Acts 20:26-27, Therefore I declare to you this day that I am not responsible for the blood of any of you, for I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God.
3. Confessional: Knowledgeable obedience as the Goal of Biblical Content:

The teaching and instruction of the Church is not merely an exercise in academic pursuit, but should be taught as a matter of personal confession and obedience. The church teaches in order that one might know what to believe (not just know) and what ought to be obeyed. Therefore, knowledgeable obedience is the goal of the Church's educational ministry. The commission therefore requires that the church teach them to obey... One historical way to categorize this kind of teaching and learning is that it be confessional, namely, teaching that seeks to build ones personal faith and obedience rather than mere intellectualism.
  • Rom. 6:17, But thanks be to God that you, having once been slaves to sin, have become obedient from the heart to the form of teaching to which you were entrusted.
  • Eph. 4:14, We must no longer be children tossed to and fro and blown about by every wind of doctrine, by people's trickery, by their craftiness and deceitful scheming.
  • Col. 2:6, As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, continue to live your lives in him, rooted and built up in him and established in the faith, just as you were taught, abounding in thanksgiving.
  • 1 Tim.3:14-15, ...I am writing these instructions to you so that if I am delayed, you may know how one ought to behave in the household of God, which is the church of the living God.
If the content of the Church's education ought to be Apostolic, comprehensive and suitable for knowledgeable obedience (confessional), the only remaining question pertains to a method suitable for teaching such content.

B. Method of Teaching most suitable to Biblical Content:

The issue of "method" is one of form rather than substance. Consequently the primary concern of any method would be to use that form most complementary to the substance itself. As we will see, if the Substance of our teaching is to be Apostolic, Comprehensive and Confessional, our methods or "forms" will want to reflect these goals. While the scripture doesn't specifically address the "form" best suited for teaching Biblical content, there is scriptural evidence that a predominantly systematic approach to teaching is best.

1. Systematic Approach:

A systematic approach is particularly well suited to presenting the "whole counsel of God's Word in a way that reflects apostolic doctrine in a confessional context. This can be accomplished by one of two ways. First, the church can use the method of comprehensive surveys of Bible doctrine (such as Creeds and Catechisms). Second, the church can study the Bible itself systematically by choosing books of the Bible "in a sequence which permits the full range of Biblical truth to be presented."(p.3, Gordon) The first approach represents what can be described as a Theological organization whereas the latter approach represents a Biblical organization.
a. Theological Organization in Scripture: (Deductive Study of Scripture)
There is some evidence that this approach was beginning to be used in the Church even before the Apostolic teachings were completed. For instance in 1 Cor.15:3ff, what appears to be a summation of the Christian gospel in some Theological organization is being referred to by Paul. Also, it is generally agreed that the confession given to us in 1 Timothy 3:16ff represents an ancient theological organization of Apostolic faith. It reads,

Great indeed is the (confession) of the mystery of our religion:
He was manifested in the flesh, vindicated in the Spirit
seen by angels, preached among the nations
believed on in the world, taken up in glory
An analysis of this passage reveals a theological organization depicting the doctrine of Christ's incarnation set in contrast to his exultation as the basic teaching of the church concerning Jesus Christ. The impetus for such a theological organization was no doubt the emergence of controversy and heterodoxy even before the Biblical canon was closed.(4:1-5) While these and other such examples don't argue for the necessity of this first kind of systematic organization, it does reveal the use of such organization even during the ministry of the apostles was found to be useful.

Throughout church history, such questions and controversy have required that theological organizations (creeds) became increasingly more comprehensive in scope. As was the case in the apostolic period, brief summaries of the teachings of scripture regarding certain topics of controversy were found to be very helpful in promoting the peace and purity of the church throughout the centuries. In other words, as the "apostolic faith" became more and more reinterpreted over time, so too the need to "clarify" the apostolic faith. To some degree, this explains why the volume of content continued to increase relative to the earlier creeds. (compare for example the Apostles Creed with the Westminster Confession of Faith) In an ironic sort of way, controversy has served the church to look more intently into the Scriptures. The results have been the formation of creeds. The impetus for churches to do this is patterned for us in Act 15 depicting an early church assembly authorized to resolve a controversy of faith.

b. Biblical Organization (Inductive Study)
The basic argument for "Book studies" is that God's design is certainly a design able to teach the whole counsel of God's word. This is affirmed in Romans 15:4; For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, so that by steadfastness and by the encouragement of the scriptures we might have hope. While seldom used anymore, most would not argue against a Biblical organization as indicated through expository preaching and some systematic strategy of teaching the whole counsel of God's word through teaching whole books of the bible.
2. Occasional Exceptions:

Gordon states that "while it is necessary to attempt to teach the full range of God's revelation, there are occasions when God's revelation must be brought to bear on matters which have arisen in a particular circumstance. (e.g., 1 Corinthians is a letter occasioned by Paul's hearing reports about the church...)"(p.4, Gordon) There will always be times when the systematic approach should be temporarily set aside to meet a specific pastoral need in the congregation. This is more or less the "topical" sermon or Bible study. For instance, when our church first baptizes infants, given the context of an individualistic orientation in modern culture, it might be appropriate to temporarily abandon the Biblical organization of preaching through a book to preach specifically on a passage about baptism. But I would suggest that this ought to be an exception rather than the rule given our desire to systematically teach whole books of the Bible. Our concern is not that occasionally teaching certain topics is wrong, but that it ought not to be the primary method of "teaching them all things that I have commanded you." Chances are, such a method would only address those topics most relevant to the teacher's personal interests or the most pressing felt needs of a congregation.

C. The Context for Teaching Biblical Content:

Since it is rather obvious that Biblical instruction ought to include both public and private contexts, it will suffice to give but a couple of biblical examples.
  • Acts 5:42, And every day in the temple and at home they did not cease to teach and proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.
  • Acts 20:20, I did not shrink from doing anything helpful, proclaiming the message to you and teaching you publicly and from house to house, as I testified to both Jews and Greeks about repentance toward God and faith toward our Lord Jesus.
Public contexts would include corporate worship and public education classes. Private instruction would include pastoral visitation, family worship, personal counseling and one-on-one teaching.

D. Conclusion of Biblical Survey

From even a brief study of Scripture, it can be readily affirmed that the church should be concerned not merely with teaching just any curriculum of Christian study, but that kind of curriculum that is most complementary to a content that is apostolic, comprehensive and confessional. Whereas there will always be special "occasions" that warrant more topical kinds of approaches, the predominant and overall teaching of the church should be systematic.

We are trying to build up the whole person in the whole counsel of God's word, not just special interests or respond to one personal crisis after another. Another way to think of it is that while the church will often need to give attention to the raging fires , she had better not forget to give her primary attention to fire prevention. Moreover, any strategy for Christian education would want to develop a strategy that seeks to integrate the different organizations of systematic and occasional forms of teaching into a complementary and unified whole. The end result is a multi-faceted and integrative strategy of theological organization, Biblical organization and occasional organizations of teaching. In Christ Presbyterian Church, we believe that expository preaching (Biblical organization) is most appropriate for worship where God is clearly speaking to his people through the preaching of his word in it's Divine arrangement. We also plan to have semi-regular "lectures and discussions" on Friday evenings together with annual conferences that will seek to think theologically about pertinent topics of modern life (occasional organization).

It therefore makes good sense that we also utilize the theological organization as represented in creeds and catechisms for our children and adult education. The use of the Westminster Standards as a reformed creed satisfies all the Biblical Criteria for accomplishing the great commission as briefly summarized above. In so far as the creed is derived from the teachings of the apostles albeit with special attention given to interpretive controversies in Church history, it proves to be apostolic. In so far as it provides a concise summary of the general scope of apostolic teaching, it is comprehensive. In so far as it represents an authorized interpretation of the apostles teachings rather than simply some persons private interpretation, it is useful in teaching Gods people confessionally what to believe and obey under the oversight of the church. In so far as it is a theological organization that can be taught systematically, it has always proven to be "friendly to the study of Christian doctrine and of course to the prevalence of Christian knowledge." (Samuel Miller, Utility of Creeds...)

III Response to Common Questions and Objections

Introducing his published lectures to children on the Westminster Shorter Catechisms, Ashbel Green acknowledges that "objections have been made and strenuously urged against the propriety of the whole proceeding on which we are now entering."(p.14) He then explains that it is "proper to endeavor to remove them from your minds, if they have an existence there; or if not, to enable you to answer them satisfactorily when you meet them as you probably will in reading or conversation." (p.14) What was true over one hundred and fifty years ago remains true today. Both for the prupose of introducing Ashbel Green to the modern reader, but also for his cogent responses, I will let Asbel speak to the modern objections as it is applicable.

Admittedly, there are many good questions regarding the use of creeds and catechisms. As I have listened to the questions, they seem to fall into four basic categories. Not only will I suggest that the objections are unfounded, but that the positive impetus underneath them is best accomplished with the use of creeds.

1. The Bias against Bias
Ashbel Green articulates this first objection as follows:
that the human mind ought not to be pre-occupied and biased by being taught the principles of any religious creed or system whatsoever, but be left perfectly free and unprejudiced; that it may at a proper time impartially judge and adopt for itself those religious tenets which shall appear most rational, and free from error. (p.14-15)
At first, this objection appears quite noble. Yet Green makes several observations, all of which reveal something of the value of creeds in educating the youth. First, Green basically argues that there is no such thing as a "neutral" or 'unbiased" religious state. Clearly the scripture doesn't present a scenario where our minds are as clean slates waiting to be imprinted upon. Rather we are told that we were conceived in our mothers womb with a natural bias against God and true religion. (Psm. 51) Green therefore responds by asking,
would they, if in this matter left to themselves really grow up with out any prepossessions in regard to this momentous subject? By no means! They would , on the contrary-- and facts prove it-- either contract a total indifference or contempt for all religion, or else acquire the most false and pernicious notions-- fortified, it is probable, because we are apt to be more attached to opinions which we have elaborated for ourselves, than to those which we have received from others; especially if our minds have been puffed up with the belief that on a given subject we are fully competent to be our own teachers, and that to be so is to be spirited and magnanimous. (p.16)

Green continues in his response by noting how it is contrary to the clear teaching of scripture that parents ought to "train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it." (Prov.22:6) This of course means more than simply reading to them scripture, but giving them some interpretation as to what it means. Whether a parent is conscious of it or not, the interpretation he or she gives is in effect a "creed." Green further notes how Paul doesn't lament the influence of Timothy's grandmother and mother upon Timothy's faith as the above objection would seem to do. But quite the contrary, he applauds it while affirming that Timothy's childhood education was "able to make you wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus. (1 Tim. 1:5)

In addition to the passages mentioned by Green, there are several more passages that pertain to training and teaching children in the Faith.
  • Deut.4:10, ...make them known to your children and your children's children... Assemble the people for me and I will let them hear my words so that they may learn to fear me as long as they live on the earth, and may teach their children so.
  • Deut.6:6-7, Keep these words that I am commanding you today in your heart, recite them to your children and talk about them when you are at home and when you are away when you lie down and when you rise. Bind them as a sign on your hand, fix them as an emblem on your forehead, and write them on the door posts of your house and on your gates.
  • Joel 1:3, Tell your children of it, and let your children tell their children and their children another generation...
  • Eph.6:4, And fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.
Several things ought to be observed by the above passages. First, the primary responsibility of Biblical training and nurture rests with the parent. Second, what we do with our children will have a profound impact not only in their lives, but on the lives of many generations. Third, there is a close relation between the corporate faith of the church and the responsibility of the parents to instruct children in the teachings of the faith.(Dt. 4) The instruction at home is most effective and good when it is affirmed and complemented by the teaching received at church. And fourth, it is the primary responsibility of the father to insure that the faith is being communicated to children. This doesn't necessarily mean the father does all the teaching, but that he should be involved, supportive and even responsible for the instruction of children in the Lord. In other words, true religion isn't for the women alone, or for women alone to promote.

Green's final response to the first objection is to remind his readers that the approach represented by the objection would be contrary to every other subject of childhood education. Green again asked, "and shall the human mind be at first almost wholly indebted to parental instruction for information on every other subject and be left entirely without it on the most important of all subjects-- the object of religion and the knowledge of God and of our duty to him?" (p.16) Green's response is a reminder to us as parents that we dare not miss such an impressionable time of our children's lives by neglecting to give them a thorough and systematic education in the Christian faith. How many men and women who even initially rebelled against a parent's kind and patient instruction in the Lord were known later to return to it in full affection? At a time when people are most able to learn and memorize, what would keep us from filing into their waiting minds those Biblical categories of spiritual reality that will for the rest of their lives serve to protect and guide them in life. Indeed, it is our responsibility to train up a child in the way she or he should go, so that they might be blessed in this life and the life to come.

Green makes the simple and profound observation that "the destinies of children are closely connected with those of their parents."(p.18) This is borne out in such passages as Exodus 34:6-7, "The Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, keeping steadfast love for the thousandth generation...but visiting the iniquity of the parents upon the children and the children's children to the third and fourth generation." He therefore concludes that "every parent should keep constantly in mind that the eternal welfare of his (her) children as well as their present happiness may depend on the religious and moral instruction which they receive in their early years-- never forgetting that example teaches even more powerfully than precept; and that without example all precepts will probably be of little avail." (p.18)


2. Biblicalism: "No creed but the Bible"

This second objection is articulated by Green as follows:
In teaching and learning a catechism, and by making the answers in that catechism the text of lectures, we substitute human composition for the Holy Scriptures-- nay, that we even set the words of men above the Word of God. (p.19)
The irony to this second objection according to Green is it fails to consider the second question and answer of the Shorter catechism itself. Rather than distracting from the exclusive authority of Scripture, it very clearly affirms that "the word of God as contained in the scriptures of the Old and New Testament is the only rule to direct us how we may glorify and enjoy God." (Q. #2, Westminster Shorter Catechism) Green then remarks that it would "be strange indeed if in the very act of teaching that the Scriptures are the only rule of faith and practice we should set up another and a superior rule in it's place."(p.19)

The heart of this second objection pertains to the method most suitable for teaching Christian doctrine. The objection wants to know why it is even necessary to have what we have already described as a theological organization rather than just a biblical organization. (systematic theology rather than a systematic study of the Bible) For instance, one method of teaching begins by looking at scripture and then extracting from scripture what it is that we believe, an inductive approach. This is in effect what takes place in expository preaching. The text is first examined followed by extracting from it a theological truth explained by the preacher. While Green affirms this method, he argues that it isn't always the most suitable for teaching. He explains that "on a whole, it seems to me a method by far the most natural, brief and easy to state the doctrine or proposition in the first place, and then to allege the proofs from Scripture for the several parts of it in detail."(p.20) He continues by noting that "it is a method precisely similar to this which is pursued in all mathematical demonstrations. It is also the method adopted in the courts of justice, where the advocate first states what he expects to prove, then brings forward and examines his evidence, and afterwards reasons to show that the evidence adduced has established his position."(p.21) Therefore, the second objection is shown to be merely one of teaching method, not a low view of scripture. As Green's further response to this objection will indicate, such a method might have as it's motivation the goal of preserving scriptural authority rather than usurping it. This second objection refuted by Ashbel Green is often prevented in yet another way as follows.
No creed or catechism ought to be formed, or taught, or explained, but what consists of the very words of Scripture.(p.21)
Rather than questioning the theological organization of scriptural teaching as before, this question wants to know why the wording itself isn't pure scripture. Whereas the first wanted to know why we would want to systematize the teaching of scripture into theological categories, this objection wants to know why scripture wording is not used to fill the content of the creeds. Closely related to this objection would be the objection, why wouldn't we just memorize and teach scripture, even if in theological categories? (For instance, the Navigators memory cards or some other system of memorizing scripture.) Green's answer to this objection is perhaps the most significant reason why the use of creeds in the church is so necessary.

Green begins by confidently asserting, "we think otherwise and are prepared to assign our reasons for believing it right that every Christian denomination should have a formula of faith expressed, generally, in different words from those of the sacred text." (p.21, underline is mine) What Green will then argue is basically that by the use of creeds, the teaching of a given passage is protected against the various and legion opinions that can arise from misinterpretations. In effect, not only do creeds protect the scripture from being misunderstood and misapplied, it also serves to promote harmony and purity. Before we look more carefully at Green's point, some explanation is needed regarding the two elements of any interpretation, namely, the objective and subjective work of the Holy Spirit.

It is true that all Christians have the Holy Spirit to illumine their minds as to the truth of God, in so far as they know it. It is yet another thing altogether to say that all people have an equal knowledge of Scripture resulting in equally valid interpretations of the Bible. Certainly church history has shown us this. For instance, Green explains that "we know that the language of Scripture has been and constantly is, most grossly perverted. We want to tell how we understand it; as we give the passages of Scripture along with our creed for this very purpose." (p.25) In other words, Green alludes to the unfortunate fact that just because a person can quote from scripture, or a denomination affirms the authority of scripture, doesn't mean that it is in keeping with a proper interpretation of Scripture itself. For instance, any Jehovah witness could quote from any portion of the Old and New Testament, but would have a very different interpretation of it's fundamental meaning with respect to the person of Christ. Likewise, a given denomination might speak of "this is my body..." with respect to the communion table but have a very different understanding of what that Biblical phrase is intended to mean. This illustrates the point that there are two, not one, factors in what makes for good interpretation.

Good interpretation is the result of God's subjective word in the interpreter and His objective work in giving us words to study. For instance, how are we to adjudicate between two mutually exclusive interpretations of a given passage, both given by people who claim to be illumined by the Holy Spirit? The generally recognized method for interpreting scripture is clearly stated by the Westminster Confession itself. "The infallible rule of the interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself and therefore when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture, it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly."(Chapter 1, Section 9) In other words, all things being equal with respect to illumination, the person who has a greater knowledge of Scripture will be most likely to have a more biblical interpretation of any given text. The idea of illumination more pertains to our willingness and ability to see and hear what God is saying in His Word, which of course is very important. This is accomplished by the power of the Holy Spirit in giving us such dispositions that love God and are eager to understand Him in His Word. But illumination doesn't somehow mystically help us to objectively know more scripture such that we can apply this knowledge to a given text. Those who have more training in Biblical study, not to say just more time given to it, are more likely to be able to interpret the scriptures using other scripture simply because they know more. This is to say that the Holy Spirit's work in illumination ought never to usurp his equally valid work of revelation as contained in the words of Scripture that can be studied and known. Those equally "illumined" are not necessarily equal in the knowledge of revelation as found in Scripture.

So the question could be raised, "whose interpretation of scripture is right?" Whereas no person, committee or counsel should claim an exclusive hold on Biblical truth , it could certainly be argued that some interpretations should carry more credibility and weight than others. As it has been shown, it is simply not true that every person's interpretation is as good as the other. It would therefore be to the advantage of any church and it's children to have access to an interpretation of scripture that has not only come to us from highly respected Biblical and theological minds, but also withstood the sacred test of time representing a more thorough knowledge of scripture. (See addendum on Westminster Standards) Perhaps the most telling example of human pride would be to discard the product of hundreds of years in preference to the untrained and rather cursory study of the typical person reading his or her own bible. While each person should seek to hold all creeds to the teachings of scripture, and is even encouraged to take exception to those teachings which he or she believes to be contradictory of scripture, it would seem only wise that the counsel of many great theological minds tested over centuries of debate might be given some benefit of the doubt. And how much more beneficial would it be to offer our children at the youngest possible age a credible interpretation of the general teachings of Scripture from which they might begin to understand the Christian faith? So as Ashbel Green argued, to use creeds and catechisms is not at all a usurping of Scripture, but the affirmation of that interpretation of Scripture that is most in accord with what is believed to be true. For Green and the Presbyterian Church in America, that Standard is the Westminster Confession of Faith and the Larger and Shorter Catechism.

Therefore, the use of creeds and catechism is the best way to not only teach the basic tenets of the faith, but to protect them against what we believe would be an abusive use of scripture. It is therefore not enough to teach a person scripture, we need to also help her or him have a Biblically consistent interpretation of scripture as well. Therefore, Green argues that having an interpretation and summary of the Christian faith that represents the best in biblical study from minds equally illumined is the very basis of "favorable harmony, order and peace among those who endeavor to walk together in Christian fellowship." (p.21) Yet this "harmony, order and peace" is maintained without sacrificing a comprehensive and theological integrity with respect to some of the more difficult issues that are taught in Scripture. In other words, the content of what the church teaches will not be reduced to only what its people have been able to understand, but rather what over the course of history the church catholic and universal has been able to understand.

Without a recognized and authoritative interpretation and summary of the Christian faith, what often results is disunity and disfellowship even within an organizational fellowship. More often, the solution is simply to ignore those biblical issues that cause controversy even though scripture teaches about them. In the one case, the church is left to agree to disagree-- not a very high view of Christian unity.(see below) Whereas in the other case, the content of the church's teaching is far less than comprehensive and apostolic, not a very high view of scripture. Therefore, one reason that creeds and catechisms are needed in the church is to provide an interpretation of scripture that can unify the church in a common faith and common duty without sacrificing theological purity and comprehensiveness. In a sentence, the use of creeds and catechisms promote both the peace and purity of the church without sacrificing the authority of Scripture to interpret itself-- an infinitely higher view of scripture I would argue than to reduce it's authoritative teachings to only what a given individual can understand for him or herself or what some consensus could possibly attain for the church. To put it most practically, I want my children to be nurtured in a Christian faith that far exceeds what I have myself been able to discover or even has been discovered by all the people in a local church.

3. Creeds are a detriment to church unity.

To some degree, this third objection is an objection against denominationalism on the grounds that it fosters disunity.
Denominationalism (subscription to creeds) keeps the church from experiencing the kind of Unity that would be possible if we would simply put aside our differences and just experience God and his love.

The fundamental error implicit to this objection is a definition for unity that is foreign to the Bible. As it has been shown already, the modern definitions of unity will emphasis a particular organizational structure or method. This "organizational" unity is defined in terms of sharing a common "method" or "technique" which is often called a philosophy of ministry. This is why a person can go into two very different denominations yet often see no difference, whereas they can go into two other churches that are both members of the same denomination and find that they are miles apart. Now some of this is of course good and natural pertaining to a given mix of gifts in a congregation and perhaps some similarities in a given demographic category-- say an economic or ethnic group of people. But it is another thing to redefine unity in social terms rather than in terms of "one faith." If what constitutes our "being one" is simply that we exist under the same organizational structure, this is no different than the kind of oneness that any other social organization experiences.

The Biblical understanding of unity is defined in terms of shared beliefs. It is a "confessional unity" rather than an "organizational unity." To the degree that we believe together is to the degree that we share oneness according to the Bible. By implication, the more we share in beliefs together (not the less), the more we are can say we are "one in Christ." This simple understanding of Christian unity results in a very different kind of program for building it. Defined organizationally, "beliefs" (creeds) are considered to be a hindrances to unity-- the less creed the better. In confessional unity, "beliefs" are the bedrock from which unity stems-- the more the better. So the Bible teaches a unity of faith, not just unity of experience. Note for instance the following passages.
  • Amos 3:3, "How can two walk together unless they be agreed'?
  • Phil. 1:27, "stand fast in one spirit with one mind"
  • 1 Cor.1:10, "speak the same thing and be on one accord of one mind"
  • Philip. 2:2, Make my joy complete, be of the same mind, having the same love, being in full accord and of one mind.
  • Eph. 4:4, "There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism.
  • Eph.4:13, "until all of us come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the son of God.

The implications of how we define unity are profound. Confessional unity over against organizational unity means that we can be committed to and share a common faith with congregations very different from us in socio-demographic or geographical categories. Christian unity therefore knows no ethnic, political, geographical or methodological boundaries, only boundaries pertaining to creed. (beliefs) To say it differently, one could have organizational disunity while having Christian (confessional) unity. Said negatively, one could have organization unity while at the same time having confessional disunity. This is all too often the practice in modern evangelical churches where factions and splits abound. Therefore, the way to grow in unity is not to reduce our creed, but to expand our creed so that we can believe more together about what the scripture teaches. As this pertains to membership in a church, while the least amount of credible faith in Jesus Christ is enough to become members of a church, our aim is to grow in the knowledge of Jesus Christ and His Kingdom such that we can share more together and give Him more glory. Anti-denominationalism (anti-creedalism) isn't the answer to disunity. Rather a more serious study of Scripture until we come to share in the knowledge of God is!

4. The New "Canon" of "Essentials" over against "Non-essentials":

A particularly modern objection regarding the use of creeds has to do with the question of "essential doctrines" over against to "nonessential doctrines." This is indirectly behind the question of denominationalism as well. The questions are usually phrased in one of two ways. First, it is questioned as to whether or not the scripture ever intended to teach as a matter of faith and practice certain elements common to many creeds-- for instance statements of faith regarding church government, sacraments and the mission of the church, etc. The second way is to question whether or not scripture every intended for certain doctrines that are in fact taught in scripture to be an issue worth learning such that they become a part of the identity in a religious organization. For instance, it might be agreed that scripture does teach some doctrine, but questioned as to whether or not the doctrine should ever be allowed to have any exclusionary powers, say excluding a person from holding a certain church office, etc. Notice first of all that neither questions are really objections against creeds per se, but as to whether or not most creeds go too far. Of course, the question then becomes, how far is too far. The first question answers this by arguing against certain doctrines being taught in scripture. This is of course fair enough. Our response then would be to go to the scripture to find the answer.

The second question, however, argues from the standpoint that different doctrines have different functions, some more important than others such that some might as well only carry the weight of "biblical suggestion" by implication. Before I go further, let me first point out what both versions of questions regarding the "essentials" have in common. Both questions represent what might be described as the new evangelical "canon within a canon"-- or establishing some set of truths by which the rest of the bible is interpreted or categorized. For instance, the classic liberal "canon" is the canon (belief) regarding the nonexistence of supernaturalism in the world as we know it. As a "canon within the canon" all of scripture is reinterpreted or classified in relation to this anti-supernatural canon.

Therefore, those in this camp simply don't believe that the Bible ever intended to teach us anything about the historicity of a given event, especially those that are supernatural. Another more sophisticated way of saying this is that the Bible never intended to teach us anything about cosmology (doctrine about the cosmos). Now rightly so, evangelicals rejected this "new canon within the canon." While evangelical Christians would all agree that the Bible doesn't speak exhaustively about the doctrine of the universe, where it has spoken we cannot simply discard it or reinterpret it in a way that the authors never intended. In other words, the Bible has the only authority to authoritatively interpret itself. Now what is perhaps peculiar is that while evangelicals have rejected the liberal's canon of "anti-supernaturalism, it has created yet another canon called "the essentials." It is conspicuously close to the same "middling standard" that Wells describes in culture at large. Whereas the liberals drank from the fountain of "high culture" resulting in their canon of anti-supernaturalism, the evangelicals, Wells has argued, drank from the fountain of "pop culture" resulting in their canon of consensus or "the essentials."

By way of a general response to this new "canon within the canon", the obvious question that has always plagued all attempts to create a "canon within a canon" (and they are legion) is where to draw the line. In other words, where is the line drawn as to what is essential and what isn't. It often ends up being a purely subjective call that is packed with some previously held theological belief as to what is essential. For instance, is it essential for a church to teach the historicity of the resurrection? How would this effect our view of Christ and his work for our salvation. Most evangelicals have said it is essential. On the other hand, is it essential that a person believe in a second blessing as indicated in the gift of some ecstatic language called "tongues?" Well in one since, most evangelicals would say no in so far as it doesn't necessarily change the fact that you are a true child of God. But what congregation would allow a person to be their pastor who differed significantly on this issue. What is "non-essential" in belief becomes essential in practice. Again, how a person believes about the future (eschatology) and the nature of the Lord's return can have a very significant influence on the relationship between church and culture. One view leads to building a kingdom on earth through social involvement, another leads to retreat from the culture. Just try working both views out in one evangelism committee. Again, what most would say is "non-essential" can easily be the kind of thing that would split even the best congregations. Again, is it essential to hold to a certain form of government? Not from the standpoint of being saved out of eternal punishment into eternal life. But perhaps it is essential from the standpoint of being saved from so many of the abuses of church power that has been so prevalent in history.

My first response is simply to say that the supposed "essential" over against "non-essential" is much more elusive than we think, so much so, that it is probably a lost cause even before we go any further. Not only is it difficult to draw the line, it is often the case that certain "non-essentials" are much more "essential" than we think if our goal is to grow in Christian maturity and stature. (Heb 6:1)

What then does the Scripture teach as a matter for faith and practice? This is in effect what the first question. Again, this is fair enough. Perhaps only a warning is needed at this point. If it is true that we live in a culture that tends to be less concerned for objective theology and more interested in the consensus, technique and therapy, we would therefore want to make sure that our reading of scripture is not simply a spiritualized version of our social context.

For instance, what did Paul mean by the "pattern of sound words" that Timothy (a second generation pastor) was being exhorted to teach. (2 Tim.2:1:13) Recently, the pattern has been reduced only to some doctrine regarding personal salvation and the doctrine of God. But is this all Paul taught in 2 Timothy and elsewhere? Do the "sound words" taught by Paul include for instance the large portions of teaching given to the government, worship and mission of the church? For example, in 1 Timothy, Paul gives instructions for worship (2:1-15) and government (3:1-13). This is followed by the following exhortation. "I am writing these instructions you may know how one ought to conduct oneself in the household of God, which is the church of the living God." Clearly, Paul doesn't say that his teaching was to be considered "preferable when expedient" for instance, but obligatory as a matter of keeping his apostolic faith. It is true that such teachings might not determin a person eternal status as a child of God, put it might very well determine the quality of Christian religion in this world. This simply raises the question, if we decide that the apostolic faith doesn't include doctrines regarding worship and government, what prevents us from making the same decision about other doctrines as well. Surely no other doctrine is so clearly taught with authority by Paul. It would have to be a decision that was made based upon some other criteria. Let me respond to two that are often utilized.

First, it is said that some teachings are simply less clear than others such that they carry less authority. What is problematic about this is that most, if not all, doctrines are riddled with some measure of scriptural difficulty. Take for instance the doctrine of Christ's nature being both God and man. Even a cursory recounting of early church history illustrates how difficult it is to prove from Scripture that Christ is both God and man from scripture. How can Jesus be one with God and cry out, "My God my God why has thou forsaken me?" How can Jesus be human and say "I and the Father am one." Does this mean we discount the dual nature of Christ as a "non-essential." Hardly! Rather we believe that while God never intended to give us an exhaustive revelation as to the nature of Christ, He gave us enough to believe the right thing. While many questions will always abound as to how this dual nature can exist in one person, it is enough for us to know that the weight of the Biblical evidence leads us to believe that it does and that it is a matter for faith and practice.

Notice what is going on here. We have in effect said that while there are certain "problematic passages" that could be pointed to (the Jehovah's witness point to them all the time) the overall teachings of scripture produce more confidence that Jesus is God and man in one person. We form our beliefs not by the absence of uncertainty, but simply because we are more certain in a given doctrine than we are of its counterpart. As in a T-scale, once it tips, it tips all the way even though some weight remains on the other side of the scale. This side of heaven, we must be content with forming our confession with relative certainty rather than in absolute certainty. But this is not to say that we are not fully confident in our beliefs. Rather we believe that God did give us some revelation-- enough to tip the scale so to speak that we might be able know Him and His will for our lives.

A second line of thought is to say that certain doctrines have more essential consequences such that they are more important. In one sense, we would agree. It is true that the consequence of believing in Jesus as the Son of God has a much different kind of consequence than say the belief in Presbyterianism or Congregationalism. However, this is not to say that church government is not important, only that it is not as important when directed to the issue of ultimate salvation. This line of reasoning then falls prey to a confusion between value and function. We fully believe that a given doctrine ought not to be applied in a way that it was never intended to function. For instance, we would never exclude someone from joining our church on the grounds that they didn't believe in Presbyterianism. However, we would want a person who seeks to function as an officer in the church to believe in Presbyterianism. Why? Because the doctrine of government functions to direct us in how we govern in a Biblical way. A person called to "govern" in a church that believes Presbyterianism is the Biblical way to do it would have a difficult time if he didn't believe it himself.
Therefore, how important is the doctrine of church government with respect to joining our church. No very important. How valuable is it with respect to becoming an officer. Very important in so far as it will effect they way we "govern."

Finally, in response to the new canon of "essentials:, it is important to remember what we believe about inspiration. One of the more "essential" "beliefs" of evangelicals is what has been described technically as "plenary inspiration." This is the "creed" that says "all scripture is inspired by God." Find me an evangelical church (with or without a creed) that wouldn't impose this upon any candidate for ministry in their church. Now if all scripture is equally inspired, then all scripture is equally important to God. Again, this isn't to confuse "important" with equal in function. If God has chosen to "inspire" it, the only grounds for not keeping it as a matter of faith and practice would be to argue from the text itself that God also made it clear that He didn't want it to be perpetual. It is difficult to put most of the issues often discounted as "non-essentials" into this category.

IV: A Practical Strategy Of How And When To Use Catechisms For The Christian Education Of Children At Christ Presbyterian Church

Therefore, the church since the time of the Apostles have chosen to use creeds and catechisms as a theological organization of systematic instruction. A.A. Hodge describes how the use of creeds in all ages of the church were useful in the following four ways:
  • 1. To mark, preserve, and disseminate the attainments made in the knowledge of Christian truth by any branch of the church in any grand crisis of its development.
  • 2. To discriminate the truth from the glosses of false teachers, and accurately to define it in its integrity and due proportions.
  • 3. To act as the bond of ecclesiastical fellowship among those so nearly agreed as to be able to labor together in harmony.
  • 4. To be used as instruments in the great work of popular instruction.
It concerns the fourth use of creeds and catechisms that we now want to address, especially with respect to the instruction of our children . Ashbel Green provides a wonderful model from the 19th century. In his words, he began with children
from the age of three or four years to that of ten or twelve... They were counseled, and admonished, and prayed with in language the most simple, plain and tender, that could be devised; and never did the speaker find the difficulty so great, in addressing any other audience or in leading any other devotions as in performing these duties for the lambs of his flock; in adapting his thoughts and his language to their capacities and becoming their mouth to God.(p.7)
In order to introduce what will admittedly be a very brief outline of our strategy for using catechisms in the instruction of our children, I again want to put before you an extended quote from Ashbel Green. It speaks very eloquently of the benefits of using the Shorter catechism for the religious instruction of children.
The great design and principal use of a catechism, or articles of faith, is to bring together in proper order, the principal truths and doctrines of the Bible; so that they may be seen at one view, and in a small compass. We believe that this is very advantageous to all, and especially to the young. Hence the practice so general, in our church, of requiring children and youth to commit accurately to memory at least our Shorter Catechism-- frequently, with the whole of the Scriptural proofs. If it be remarked that much of this catechism cannot be fully understood by children in their early years, it should be remembered that this is no more than is true of almost all elementary instruction. Scarcely ever is it more than partially understood at first; but being treasured up in the memory, it is there for meditation, investigation, and application as the mind gradually advances in knowledge and in strength... The catechetical or questionary form of religious summaries renders them most easy and interesting to children and youth; and indeed to Christians of all ages and descriptions. (p.26-27,29)

At Christ Presbyterian church, we have developed a strategy for teaching our children the Christian faith as summarized in the Westminster Shorter Catechisms. Our strategy will seek to complement the work of parents at home with Sunday morning review and instruction. Notice that I said "complement the work of the parents" rather than for the parents to be set in a role of "complementing the work of the church." We believe that the primary responsibility for raising up children in the Lord rests with the parent. The relative responsibilities of the church and home in children discipleship is wonderfully summarized in our Book of Church Order as follows:
28-1. The spiritual nurture, instruction and training of the children of the church are committed by God primarily to their parents. They are responsible to the church for the faithful discharge of their obligations. It is a principal duty of the church to promote true religion in the home. True discipleship involves learning the Word of God under the guidance of the Holy Spirit both at home and in the church. Without learning there is no growth and without growth there is no discipline and without discipline there is sin and iniquity (1 Timothy 4:7).
It is therefore suggested that at an early age, parents begin to help their children memorize the answers to the Shorter catechism with some brief and simple explanations.

The big question of course is, "At what age do I begin? As was the custom in Green's day, I would suggest that the "mothers catechism" better known today as the Catechism for Young Children. put out by Great Commission Publications, be used with our two and three year old children. The idea is not to complete this catechism, but merley help our children begin to learn how to memorize even as they are introduced to some of the most simple themes of our faith. You may prefer to simply begin with the Shorter Catechism however. At about the age of four or four and a half, I would suggest you begin to help your children memorize the Shorter Catechism at home. Meanwhile, the catechism questions and answers will be recited as a group in children's assembly and briefly reviewed in each Sunday School class. The effect will be that the work you are doing at home will be reinforced both for motivation and learning purposes in the children's education at church. From the standpoint of the child, you will not be asking your child to do something that is not being done by the other kids. Not only will we be helping our children and ourselves learn the important teachings of scripture concerning saving faith, we will also be building a solid base for the unity of faith in our church.

With respect to a specific plan of how to teach your children the catechisms, I can offer you some of the conventional wisdom. Of course, you will want to learn what is best for your family situation and child. First, choose a time that will be most consistent, about ten or fifteen minutes every day. Second, choose a quiet room so as to have little or no interuptions. Third, for those children that cannot read (and perhaps those who do but have never learned how to memorize in a systematic way), you should meet with them so as to break down the answer of a given question into portions most suitable for quick memory, slowly adding on to the first portion until the whole answer is memorized. For instance, on Question # 36 you would begin with "the benefits which in this life do accompany or flow." After this had been recited several times without help, add the second portion "from justification, adoption and sanctification....etc. This continues until the whole answer has been set to memory. The finally step is for your child to recite the answer at least five times perfectly. Make a game of this stage, perhaps making your fingers go down as he/she recites the answer five times. Although you will want to do your best to make it a game, so not be afraid to teach your child the value of non-entertaining work. As your child learns to read and/or memorize, you may merely ask your child to go to a private place to learn a catechism, and return for recitation. Lastly, periodically remind your child that you love him/her with acceptance regardless of how well or fast he/she memorizes the catechism. (First things first.)

To give you an idea of how long it takes, most children will learn about 36 questions over the time of a year. But you should go at a pace most suitable for your child. I can't emphasis this enough. It is not important how fast you get through the questions. What is important is that this become a regular and non-negotiable part of your life with your child. While there will be times when both you and your child's patience will be tested, I think you will both find it to be a very special time.
Finally, I would not be concerned that your child has only a rudimentary understanding of the theology being learned. For instance, you should intentionally use many of the great words of our faith like justification, adoption, and sanctification knowing these words have the capacity for huge ideas later in life. But for now, it is enough that your child associate "justification" with forgiveness of sins because of what Christ did on the cross. He will later learn to appreciate the accomplishment of justice by a legal representative and his status being credited to our spiritual account, and so on. This is true with other such profound words like "imputed", "decree," "providence," etc. For your personal use in helping your child understand the catechism, you should consider getting A.A. Hodge's Commentary on the Westminister Confession.

You will discover many great benefits to catechism training with your child. There will be many times when in the context of a walk or working in the yard, you will find that your child's memory of the catechisms will be your friend in discussing something of God's ways and saving activity. How many times in the most unexpected situations has an opportunity to explain the Faith been more fully utilized because a parent could relate a discussion or event to something their child had already committed to memory. Indeed, it is in these moments that the rudimentary understanding if the faith begins to make the transition into a deeper, more intricate one. May God bless you and even thrill you in your efforts!

Conclusion:

Calvin wrote in his preface to writing the Catechism of the Church of Geneva ,
It has ever been the practice of the Church, and one carefully attended to, to see that children should be duly instructed in the Christian religion... To secure this being done in order, there was written out a formula, which was called a Catechism or Institute. Thereafter the devil miserably rending the Church of God, and bringing upon it fearful ruin, overthrew this sacred policy, and left nothing behind but certain trifles, which only beget superstition, without any fruit of edification... What we now bring forward, therefore, is nothing else than the use of things which from ancient times were observed by Christians, and the true worshippers of God, and which never were laid aside until the Church was wholly corrupted. (In "To the Reader", written in 1545)

Some people might consider it one of the greatest ironies of all that reformers such as Martin Luther and John Calvin, men deeply committed to the reformation doctrines of sola scriptura, the priesthood of believers and justification by faith alone, would also find it worth their time to pen creeds and catechisms to use in church instruction. Of all the people most concerned for the authority of the Bible, the freedom of conscience and religion that is personally vital and spirit-filled, these men staked their professions and lives upon it. Yet they thought it necessary to use creeds and catechisms for the edification of the church. What may be even more surprising to those who often see themselves in the evangelical wake of the reformation is that the reformer John Calvin, among others, attributed the demise of Christianity not to the use of Creeds resulting in dead orthodoxy, but to the neglect of creeds resulting in heterodoxy. He argues in 1545 that the true Gospel believing church had always used creeds and catechisms in promoting godly religion. When this "sacred policy" was discarded, it was said to have resulted in "trifles" and "superstition" leading to the "ruin of the church." It is not surprising then that one of the notable accomplishments of the "reformation" was the "formation" of numerous creeds and catechisms for the Church. I have tried to offer some of the rational for why this was so then, and ought to be so now more than ever-- which leads us to ask the question, "Are the signs of ruin as noted by Calvin presently upon us?"

As David Well's has argued, Modern evangelicalism is presently known for its anti-creedalism and the propensity to reduce the content of the Christian faith to mere "trifles." Can the confession of religion be so reduced to mere "essentials" without the practice of religion soon becoming increasingly heterodox and ungodly? Suffice it to say that it would be novel to history. I have tried to demonstrate that it would also be contrary to the teachings of scripture. Let us be moved to pray that it will never be said of Christ Presbyterian Church, "they have a zeal for God but not according to knowledge." (Romans 10:2) In concluding his article entitled "Dead End for the Mainline", Kenneth Woodward articulates what has perhaps been the point of this now long defense for the use of creeds. He observes that "the mainline denominations may be dying because they lost their theological integrity. The only thing worse, perhaps, would be the rise of a new Protestant establishment that succeeds because it never had any." Think about what he is saying. What would the church be, if her only creed was "no creed." May God be pleased to add to our number more and more people who are increasingly becoming "one faith"-- that we may all be fully blessed with a true knowledge of God leading to his glory and our enjoyment forever. Amen!

Finally, we are aware that for many of you, the use of creeds and catechism, will be a very new and awkward experience. Please do not hesitate to let us help you in any way that we can.. I should also say again that our use of creeds and catechisms ought never to replace the reading and study of God's word. Whatever confessional authority the Westminster standards might have in our life, it's authority is only derivative in so far as it holds true to the teachings of the Bible. Moreover, as useful and important as the Westminster Standards may be, it is ultimately only an authorized but fallible summary and interpretation of the teachings contained in the Bible. This is indeed the teaching of the Westminster Confession itself when it states in Chapter 1, section 9, "The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself." And in Section 10 of the same chapter, "The supreme judge by which all controversies of religion are to be determined. can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture."

Addendum: The Westminster Standards

Ashbel Green comments that "the Catechism on which the subsequent lectures are to be founded is the production of some of the most learned and pious divines that ever lived." (p.30) This estimation of the quality of people assembled to develop a system of Faith derived from the teachings of the Bible is not unique. Philip Schaff, perhaps one of the most respected historians of the church and church creeds, could say about the Westminster Assembly that "whether we look at the extent or ability of its labors, or its influence upon future generations, it stands first among Protestant Councils." (p.728, The Creeds of Christendom, Vol. 1) He later quotes Anglican Puritan Richard Baxter, although himself not a member of the Assembly but well qualified under any estimation to evaluate its quality as saying;
The divines there congregated were men of eminent learning, godliness, ministerial abilities, and fidelity; and being not worthy to be one of them myself, I may the more freely speak the truth even in the face of malice and envy, that as far as I am able to judge by the information of all history of that kind, and by any other evidences left us, the Christian world since the days of the apostles had never a synod of more excellent divines that this and the Synod of Dort." (p.729, Schaff)
The Westminster Assembly occupied about five years and six months for the completion of its proper work. (July 1, 1643- February 22, 1649) It held no less that 1163 regular sessions. It met every day except Saturday and Sunday, from nine o'clock till one or two in the afternoon. The afternoons were spent in committees. The divines which attended consisted of 121 men from several different affiliations such as Episcopalian, the Belgic, French, Helvetic and other Reformed Churches, Presbyterians, etc. . There were two classes of Presbyterians corresponding to the Low and High Church Episcopalians.

The significance of such facts ought to jump out to the modern reader. Yes, we could start from scratch and develop our own interpretation of scripture. In fact, every student of the Bible necessarily does this in the very process of understanding and digesting it's teaching. And if we refuse the assistance afforded by the statements of doctrine that were meticulously and cautiously elaborated and defined by the church over a period of 1163 intensive days by some of the most able of men in the church's history, we must then make out our own creed by our own unaided wisdom. So the real question is really not weather or not we have a creed. Every time we use the scripture to determine our belief, we have in effect created one. The real question is will we be humble and wise enough to utilize the great wealth of wisdom represented in such a document as the Westminster Confession of Faith. Common sense tells us we should consider the collected wisdom of the church throughout the ages as represented in our church's creed. And why not give our children the added benefit of not being left to the hurried attempts of even the most ambitious modern teacher. So we at Christ Presbyterian, while never relinquishing our responsibility to teach our children, will humbly utilize those grand statements of faith that might provide greater depth and less error in what we do teach.
