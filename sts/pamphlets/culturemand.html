Is there such a thing as "high-culture?" If so, can we define it so as to avoid a kind of Euro-centric snobbishness? The question is raised first to introducing both our Elm City Toasts and the upcoming Christian Faith and Practice on the topic of Art (see inside this issue). But make no mistake about it-- the question gets at the very heart of the reformational view of "calling" as this impacts so much of what each of us is about every Monday through Friday. So briefly-- very briefly-- a few words on the Biblical idea concerning "high culture."

Old Testament scholar Meredith Kline notes about the creation event as described in Genesis that "Elohim, the Creator, is portrayed not as a mighty warrior but as an omnipotent artisan, not as a cunning conqueror but as an omniscient architect." (Kingdom Prologue, p.17, 1989) According to Isaiah, God as "architect/artist" also created the world "not to be empty but formed it as a place to live."(Isa.45:18) Creation then, as the creative expression of God's activity as artist/architect became a kind of practical paradise for living. "Living" has both an aesthetic value and a practical value. This dual aspect of living is very clearly depicted in Genesis 2:9, "And out of the ground the Lord God made every tree grow that is pleasant to the sight and good for food. " Therefore, from the wisdom perspective given in Proverbs 8:22-30, God is personified as a master builder. Heaven and earth was created ultimately as a kind of "house of God", a palace of the Great King, the seat of sovereignty even as this is depicted with great beauty and provision as unto life !

In the creation of men/women, there was then the creation of the imago dei (image of God--Gen.1:27) The mandate given to men/women as within the covenant of creation was both horizontal and vertical as this related to both the "cultic" and "culture" dimensions. (By cult, it is not meant in the common use today as a "false religion, but as that aspect of work related to preserving the holy presence/reputation of God from defilement. ) Meredith Kline notices that "as a garden-paradise it would occupy man with the royal-cultural labor of cultivating its bounty and beauty. As a sanctuary of God it presented man with the cultic vocation of priestly guardianship." (Kline, p.42,See Gen. 1:27-28, where the word "fill" is literally the word "guard" as today our word "fence" is sometimes used as applied to guarding Christ's table.)

Notice then some preliminary notes toward a definition of "high-culture?" First, we will want to notice that "work" was given prior to the curse and is very much a positive aspect of human life. It was only after the curse that "labor" entered into our work with all its difficulties and frustrations. Second, our "work" is "religious" even as this includes both a horizontal (cultural) and the vertical (cultic) dimension. And third, as we anticipate the consummate redemption of heaven and earth, we ought then to participate in both dimensions of this redemption-- as these are applied to both the cultural and cultic dimensions. This is perfectly illustrated by Romans 8:19-24, where the "whole creation" is depicted as waiting for its redemption together with the saints. As Christians being redeemed in Christ, we are being renewed in the image of God (imago dei) such that we image God in his work as architect/artist by making the world "pleasant to the sight." Whereas it is a practical necessity to do what is necessary for food, we often forget that aspect of the cultural mandate to make things "pleasant to the sight" even as this is reflected in what we will call "high-culture."

So what is high culture? It seems to me that high culture is "culture" which affirms creation and promotes life itself-- espsecially in its aesthetic dimension. We represent God as the divine master builder in so far as we transform fallen culture from death to life. We do this both with the ideas we communicate AND in the media we use to communicate them. So for instance, high culture applied to music would be that kind of music that makes us want to live and respect creation-- so too with architecture and art. It would go beyond my abilities to analyse this principle as applied to the various historical and anthropolotical trends. But to even raise the question is itself a victory against the notion of absolute relativism as applied to aesthetics. Therefore, whereas we might should affirm "high culture" as relative to many human cultures perhaps-- African, European, Asian, etc.-- we would also affirm as an absolute that it ought to make us want to live and respect creation as a reflection of the mind of God if it is to be labled "high culture." And I hope that all this wets your appitite for the upcoming Christian Faith and Practice on Art as we celebrate the redemption of culture as through the eyes of one prominent Asian-American artist who is also a Christian.
