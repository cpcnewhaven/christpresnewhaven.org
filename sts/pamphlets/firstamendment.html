A REAFFIRMATION OF
RELIGIOUS LIBERTY AND THE CIVIL SOCIETY


PURPOSE: TO REAFFIRM THE RELIGIOUS LIBERTY CLAUSES OF THE FIRST AMENDMENT AND TO ESTABLISH A STRATEGY ON HOW PERSONAL AND COMMUNAL BELIEFS SHOULD BE RELATED TO PUBLIC LIFE ESPECIALLY IN THE PUBLIC SCHOOLS.


THREE FUNDAMENTAL REAFFIRMATIONS
(Three Reaffirmations has been adapted from the Williamsburg Charter, A National Celebration and Reaffirmation of the First Amendment Religious Liberty clauses, drafted on the occasion of the 200th anniversary of the call for the Bill of Rights and signed by a broad representation of religious, political and educational parties. [The Williamsburg Charter Foundation, 1988. ] The bulleted guidelines under the first two reaffirmations were originally published by the Public Education Religious Studies Center at Wright State University. The Guidelines are taken from "Religion in the Public School Curriculum" as sponsored by American Academy of Religion, American Federation of Teachers, American Association of School Administrators, American Jewish Congress, Amnericans United Research Foundation, Association for Supervision and Curriculum Development, Baptist Joint Committee on Public Affairs, Christian Legal Society, The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, National Association of Evangelicals, National Conference of Christians and Jews, National Council of CHurches of Christ in the USA, National Council on Religion and Public Education, National Council for the Social Studies, National Education Association, and National School Boards Association. The bulleted guidelines of the third reaffirmation was adapted from the Williamsburg Charter.)

1. We Affirm The Obligation Of The State To Resist The Establishment Of Any Particular Beliefs Including All Religions And None

Beliefs are most our own when a matter of conviction rather than coercion. Thus James Madison affirmed that "the religion then of every man must be left to the conviction and conscience of every man." Such convictions of conscience ought to be allowed free expression and exercise without loss of civil liberties. Neither should any particular faith or none be given preferential status so as to enjoy the privilege of "sword or purse" as controlled by the State. We therefore affirm that the state or state sponsored school may not coerce the consciences of students including the official endorsement of any religion or none.

While the government may not use schools to coerce the consciences of our students, or to convey official endorsement of religion, the public schools also may not discriminate against private religious expression during the school day.
President Clinton
July 12, 1995

  • The school's approach to religion is academic, not devotional.
  • The school may strive for student awareness of religions, but should not press for student acceptance of any one religion.
  • The school may sponsor study about religion, but may not sponsor the practice of religion.
  • The school may expose students to a diversity of religious views, but may not impose any particular view.
  • The school may educate about all religions, but may not promote or denigrate any religion.
  • The school may inform the student about various beliefs, but should not seek to conform him or her to any particular belief.


2. We Affirm The Free Exercise Of All Beliefs In Public Life Including All Religious Beliefs And None

Nothing is more characteristic of humankind than the natural and inescapable drive toward meaning and belonging so as to mak sense of life and find community in the world. As fundamental and precious as life itself, this "will to meaning" finds expression in ultimate beliefs, whether theistic or non-theistic, transcendent or naturalistic. The right to freedom of conscience concerning "beliefs" and especially "ultimate beliefs" is premised not upon science, nor upon social utility, nor upon pride of minorities or majorities, but upon the inviolable dignity of the human person. While particular beliefs may be ultimately true or false, better or worse-- the right to reach, hold, exercise them freely, debate them with civility or change them when so persuaded, is one of the most basic foundations of a free society. In so far as "ultimate beliefs" function to answer such questions as pertains to life's beginnings and end, a basis for morality, meaning and purpose in life, and human rights-- we affirm that "ultimate beliefs" by definition, consist of both all religions and none-- all of which enjoy the right to freedom of conscience including the privilege of free exercise and expression in public schools as described in the statement of principles in the Religious Freedom Act of July 12, 1995.

Nothing in the First Amendment converts our public schools into religion-free zones, or requires all religious expression to be left behind at the schoolhouse door. President Clinton
July 12, 1995
  • Schools may not forbid students acting on their own from expressing their personal religious views or beliefs solely because they are religious in nature.
  • Students may pray in a non-disruptive manner during the school day when they are not engaged in school activities and instruction, subject to the same rules of order as apply to other student speech.
  • Students may participate in before or after school events with religious content on the same terms as they may participate in other non-curriculum activities on school premises. School officials may neither discourage nor encourage participation in such an event.
  • Students may express their beliefs about religion in the form of homework, artwork, and other written and oral assignments free of discrimination based on the religious content of their submissions.
  • Students have a right to distribute religious literature to their schoolmates on the same terms as they are permitted to distribute other literature that is unrelated to school curriculum or activities.
  • If is is proved that particular lessons substantially burden a student's free exercise of religion and if the school cannot prove a compelling interest in requiring attendance, the school would be legally required to excuse the student.
  • The fact that some civic values and virtues are hald also by religions does not make it unlawful to teach them in school.
  • Students may display religious messages or items of clothing to the same extent that they are premitted to display other comparable messages.
  • The Equal Access act provides for the use of school media, including public address sysetems, school newspapers and school bulletin boards, to announce religious meetings on the same terms as other noncurriculum-related student groups. Lunch times and recesses are covered by the Equal Access Act when a school creates a limited open forum, triggering equal access rights for religious groups, when it allows students to meet during their lunch periods or other noninstructional time during the school day. Schools, however, may inform students that certain activities or announcements are not school sponsored.
(Note: The Supreme court has at times chosen to recognize that the state has a legitimate interest in social order so as to restrict those practices which are determined to be detrimental to the common good. See for instance Employment Division, Department of Human Resources v. Smith (1990). Smith was denied the civil protection of the religiously inspired and supervised consumption of peyote since peyote is a "controlled substance." Summarized in Constitutional Law, second edition, ed. Stone, Seidman, Sunstein, Tushnet. )


3. We Affirm That A Commitment To Genuine Citizenship Will Seek To Teach And Sponsor Civility In Public Discourse, Especially Concerning Our Ultimate Beliefs.

Commitment to democratic pluralism assumes the coexistence within one political community those beliefs, both of religious communities and none, whose ultimate faith commitments may be incompatible, yet whose common commitment to social unity and religious liberty will result in civility. Civility obliges citizens in a pluralistic society to take great care in using words and casting issues so as to preserve mutual respect with and along side of dynamic debate. Pluralism must not be confused with, and is in fact endangered by, philosophical and ethical indifference. Nor should strong religious commitment imply either intolerance or opposition to democratic pluralism. We therefore affirm a citizenship that is committed to the right of persons holding to any religious belief and none to participate in debate wherein each participant assumes responsibility in the following ways:

  • To accept the responsibility to think charitably about those who hold to different beliefs, especially so as to resist thinking ill of another persons motives or reputations.
  • To accept the responsibility to comprehend the beliefs of others, especially so as to resist false representations of other views in extremist or fictitious ways.
  • To accept the responsibility to foster civil debate since the issue in not only what we debate but how. Debate within civility resists saying things that intentionally inflame another to anger and rather looks for ways to express views that encourage reasonable dialogue.
  • To accept the responsibility to foster and preserve "common ground" in so far as the common good is concerned and many common interests will allow. For example, two students may properly disagree about ultimate beliefs but remain good class mates, team mates, club mates, and simply good friends. Moreover, two students who disagree about ultimate beliefs may join efforts in student government toward the promotion of policies that are genuinely for the common good of all students.
