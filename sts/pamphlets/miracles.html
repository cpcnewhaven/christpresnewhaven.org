Do Miracles Continue in Our Day?
by David Sherwood


THE QUESTION AT ISSUE. The ministries of Christ and the Apostles were characterized by the performance of miracles: signs and wonders such as walking on water, turning water into wine, healing the sick, and raising the dead. Should we expect to see the same kind of phenomena in our day? Note very carefully: the question is not whether God is powerful enough to perform miracles, nor whether we believe in the supernatural, nor whether God works and answers prayer in remarkable ways. Rather, the question is whether we should expect the particular kind of phenomena that characterized the ministries of Christ and the Apostles to characterize the ongoing history of the Church.

THE PROBLEM OF DEFINITION. Answering this question is complicated by a lack of clarity as to how to define a miracle. Is the birth of a baby a miracle? Was the N.Y. Mets' winning the '69 pennant a miracle? Are notable answers to prayer miracles? Is regeneration a miracle? While gladly wishing to acknowledge the providence of God in these and other events, we maintain that they are not miracles in that they may not be naturally categorized with the sorts of miracles we see in the New Testament.

A.A. Hodge, in his Evangelical Theology, offers a well-crafted and carefully-nuanced definition of miracle:

"A miracle is (1) an event (2) occurring in the material world, (3) obvious to the senses, (4) of such a nature that it can be rationally referred only to the immediate act of God as its direct cause, (5) accompanying a teacher of religion sent from God, (6) and designed to authenticate his divine commission."


This definition obviously excludes many remarkable events, such as a sinner's being brought repentance and saving faith, narrowly avoiding a head-on collision with a truck on 121, and the '69 Mets. It is a narrow definition intended to encompass only certain phenomena. While this definition does not resolve every question, and while there may be some phenomena whose categorization would be difficult, it is a good working definition.

DISCONTINUITY THROUGHOUT REDEMPTIVE HISTORY. Keeping this definition in mind, it will be immediately obvious that miracles have been discontinuous throughout redemptive history; they have not always been present. We first see miracles in connection with Moses. We then see them in connection with the ministries of Elijah and Elisha and of Daniel. Most notably, of course, we see an abundance of miracles in connection with the ministries of Jesus and the apostles. But there were long periods of time when miracles were not present. The lack of miracles in such periods (such as the days of Abraham) did not reflect the people's lack of faith or any such thing; it was simply that God, for his own good reasons, was not working in miraculous ways.

THE PURPOSE OF MIRACLES. The explanation of why miracles are discontinuous throughout redemptive history, and specifically why we should not expect to see miracles in our day, is that their God-ordained function appears to have been to authenticate messengers of revelation. As the widow of Zarephath put it when her son was miraculously brought back to life by Elijah: "Now I know that you are a man of God, and that the word of the LORD in your mouth is truth." (1 Kings 17:24)

We believe that the following texts adequately demonstrate that this is the purpose of miracles:

  • And Jesus answered them, "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them. (Mt. 11:4-5)


  • But the testimony which I have is greater than that of John; for the works which the Father has granted me to accomplish, these very works which I am doing, bear me witness that the Father has sent me. (Jn. 5:36)

  • Jesus answered them, "I told you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in my Father's name, they bear witness to me." (Jn. 10:25)

  • But if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works, that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father." (Jn. 10:38)

  • Men of Israel, hear these words: Jesus of Nazareth, a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs which God did through him in your midst, as you yourselves know. (Acts 2:22)

  • So they remained for a long time, speaking boldly for the Lord, who bore witness to the word of his grace, granting signs and wonders to be done by their hands. (Acts 14:3)

  • The signs of a true apostle were performed among you in all patience, with signs and wonders and mighty works. (2 Cor. 12:12)

  • ...while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his own will. (Heb. 2:4)


Now, if the purpose of miracles is to authenticate messengers of revelation, and if revelation came to a close with the fullness of revelation through Christ and his apostles, then the natural conclusion is that miracles (as defined above) have ceased as well.

THE EXAMPLE OF HEALING. Perhaps the issue that is of greatest interest to people is miraculous healing. Here we must be careful to distinguish between healing in general (which sometimes occurs in remarkable and extraordinary ways) and miraculous healing. Certainly the former still occurs; certainly we may and ought to pray for healing; certainly God works providentially to heal the sick. But miraculous healing, on the order of "In the name of Jesus Christ be healed," was apparently restricted to the ministry of Christ and the apostolic era as a means of authenticating Christ and the apostles as infallible agents of revelation.

Dr. John Gerstner has instructively noted the differences between biblical miracles of healing and modern wonders:

BIBLICAL MIRACLES MODERN WONDERS

Always successful Usually unsuccessful
No known relapses Admitted relapses
Always immediate or almost immediate Usually not immediate; often very gradual
Raisings of dead No raisings of dead
All varieties of diseases Usually functional diseases
Usually played down Usually played up
Include power over non-human nature No power over non-human nature
Credentials of messengers of Christian revelation Not credentials of messengers of revelation
No dependence on psychological build-up Dependence on psychological build-up

CONCLUSION. We believe in a sovereign God who works all things after the counsel of his will. We believe in God's works of providence, his most holy, wise, and powerful preserving and governing all his creatures and all their actions. We believe in a God who answers prayer, who is intimately at work in the lives of his people. And we believe that at times God's providence manifests itself in extraordinary and remarkable ways which often defy rational explanation.

But we also believe that different epochs of redemptive history have their unique characteristics, and that one of the characteristics of this epoch of redemptive history is that revelation has ceased; it was completed with the coming of Christ and the finality of the apostolic witness to him. Since the purpose of miracles (as defined above-this is important to note) was to authenticate the messengers of revelation at different points in redemptive history, we may safely assume that miracles, having served their divinely-ordained purpose, have ceased.
