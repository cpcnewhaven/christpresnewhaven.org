Some "Off The Cuff" Thoughts on Preaching:

From Text to Sermon

Preston Graham, Jr.

 

I. Some of the "bigger" questions that you will need to answer for yourself.

1. What is preaching?

2. Where do you locate the "word" (behind the text, text, before the text)

3. Who exactly has been entrusted with the "word" (individual preacher, church/tradition, individual listener?) such as to hold the preacher accountable so to speak?

4. How do you relate text to greater biblical context? E.g. progressive revelation?

5. How do you relate the Old Testament with the New?

6. How do you relate the Biblical text to the present with respect to "revelation?"

7. What is the relation of "person" to "office" in the preaching event itself?

 

II. Some general thoughts on preaching-- "from text to sermon"

1. What is preaching?

a. Neh.8:1-8 -- at the very least, it is reading the sacred text and giving a sense as to what it means in the popular language.

b. Titus 2:11-15-- its aim is most fundamentally to reconcile people to God by proclaiming the grace of God in Christ, both now and in the age to come.

2. Where is the "word?"

a. 1 Tim.3:16-- If in the words themselves, then I am less concerned (for the purpose of preaching) with authorship kinds of issues, less concerned to understand the source debates (not that they have no relevance-- a matter of priority for preaching) and more concerned with the content that is in the text itself-- a historical-grammatical-literary analysis.

b. Working with the original languages IS helpful. (see "the Peril… ")

    • You will find it difficult to maintain your language skills given a typical church schedule. Hints-- a)preach a good balance of Old and New Testament texts with the goal never to skip translation step. b) Review basic paradigms and grammar regularly)

3. There is great value in preaching "expositional" sermons through whole books of the bible.

  • There is no more natural and sure way to preach the "whole counsel of God's word."
  • It helps prevent against a single issue church-- either driven by the pastors pet projects or driven by the "power brokers" that inevitably rise up in a particular congregation.
  • There is a beautiful nuance concerning the revelation of God and/or salvation that would often be lost if not for expositional preaching through whole books of the bible. . E.g. Some of my most effective sermons are ones I would have never preached if left to my own determination which suggests that many of the greatest jewels are located in some of the most obscure or difficult passages.
  • It guards against a "gospel" that is polemically driven. Not only will many a preacher avoid spiritual burnout by expositional preaching, but so too will many congregations be spared of spiritual burnout. E.g. It is very easy to let a hot issue dominate our thoughts and ministry. But how refreshing then for both the preacher and the congregation to be forced to "move on" by an expositional method of preaching.
  • It teaches people how to read their own bibles by way of example.
  • So many points are missed because a given text is decontextualized from its own narrative context. E.g. Certain themes and key words emerge that play very significantly into the way I understand and even preach a given passage--themes that can only be discerned when I have been reading and preaching the whole book.
  • The preacher's own Biblical education is best served by expositional preaching. For what greater compliment could be said than that the preacher is wise in the Word of God.
  • On a more practical note, the preacher is spared the doubt of what to preach next.
  • A large portion of exegetical work is accomplished at the "front end" such that sermons through whole books get easier and easier as one progresses through the book. E.g. On the front end, a "Biblical theology" on a given book might require some work-- but it pays off as the series progresses.

4. My view of "progressive revelation" as affirming the unity of the whole Bible in one redemptive history leads me to a redemptive-historical approach to preaching that culminates with Christ.

a. My goal, given my understanding of Scripture within a redemptive history, is for each sermon to be an Emmaus Road experience for the hearer. (note below, this will not mean that I ignore a texts historical location at one level, but rather that I will relate the first level meaning to the a second level meaning as revealed in Jesus Christ.

Luke 24:13 ¶ Now on that same day two of them were going to a village called Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem…15 While they were talking and discussing, Jesus himself came near and went with them, … 25 Then he said to them, "Oh, how foolish you are, and how slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have declared! 26 Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things and then enter into his glory?" 27 Then beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things about himself in all the scriptures.

b. This does not mean that the sermon is monolithic however. There can be many variations of sermon organization depending on the specific genre being preached, yet they will all lead to Christ. One the one hand then expect that God will speak to us in a variety of forms with respect to preaching. ( It could be said that to prefer "story telling" would be then to prefer only one genre of Biblical text. While it is true I suspect that "post-modern" people tend toward "narrative"-- it isn't true that God reduces himself to this genre in speaking to us.) We should expect that if God spoke to us in a multi-dimensional way in the scriptures, then the "truth" itself is multi-dimensional as well and will be better understood if the sermon will respect this. E.g. typical sermon is not only driven by the text with respect to its meaning, but even its form in some respects. For example:

a. OC narrative-- the sermon would ordinarily be more narrative in style and focus upon the "story" of God's intervention throughout redemptive history to preserve the covenant and build his Kingdom. The ultimate "intervention" is Christ and how God preserves His covenant by the work of Christ as received by faith alone… The style best suited to a "narrative" is in fact "story-telling" at the dual level of the immediate context and then as in the greater redemptive context leading to Christ. He hearer will be directed less to their own moral responsibilities and/or duties and more to God's dealings in the past that we might trust his dealings in the future through Christ.

b. Wisdom literature (Proverbs)-- the sermon would take on the form of a "law-grace" kind of organization. The Law as consisting of the wisdom of God would be preached such as to reveal our failure to keep the law-- "Who among you is wise? The hearer, once convinced of his/her failure to keep the law and the folly of their lack of wisdom-- will then be led to Christ who became for us the "wisdom of God." Etc. This "law-grace" form would obviously apply to the law as well. The hearer, by the end of the first part of the sermon will be filled with the despair of sin in their lives-- realizing by the gravity and depth of the "law" just how holy is God and how unholy we are. This then lead to the second part of the sermon which then expounds upon the greater depth and greater power of grace as accomplished by Christ and applied to us by the Holy Spirit in the gift of faith.

c. New Covenant Epistle-- the genre is more didactic and will require a more didactic vs. narrative style of preaching. Moreover, the form will follow the epistles themselve which tend toward a "doctrine-application" kind of structure- for lack of a better description, a this was the style most prominent in puritan preaching. It would be important thenpreach a given section of an epistle with the other section in view. So that if the sermon was on Rom.12, the theology of Salvation in Rom 1:18-11 would be kept in view so as to demonstrate that our living sacrificial lives is properly done not in order to be saved but as in response to our salvation by Christ. Therefore, the sermon would be "saving grace doctrine- how then shall we live" kind of style. The "grace" then is viewed not only as saving us from the penalty of our sin, but also the sins themselves and their subsequent miseries even in this life. ("For the grace of God has been revealed, teaching us to deny ungodliness and worldly desires." Titus 211) And yet this "grace" is viewed as in the imitation of Christ and in the power that Christ supplies, never in order to be saved but as a consequence of being saved. The primary motivation then for morals is gratitude given the doctrine of grace that are before the morals.

My point is that while all texts are not preached in the same way, they all lead to Christ.

5. I hold to a biblical-theological approach to preaching (given my understanding that all texts are covenantally conditioned). A covenant hermeneutic will show itself in relating a given passage to the relevant covenant conditions, promises, curses-- both with respect to its immediate covenant context and secondly, when not a New covenant passage, as the meaning would translate into the New Covenant as accomplished by Christ. Thus, in the Old Testament, (and I believe significant portions of the gospels as Christ ministers "under the law.") the need for a two level analysis. 1 to first understand a given text in its own redemptive context, and then 2) to locate the point in the greater redemptive story that leads ultimately to Christ. (See E.P. Clowney, Preaching and Biblical Theology) One of the advantages to this is to help the preacher avoid moralizing certain passages through false analogies between a biblical narrative and contemporary life.

6. The "vernacular" sermon consist of more than merely speaking in the same language type. The preacher will want to consider the various demographic realities that might shape the form and circumstance of the preaching event. E.g. How long, style ( formal vs. informal), dress of preacher, the degree to which "demonstrations of logic" are necessary, tone (preachy vs. more conversational), etc. Not only will the preacher need to know the people, but study them and their "street language" kinds of communications. (Note, I think the issue of vernacular will also play out in the kind of worship style we have, the kind of music and accompaniment, etc-- while I would not let demographics control the content of worship or the "elements" included in worship-- say whether to serve Lord's supper or not-- I would put a certain content into a medium that is more or less vernacular. Thus, I hold to a kind of principled vernacular worship service. I recognize that this opens up a whole other set of issues not directly relevant to these "thoughts." ) A vernacular sermon is no less a God-centered sermon. Nor is a vernacular sermon any less of the authoritative voice of God a mediated through the preaching event. My point is that the what this is communicated will in fact be relative at some level to the people themselves. At the end of the day, it is my goal that all people have gotten the sense of "thus saith the Lord"-- albeit as within their own demographic sensitive way.

7. The place of "person" in the preaching event: The character of the preacher IS relevant, although in a qualified sense.

    • One the one hand, the preacher should speak out of office less out of person. This means that I try to distance myself and my own struggles from what I can say in the pulpit. Many, many times, in fact just about all the time, I would not be able to preach if I thought that in some manner I lived up to the implications of the sermon.
    • However, the people ought to see sincerity in the preacher, especially in so far as he/she is reliant upon the grace of the gospel such as to offer no pretence of "measuring up" to the "law" so to speak.
    • A preacher, in so far as he/she is an "elder" by way of the faith, ought to be exemplary. (which is NOT to be confused with perfect) I do suspect there are certain sins that rightly are forgivable but are not forgettable in so far as the calling to the office of preacher is concerned. E..g. Some "sins" are just such as to seriously harm the trust needed in the person filling the office.
    • While it is certainly my goal to be "one of them" during the course of the week, I am also mindful that I am not "one of them" as God's spokesmen on Sunday morning. This creates a kind of "distance" that is both difficult personally but I think positive.
    • Therefore, every preacher should consider being accountable to some other person outside the congregation perhaps with respect to his/her moral life. Also someone that can help you find perspective when you are discouraged, etc.
    • Again, it is my opinion, but I think too often preachers bring too much of their own problems and struggles to the pulpit out of their own insecurities. While I do think there is room for some degree of vulnerability in the pulpit, at least enough that the people see that you are being preached too along with everyone else-- it becomes a sad situation if in fact the congregation finds themselves without a "voice from God" because of the human voice that has become too prominent.
    • In short, people come less to hear the person preaching and more the voice of God albeit mediated through the human incarnation of that voice via the human preacher. This is perhaps the greatest mystery in preaching.

III. Some practical tips for the pastor in moving from text to sermon: (keeping in mind that the process is less a science and more an art)

1. A Process:

i. Determine a self-contained unit of scripture within literary structure of the book. (this is a relatively simple matter of knowing the book coupled with your pastoral insights)

ii. Exegesis from the original text-- see Stuart's Old Testament Exegesis

Note: A pastor's schedule will quickly wean you from unnecessary exegetical rabbit trails-- i.e. your energy willnot be spent following those questions that will follow various secondary and tertiary matters that might be raised by a given text. E.g. Ordinarily, the "main" point drives the research. During this phase of work, I will ordinarily organize my work less under Stuart's "steps" and more under three broad headings-- translation (which might include sentence flow analysis), Word Studies, and exegetical Notes (which could include anything from grammatical observations to cross referencing to even theological reflection)

iii. Create an exegetical outline

a. "Style" is determined by the form of the passage itself-- narrative, didactic, law-grace, etc.

b. The content is less determined by the things I discovered and more by what will have a rhetorical value-- e.g. Inevitably, you will have more exegetical material than would be useful to a good sermon. You will want to organize and regulate your exegetical points rhetorically-- e.g. as to its value in demonstrating the point both convincingly and economically (and be sure you have a point, one point, that will tie the whole sermon together)

iv. Create the sermon outline: One typical way to do it (although some prefer to combine all three parts intermittently-- my real concern is that we think of 3 aspects to a sermon, less three linear steps…)

Part 1-- Exegetical outline with transitions and development(35-40% of serrmon) moving toward a clear conclusion

Conclusion or main-point clearly stated

Part 2-- Pastoral/Theological/Sociological development of mainpoint (40-50%) which lead toward "exhortation ( I often will combine part 2 and part 3)

    • Ways to develop the point include the various rhetorical aspects of say compare and contrast, Ironies, even humor when natural to the flow. (I hardly ever "plan" humor, jokes, etc. I think it detracts from sermon and makes it into an entertainment medium less a sermon. However, I do allow for "natural" humor that in fact serves the message. I do at time plan for humor if the passage deals with a subject that is already "hot" in the community-- a kind of cooling down so that we can really see the point kind of a tactic.)
    • Illustrations are helpful ONLY when they really do serve the point rather than oratory itself. Again, I rarely give more than one illustration per sermon, and even then somewhat carefully and when genuinely relevant.
    • In short, I believe that the ideas of the text are in themselves interesting and are better kept in view…

Part 3-- Exhortation (application, response, etc) (10-20%)
    • I think it is a real mistake to artificially contrive applications-- some texts should simply lead to thankfulness and worship, reflection as to the nature and goodness of God, etc.
    • I believe more sermons are harmed at this stage than perhaps any other-- for the simple reason that the "exhortation" has little to do with the intention of the passage, which then undermines the authority of scripture itself-- the exact opposite of what a good sermon should be doing.
    • Always, always ask the "so what" question. Again, no so as to contrive an application, but to think about how the passage in its greater redemptive context now is relevant to the hearer today.

2. A Schedule: Schedule your sermon preparation early in the week. (I try to have the sermon written no later than Thursday lunch.)

  • A pastor's schedule is hardly ever predictable.
  • I try to schedule at least a full day between the sermon prep. and the day I preach the sermon so as to be more personally affected by the fresh preaching of the passage myself. I also think my delivery is more genuinely "fresh" as a result.
  • More time to "meditate" on the passage in order to discover its profundity.

A typical week for me (which I realize is highly subjective) is as follows:

Tuesday AM-- Textual translation, grammar, literary analysis, etc.

Wed AM-- Commentary readings, Theology readings, etc.

Thurs. AM-- write sermon

3. A Product: I believe that good sermons will balance three things-- which then will be reflected in what we read and study.

1. Text driven-- such that we will want to be students of the Bible as by our doing-- week in and week out-- the necessary exegetical work. From exegesis, the sermon will derive its main point and perhaps also its "form"-- e.g. rhetorical, narrative, law-grace, etc. (see below)

2. Confessional-- A good sermon is not "academic" in the sense that we can personally detach ourselves from what we are preaching and hearing. Rather it is to feed our beliefs as these impact our lives. According to 1 Tim.3:15, it is the church that is the "pillar and bulwark of the truth." Beliefs are best guarded by the church and not by a single individual such that the preacher should be humble to recognize this. This is why the preacher ought to be fluent in his/her tradition and a student of theology in general. (I assume here a basic appreciation for confessionalism.)

3. Vernacular-- I mean by this more than semantics. A good sermon is in the language and demographic ethos of the people. While the text drives the main point of a sermon and a confession locates the text within a theological "orthodoxy," the unique demographic realities of the people themselves will drive some aspects of the form and/or circumstance of the sermon. The preacher then will want to be a student of sociology and especially "street stuff" not only that he/she might better understand the relevance of a given passage, but so as to better understand the best way to communicate it.
