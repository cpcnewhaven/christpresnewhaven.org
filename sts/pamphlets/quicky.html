A QUICKIE ON COVENANT THEOLOGY
David A. Sherwood and slightly modified by P. Graham


A covenant may be defined as an administration of the Kingdom of God. God is a king, and he enters into a relationship with his subjects, providing certain benefits, making certain demands (stipulations), making promises and threats, and swearing to uphold the covenant. To one degree or another, these are the elements of the covenants that God makes.

We may use covenant as a theological term when we refer to the Covenant of Creation (Works) and the Covenant of Redemption (Grace, although this is not so much a "new covenant" as it is a promise to give grace through the satisfaction of the "works" covenant by a representative substitute-- see Kline). Under the original covenant made with humanity, people could realize God's favor by perfect obedience to his will. When this covenant was broken, God promised a gracious provision, whereby he dealt graciously with sinners, accepting them ultimately on the basis of the work of Christ. As such, God's covenant in this sense is a thread running through different periods of redemption, different biblical covenants, culminating in the New Covenant made through the death and resurrection of Christ. The two most fundamental elements which run through the various covenants is the combination of works and grace. No covenant made by God lack either. Both elements are manifest through the varioius old covenants so as to foreshadow the ultimate satisfaction of works and grace in the Christ who is described as the second Adam in scripture and also the second Moses, etc.

But covenant is also a biblical term which is used to describe various contracts between human to human, and for our present purposes, human to God. And we must not permit our belief in one covenant of grace to obscure the reality of a variety of divine covenants made known in Scripture. There is a covenant with Adam, with Noah before the Flood (which in turn refers to an earlier covenant at Gen. 3:15), with Noah after the Flood, with Abraham, with Moses, with David, and the New Covenant. These are all distinct covenants which God makes with particular groups of people. It is the parties of the covenant (God and whoever) that are obligated to the terms of the covenant (i.e. the requirements and consequences of obedience and disobedience). We are under the New Covenant. Thus there are very few questions about the applicability of the terms of this covenant to us. We are also under the Covenant with Noah after the Flood, since this was made with all creation until the end of the world. But we are not under every covenant that God has ever made. In particular, we are not under the Sinai/Mosaic Covenant. It was not made with us. Therefore we are not somehow automatically subject to its terms. Of course, we will expect to find much in the Law of Moses regarding what we are to believe concerning God and what duty God requires of us. But especially when it comes to our duty, we must be careful to let the revelation of the Sinai Coveant "pass through" the grid of the New Covenant, the latter being the covenant which we are under. Only then may we accurately appreciate the contemporary significance of God's revelation at Sinai.

The strength of covenant theology is and always has been to accent the unity and continuity of God's redemptive purposes throughout history. Our commitment to this truth, while good in itself, must not permit us to lose sight of the differences that emerge in God's several covenantal dealings with people and the ways in which our duty to God may differ, depending on which covenant we find ourselves under.
