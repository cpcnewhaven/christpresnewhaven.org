Our Strange Love...


Many of you have commented how the expositions through 1 Peter are making a difference in your life. That we "live as strangers in this world" (1 Peter 1:17ff) makes sense of the obvious realities associated with our being citizens not of this world but of a heavenly world. It explains why we often feel weird around our neighbors and colleagues when we really do take our faith seriously. Ours is a strange way of life if led by conviction and not social convenience. It is strange that we hope not in degrees, financial portfolios, material possessions or popularity, but rather in a redemption purchased by the precious blood of Christ. It is strange that those who follow Christ consider it worth their while to devote a significant portion of their temporal resources to God, especially in a world that is increasingly ambitious for personal freedoms and comforts. It is strange that we would understand our devotion to God as within an assembled/corporate context rather than merely a private devotion, especially in a world that is increasingly individualistic even in its religious expressions. And so, ours is a strange way of life-- but is it merely for the sake of being strange? Obviously not. The scriptures teach that our strangeness ought to extend itself in love, albeit a strange kind of love!

It was Christ, you will remember, who said the strangest things about love-- how for instance we ought to love not only those considered our allies, but also our enemies. And of course it was Christ that defined love as sacrificial rather than self-serving. Both these descriptions indicate that our love is strange when it exceeds our own personal interest somehow. And it was Christ's example-of a strange kind of love that he came into the world not because it was to HIS interest to come, but rather for our interest. (Philip 2:6ff) As to authentic Christian discipleship, Paul tells us therefore to imitate Christ's love! (Eph.5:1-2) So too, it was the "love of Christ" that compelled Paul to take the gospel and his acts of service, not only to his own kind, but to those of another kind, even the Greeks! Likewise, ours will be a strange love when we consider the interest of others even before our own so as to consider our time and resources spent for the good of others as worthy of our own time and resources-- and herein is the pattern of Christ's strange kind of love (Philip.2: 3ff)

This strange kind of love is in vivid contrast to what we see ordinarily. More often than not, we see benevolence and altruism as with some personal agenda or so as to eventually gain some kind of advantage, or extended to those who share our interest. Sadly, this seems all the more true by those who call themselves Christians who often find it ironically "unspiritual" to really "do good" to those who are their political or even moral opposites. In a world of political posturing, we find it strange when love is bi-partisan. Amidst our modern culture wars, it seems strange if say a pro-lifer were to serve, really serve, a pro-abortioner. I wonder, would Christ let his political, even moral "opposite" park in his parking lot, or would he participate in political finger pointing and posturing while keeping his "enemies" at a distance? Would Christ think it possible to have a genuine conversation with an adulterer or thief, or would he distance himself and his services from such people? Somehow, it seems that Christ was able to maintain his righteousness while also loving his enemies, all of whom were real sinners. And aren't we glad!

Again, it is strange for someone to love another when the "another" is not somehow invested in our private interest. It is too often strange that we support such and such activity or serve such and such ministry when it does not "interest" us so as to represent our "special interest" And yet, wouldn't this be more the strange kind of love that would consider others interests as more important than our own? This is why it is so exciting to see people of different stages of life, different regions of our city, different ethnic origins, even different nationalities, serving one another as was the scene two Saturdays ago at the study center-- painting for the common good as it where. This is why we will forever work against the "special interest" mentality that can so easily divid up the body of Christ. Our strange love will inspire a discipleship that enables people respresenting "hands and feet, noses and eyes" to not only mingle, but to genuinely love one another-- like Paul's exhortation to the Romans, "let love be genuine." It was immediately following his description of a wonderfully diverse body of Christ, where each part of the body was formed as an organic and integrated whole. In a few verses later, Paul speaks of "contributing to the needs of the saints"as an expression of their love, and yet he was asking the Romans to contribute to the people in Jerusalem-- how strange--people they didn't even know.

At CPC, we might want to target special needs at times, but God forbid we establish identity barriers such that in all practicality, one part of the body can feel or act as if they are not obliged to or in need of other parts of the body. I think it has been our greatest strength at CPC, that in the face of a world that more and more splinters into special interest groups, we have tried to break down organizational barriers to loving one another across special interest categories. We have striven for more integreation, not less. We have sought for ways to unite the town-gown, center city-edge city, singles-married, parents-nonparents, women-men, nations-internationals, etc. etc. I am still hopeful that one day we might also unite the middle class-survival class. It is this strange kind of love that will be our greatest apologetic to the truth of Christ. It is what makes our communion table all the more authentic. Please join me in praying for more love without boundaries-- that in the words of the apostle, "our love may abound more and more" "God help us to maintain the beatific vision of a love all the more strange for its being after the pattern of Christ's love, who looked not to his own interest but to ours, who came not to be served but to serve.
