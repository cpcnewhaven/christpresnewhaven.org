A Response to Historicism in Biblical Interpretation

by Preston Graham, Jr.


To what extent can the reader of the Bible attain objectivity in interpretation? Paradigmatic of the modern answer, Hans-Georg Gadamer has written:

In view of the historicity of our being, the rehabilitation of (a text's) original conditions is a futile undertaking. What is rehabilitated from an alien past is not the original. In its continued alienation it has a merely secondary existence." (Quoted by E.D. Hirsch, Jr., Validity in Interpretation, New Haven: Yale University Press; 1967 p. 247 from his personal translation of Georg Hans Gadamar, Wahrheit und Methode, For the rest of this paper, Truth and Method p. 159)

This statement by Gadamer features a skepticism that Mark Noll has described as the "crisis in historical knowledge." ("Traditional Christianity and the Possibility of Historical Knowledge", Christian Scholar's Review, Volume XIX Number 4, 1990, p.388-406) Any and all aspirations toward objectivity in modern hermeneutics is at best considered to be naive. As noted by Noll, "the result is that for the last few years rumors of an `epistemological crisis' concerning the status of knowledge about the past have swept even the out-of-the way landscapes that historians inhabit." (Noll, p.391)

The crisis in historical knowledge is particularly relevant for the Christian in two interrelated landscapes of scholarly attention. The first landscape pertains to the historicity of the events recorded in the Bible. Skepticism regarding the actuality of the miraculous events in the Bible has called into question the veracity of the Biblical record itself. As a result, Historical-Criticism emerged as the primary carrier of a skepticism regarding the historical accurateness of the Bible. As described by Gerhard Maier in his book, The End of the Historical-Critical Method, the decisive impetus for this movement can be traced to the assumptions produced by three interrelated Enlightenment movements. In the case of English deism, "human reason served as a touchstone and yardstick of everything revealed in Scripture...From French skepticism 'transcendence was finished off' as epitomized by the work of Immanual Kant. ...From the German enlightenment the zeal for "freedom" was trafficked into a "freedom from divine principles of scripture and revelation." (Gerhard Maier, p. 13-14) Common to all three is the anti-supernatural bias which sought to distinguish between spurious faith derived from ancient superstition and genuine faith, both of which are said to be contained within the Scriptures.

Known as the father of modern Historical-Criticism, Johann Salomo Semler reveals the basic yet subtle conviction of the adherents to the Historical-Critical method. He states, "The root of the evil (in theology) is the interchangeable use of the terms `Scripture' and the `Word of God'." (Quoted by Maier, p.15) Stated succinctly, the agenda established for the Historical-Critical method is the attempt to discover the "canon within the canon", one being the true "Word of God," the other being those portions of scripture depicting erroneous superstitions or myths of ancient history. Yet it is important to note what Maier recognizes as the fundamental method intrinsic to this agenda, namely, to construct an analogous relationship between present historical realities and those realities of the ancient history concurrent with the Biblical period. As stated by Maier, "as long as one makes analogous classification a precondition for acceptance, much in the word of the Bible remains without foundation." (Maier. p.16) Therefore, what is doubted in this first landscape of debate concerning the objectivity of historical knowledge is the veracity of the history recorded in the Bible itself. The method for determination is an analogous classification between the present and the past such that the present is allowed to sit in judgment over Biblical accounts of the past.

The second major area of scholarly attention concerning the crisis of historical knowledge pertains to the ability to look through the lens of historical distance at an ancient text in order to understand the author's original intent. The "New Hermeneutic" as espoused by Hans-Georg Gadamer has provided a justification for interpretations "that are unencumbered by a concern for the author's original intention." (Hirsch. p.246) As already noted in the earlier quote, Gadamer's impetus rests primarily in a skepticism concerning the possibility of objective historical knowledge. In reference to Gadamer, E.D. Hirsch notes in his book, Validity in Interpretation,

If an interpreter cannot overcome the distorting perspective of his own historicity, no matter how hard he tries, then it follows that `one understands differently when one understands at all. (Hirsch. p.252.)

Hirsch identifies the primary grounds for Gadamer's "anti-intentionalism" as the "radical historicism of Martin Heideggar." (Ibid. p.246) For instance, Gadamer writes, "Distance in time could only be thought of in its hermeneutical productiveness after Heideggar had lent an existential sense to the idea of understanding." (Quoted by Hirsch. p.246-257) As this statement suggests, in the case of Gadamer, a skepticism regarding objective historical knowledge resulted not in surgically removing portions of scripture from the pristine portion of the Word of God as in the case of the historical-critical method, but in an optimism which seeks to find ever changing interpretations which go beyond the author's intent. The historicity of the events recorded in the Bible is secondary for Gadamer. Rather for Gadamer, the primary agenda is to allow the existential present to act upon a historical past in such a way as to create ever new and expanding meanings for the present. This is clearly articulated by Gadamer when he states,

The meaning of a text goes beyond its author not just sometimes but always. Understanding is not a reproductive but always a productive activity... the winning of the true sense contained in a text or artistic work never comes to an end. It is an infinite process. (Quoted by Hirsch. p.249 from Gadamar's Truth and Method. p.280, 282)

Again, it is important to understand the fundamental method employed by Gadamer's New Hermeneutic. And again, like the historical-critical method, an analogy is made between the present and the past yet for the very different purpose of expanding a historical text rather than to reduce it. In other words, by a "fusion of horizons", the present perspective of the interpreter is allowed to act upon a historical text in such a way as to expand and even change an author's intended meaning. As Gadamer states. The real meaning of a text as it addresses itself to an interpreter... is always codetermined by the historical situation of the interpreter. (Quoted by Hirsch. p.253 form Truth and Method.) In order to make this epistemological move, Gadamer distinguishes between the "sache" and the "res" of the text, the "res" being the mental processes of the author that is inaccessible and the "sache" being the subject matter of the text that "while independent of the author and reader, is shared by both." (Hirsch. p.247)

So far, I have wanted to make the point that both the "New Hermeneutic" and the "Historical-Critical Method", while differing in their agenda, share two things in common. First, both share in the method of constructing an analogous relationship between the present and the past, one for the purpose of classifying true from false portions of scripture as the "Word of God", the other for the purpose of discovering the meaning of historical texts with an existential bias for the present. Secondly, both the New Hermeneutic and the Historical-Critical Method have as their impetus a radical relativism concerning historical knowledge. Ronald Nash describes radical relativism to be "the view that impartial and objective historical knowledge about anything in the past is impossible." (Ronald Nash, Christian Faith and Historical Understanding,(Zondervan: Grand Rapids, Mich.; 1984) p.77)

What is at stake for the Christian is nothing less than the foundation of the Christian faith. On the one hand, as asserted by Mark Noll, confessional Christians affirm that "their very existence is defined by the meaning of purportedly historical events." (Noll. p.392) On the other hand, "implicit in affirming these events in the history of salvation is a definite view of historical understanding. These, events, Christians hold, may be known to be factual, and there may be a reasonable degree of certainty as to what these long-past events mean for our lives in the late twentieth century." (Noll. p.392) Therefore, the counterpart of revelation for the Christian is not critique but submission. The foundation of Biblical authority over an individual Christian presupposes that the Bible has its own, autonomous voice of which it is possible for the modern Christian to hear in spite of his/her own personal subjectivity.

A tempting "knee-jerk" reaction to this radical relativism by the Christian is to opt for a radical historical objectivism. Nash defines the latter as a "hard objectivism" such that "all subjectivity can in principle be eliminated from history, a fact that if true, would make history a value-free inquiry." (Nash. p.80) Yet upon further scrutiny, this also places in jeopardy any optimism toward gaining access to historical Christianity in that "anything less than a complete and impartial account of some event or series of events in the past would be considered bad history."(Nash. p.78) For instance, once it is conceded that the New Testament writers are partisan interpreters of the life and teaching of Christ, the historical veracity of those writers is called into question. Likewise, a pro-bias for God in any modern Christian interpreter could, in principle, be used as the grounds to annul all attempts to get at a Biblical author's original intent.

What then is a proper response to radical relativism concerning historical knowledge without falling into a radical historical objectivism? Certainly the fact that the interpreter is not value free does not necessitate a view that his/her interpretation is either arbitrary or beyond objective criticism and revision based upon objective historical knowledge! In constructing a response to historical relativism, a deductive mode of reasoning is preferred over an inductive one for the simple reason that the inherent assumptions of the discussion are immediately exposed. Only after these metahistorical issues are uncovered can a response to the particular forms of radical relativism be profitable.

To begin, it ought to be noted that the Christian understanding of God and the corresponding cosmology is in direct contrast to the cosmology derived from the Enlightenment view of God. Fueled by a fundamental faith in epistemological foundationalism, the enlightenment agenda embarked upon a method which presupposed a confidence in the capabilities of human rationality such that all other beliefs needed to be rationally demonstrable. In that God could not be demonstrated as argued by Kant in his Critique of Pure Reason, at best God was conceived of in the deistical notion that "God made the world, established its laws, created humans with certain mental capabilities to understand the world, and then stepped back to see what would happen." (Noll. p.399) This view was naively adopted by Christians such that a search for God was contained to the search for arguments from Design and Definition in order to justify faith. Yet fundamental to the deistic notion of God is a cosmology that accepts as its premise a "closed system universe", that is, a universe void of transcendent God that is also imminent with respect to a sovereign activity within this world. Likewise, historical knowledge without the aid of a transcendent God who is active in the universe is confined to existential or rationalistic subjectivity, bound to the historicity of the historian or interpreter. Therefore, under the premise of a closed system universe, the only means for the attainment of historical knowledge is that of making an analogy between the past and the present whereby the present is given the epistemological advantage.

Against these Enlightenment conceptions of God, Mark Noll notes how the Christian faith affirms that "God is not just the creator and passive sustainer of the world, but also that His energy is the source of the world's energy and His will the foundation of its existence." (Ibid. p.398) As further noted by Noll:

Christians like Malebranche, Berkeley and Edwards postulated a deity who filled the universe he had created, who activated the minds he had made in his own image, who brooded over the world with constant love as well as distant power. This is the sort of Christianity that can rescue Historical knowledge. (Ibid. p.399)

A stress upon God's benevolent sovereignty suggests a radical dependency upon God for all historical understanding. Not only does God move and sustain history, but he also must give to the human mind His intentions and explanations for the understanding of history itself. Furthermore, a belief in the benevolence of God maintains that it is God's intention to share with humans an understanding of reality as he sustains it. (For the basic content of these statements, see Noll. p.398) In the words of J.O. Urmson, there is a "metaphysical reality" that can be explained by "the activity of God." (Noll. p.398; Quoting from J.O.Urmson, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1982 p.37 ) Perhaps stated most succinctly by Jonathan Edwards,

That which truly is the substance of all bodies is the infinitely exact and precise and perfectly stable idea in God's mind, together with his stable will that the same shall gradually be communicated to us, and to other minds, according to certain fixed and exact established methods and laws. (Noll. p.398 Quoting Jonathan Edwards, "The Mind", The Works of Jonathan Edwards: Scientific and Philosophical Writings, ed. Wallace Anderson (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1980), 344

Therefore, faith in God, who is present in history and active in the universe rather than in the capabilities of humans as defined by Enlightenment visionaries enables us to restore a fundamental confidence in historical knowledge. Rather than a closed system universe, a Christian world view adheres to the belief in the God "who is there and is not silent" (to borrow a phrase from Francis Schaeffer) and therefore an "open system universe." Derived from the fundamental belief in a Sovereign God who is active in the universe, Noll argues that there are four basic Christian beliefs which together rescue a working confidence in historical knowledge. (Noll. p.299-405 ) Together, they remove the epistomological bias of present experience in the task of understanding historical texts. They could be described as the four pillars resting upon the Sovereignty of God that support a Biblical cosmology or an open system universe.

1. An open system universe affirms that "the divine creation and sustaining of the world is the foundation for epistemological confidence of whatever sort." (Ibid. p.399) There is a correspondence between our minds and God's mind such that historical knowledge is possible.

2. An open system universe can affirm the doctrine of the fall such that "the resultant depravity of human nature suggests that the human moral condition obscures vision, presumably for historical as well as moral reasoning." (Ibid. p.401) Therefore, there is no guarantee for a perfect knowledge of the universe due to the fall and the noetic repercussion of sin upon human thought. There is truth in the fact that all knowledge, whether historical or scientific, is a function of some point of view and in that sense relativistic. Yet it is helpful to distinguish between what Nash describes as a "hard relativism" as previously described and a "soft relativism." By "soft", Nash means that while history is subjective, it is not arbitrary. "Just because a historian cannot know everything about the past, does it follow that he cannot know anything about it?" (Nash. p.85) This means that an interpreter's interpretation of an ancient text or the historian's reconstruction of the past is always open to criticism and revision without being considered an arbitrary interpretation or reconstruction. "In Christian terms: We do see, but through a glass, darkly." (Noll. p.404)

3. An open system universe can affirm that writing history or interpreting an ancient text "from the point of view of a particular culture becomes not only inescapable, but also divinely ordained and good. Relativism, in other words, has a divine sanction." Noll. p.401 It is true that to get at an ancient text, one must look through the lens of his/her cultural and historical particularities. However, the skepticism that is usually associated with this subjective reality is rendered unfounded given an open system universe and the belief in a benevolent, sovereign God who is active in history. What's to say, then, that the historical distance is necessarily harmful to objective understanding? In the case of eye vision, for instance, the optician might construct a series of lenses which together promote sound and proper vision in the patient. The lens actually correct, not distort the vision. What is to say that the lens of historical and cultural dimensions, constructed by a sovereign God working in history, might not serve to correct an understanding of the past rather than inhibit it. Whose to say that the historical and cultural distance might not provide the needed perspective to understand the significance of an author's intended meaning rather than preventing one from understanding the author? In other words, the skepticism itself is what ought to be questioned if one holds to an open system universe. Skepticism, that is, that functions as an a priori presupposition to interpreting historical texts.

4. An open system universe and a Biblical understanding of the cosmos means that the "recognition that it is God who brings about belief is the source not of despair, but of hope." (Ibid. p.405) The stress in the Bible is that even the ability to believe rests upon God's activity of revelation. (1 Cor.6-16) God must alter our frame of reference before we are eager to come to God and know His mind. (Jn.6:37) Yet this is a source of hope in that my own epistemological frailties can be overcome through the divine intervention of a Sovereign and benevolent God who is there. Noll writes, "To be sure, a person will not be able to experience the satisfactions of the Christian paradigm until that person undergoes a revolution, a gestalt shift from a paradigm defined by the centrality of the self to one defined by the centrality of God." (Noll. p.405) While there is a certain degree of knowledge accessible in the natural laws and order of creation, there is the anticipation of revelation by God's special grace that begins to overcome the limitations imposed by our fallen state.

Having constructed the foundations for epistemological optimism concerning historical knowledge, it is now possible to direct some questions to the particular two methods described earlier. Of special interest is the method of analogy used by both. For instance, Maier asks concerning the Historical-Critical Method, "To be sure, as long as one makes analogous classification a precondition for acceptance, much in the world of the Bible remains without foundation. But how can the pure historian without further ado reject something just because it happens only once? What can be experienced and what has analogies certainly can not be declared synonymous." (Maier. p.16) Upon closer scrutiny, the a priori skepticism proves to be the determinative element of Historical-Criticism. Therefore, the conclusion is established prior to the interpretation, a conclusion without any foundation upon which to rest.

Maier further notes that the Historical-Critical method is itself proof of the faulty premise in that the task of discovering the "canon within the canon" has proven to be practically impossible. He states, "none of them were able to delimit or even discover a convincing canon in the canon." (Maier.p. 27) First, scripture gives no clear criteria for distinguishing between the Word of God and the Scriptures. While it is true that the Bible is the Word of God in human words, it doesn't permit itself to be separated into "either/or" categories , but rather "both/and categories." As it turns out, Maier puts his finger directly on the crux of the matter when he writes, "this method would take human reason out of the fall into sin and use it critically, i.e. to discriminate and make judgments in matters of revelation... accordingly, the higher-critical method, as a matter of basic principle, means a procedure according to which the Bible is approached from an extra-Biblical position and with extra-Biblical standards with the objective of discovering the Word of God in the process." (Maier. p.24)

With respect to the "new hermeneutic" of Gadamer, Hirsch (like Maier) is also particularly interested in the method of analogy used by Gadamer and the questions that can be raised against it. For instance, Hirsch quotes Gadamer as writing, "In the process of understanding there always occurs a true fusion of perspectives in which the projection of the historical perspective really brings about a sublation of the same." (Hirsch. p.254, quoting from Truth and Method,p. 290) Hirsch then asks the question, "How can an interpreter fuse two perspectives-- his own and that of the text-- unless he has somehow appropriated the original perspective and amalgamated it to his own? How can a fusion take place unless the things to be fused are made actual, which is to say, unless the original sense of the text has been understood?... Indeed, how can it be affirmed that the original sense of a text is beyond our reach and at the same time that valid interpretation is impossible?" (Hirsch. p.254) The way Hirsch answers his questions is in keeping with the basic premise of his book, Validity in Interpretation. He states, "the fundamental distinction overlooked by Gadamer is that between the `meaning' of the text and the `significance' of that meaning to a present situation."(Hirsch. p.255) The meaning of the text is that which the author meant "by his particular use of linguistic symbols" whereas the significance of the text is the "interpreter's construing and understanding of textual meaning."(Ibid. p.255) It is the difference between "teaching" and "application." The former presupposes an objectivity whereas the latter our own subjectivity.
Again, like Maier concerning Historical-Criticism, Hirsch exposes the primary logic of Gadamer to be the inherent assumption of radical historicism. He notes, "The doctrine of radical historicity is ultimately a dogma, an idea of reason, an act of faith." (Ibid. p.256-257) Of considerable interest is the way Hirsch is honest enough to acknowledge the same for the opposite doctrine as he states, "So of course is the contrary doctrine: not all acts of understanding fail to re-cognize past meanings." Then he further notes, "While either dogma could be falsified, one may well be more probable than the other."(Ibid. p.257)

The attempt has been made to give a response to what has proven to be the "dogma" of radical relativism in historical understanding. As manifest in both Historical-Criticism and the New Hermeneutic, the fundamental premise of radical relativism is the belief in a closed system universe. Its primary method consists of making an analogy between the past and the present giving the present an epistemological advantage. With respect to Historical-Criticism, the present experience is used to distinguish between the true and pseudo Word of God in Scripture. For the New Hermeneutic, an analogy is made between the past and the present such that a "fusion of horizons" produce ever changing and expanding meanings with little or no concern for the author's original intent. Both share the same skepticism concerning historical knowledge. In response, the attempt has been made to construct the Biblical "dogma" which depicts an open system cosmology that understands God to be benevolent, sovereign and active in all of history such that epistemological optimism is cogent. While it would exceed the purpose of this paper to fully explicate the implications of the open system cosmology pertaining to historical and hermeneutical method, it ought to at least be mentioned.

The optimism in historical knowledge can be manifest in a variety of ways. Primarily, many of the observations and practices of the two skeptical methods can be appreciated if the principle of analogy is first disqualified as an epistemological necessity. Rather than asserting ourselves in our own unique historicity upon the epistemological throne, we confess our radical dependence upon the God who preserves us moment by moment and who alone acts on us and for us such that our knowledge is a derivitive of divine knowledge graciously imparted. As noted by Maier, we dare not "critique" the Bible in that "the Bible is a book `sui generis' (peculiar to itself)." (Maier. p.51) If God is indeed Sovereign, then it would be a trait of His to reveal Himself whenever and in whatever means He so chooses. Therefore, there is a need to replace the Historical-Critical Method with what might be described as the "Historical-Biblical Method."

A Biblical-Historical Method will first of all value textual analysis (finding the text given the varients of represented in a multiple of manuscripts). Secondly, an optimism about discovering the intended meaning of the Biblical authors will result in the necessity of working in the original languages for the purpose of lexical, grammatical, syntactical and discourse analysis. Thirdly, a Biblical-Historical Method will take into consideration the literary and historical context as with the literary genre. Fourth, the Biblical-Historical method will seek to understand the redemptive-history represented within the scripture itself, both in its unity, as the single plan of God moving forward and also in its differentiations between different redemptive periods. (For further discussion on Interpretation and Biblcial Theology, see the following: Edmund P. Clowney, Preaching and Biblcial Theology, Presbyterian and Reformed Publishing Co.: Phillipsburg, New Jersey, 1961; Vern Sheridan Poythress, "Divine Meaning of Scripture", WTJ 48 ,1986, pp.241-279.) The Historical-Biblical Method will seek to be "Biblical-Theological" in its search for unity as well as diversity in the Scripture. And finally, an optimism in historical knowledge will maintain that a distinction between meaning and significance ought to be maintained in Biblical interpretation. As noted by Hirsch, "The fact that our interpretations are always governed by our prejudices is really the best guarantee that texts will have significance for us. Instead of trying to overcome our prejudices, we should welcome them as the best means of preserving the vitality of our inheritance and our tradition." (Ibid.p.260) In conclusion, Mark Noll writes,

The historiographical conclusion, then, is that Christian teaching offers a solution to the crisis of historical knowledge, and indeed of scientific and other kinds of knowledge. The nature of the solution is to propose that we can have reliable knowledge and real understanding of the past, but that this knowledge and understanding must be modest. Christian teaching does not warrant the belief that we can obtain full and complete understanding, but it does provide reasons for a chastening realism about our grasp of history and indeed for other areas of research. (Noll. p.404)

And as noted by Maier, this move in method from the Historical critical to the Biblical-Historical is not a "sacrifice of intelligence but rather a sacrifice of pride." (Maier. p.54)
