Answer: Story of Exodus was nothing short of “The Gospel according to Moses” 

 

He knew that if his religion regarding Christ was new (and for us only 2000 yrs) that it was no true religion at all!

 

To help us understand what this was so, we will today seek to understand something that Peter said in in his first sermon at Pentecost, a quote from Deut. as related to the redemptive historical significance of Moses the prophet and OT mediator of God’s salvation to Israel from Egypt. 

 

Exposition:

 

2 Initial Observations:

 

1.  Principle of Prophetic succession: Offered as evidence that Jesus was in fact the Messiah, Peter quotes from the words of Moses himself in Dt. 18, which established the expectation of a prophet greater even than Moses who would mediate an even more profound and significant deliverance than Moses did. 

22 Moses said, ‘The Lord your God will raise up for you from your own people a prophet like me.

 

To be sure,

Dt. 18:15-22 defined the meaning of “prophet” in terms of Moses as a “paradigm” or “image” of things to come in the OT.

This passage become the constitutional provision within the nation of Israel for the succession of Old Testament prophets

 

And yet, according to our passage in Acts, we see then how Moses (and even the Mosaic covenant)  was ordained by God to point beyond even the OT prophets to their messianic conclusion…

 

Heb. 3:5

Now Moses was faithful in all God’s house as a servant, to testify to the things that would be spoken later.

 

Moses and all future OT prophets were ultimate succeeded by yet an even greater prophet who Peter declares as Jesus the Christ!

 

2. Prophetic Warning:  How one relates to this prophet likened unto Moses would impact how one relate even to God himself! 

You must listen to whatever he tells you.  23 And it will be that everyone who does not listen to that prophet will be utterly rooted out of the people.’

Where to be utterly rooted out of “the” people is another way of saying “excommunicated” such then to be  outside of God’s covenant community and therefore outside of God’s covenant provisions and blessings!  (Biblically, to be a member  of Gods’ covenant community was another way of saying you are have a saving relationship with God.)

 

Therefore, the clear point being made by Peter is this: 

How one relates to Jesus determines a persons relationship to God Himself, even as this was foreshadowed under the OC  in terms of how one related to Moses.

 

What I would like to do then today is to try and unpack the interpretative assumptions that Peter had from redemptive history that would allow him to make this claim.  In so doing, it will guide us over the course of the next year or so as we seek to understand Exodus as nothing less than “the Gospel according to Moses.” 

 

 

I.  First (and for most of our time today)—we need to look at this issue of a succession principle as related to Moses. 

 

Peter’s claim is no mere convenience as to the fabrication of human ingenuity and creativity…. Rather, it is a conclusion born out of a succession principle that is inherent to the story of redemption itself…

 

IF we are to fully understand the theology that Peter is here ascribing, we must read the Bible as a single story—as especially related to the role of prophet beginning even at creation..

 

There is throughout the OT as clearly articulated in our Dt. passage as then picked up again in our Acts passage a purposeful logic whereby the former is related to the latter in paradigmatic way.  In other words, earlier themes, personalities and history is divinely directed such as to set up later themes, personalities, and history in order to impart to them  a deeper meaning.

 

E.g. In so far as Moses brokered (mediated) between God and God’s covenant people a deliverance from Egypt and the corresponding national privileges as related to a promise land—Moses was the “greatest of all prophets.  All future OT prophets were bound to execute what God has previously mediated through Moses… all other prophets, were subordinate prophets in comparison  to Moses !

1) Moses is the greatest of prophets after whose image all other prophets are established…

2) Corresponding warning concerning how one relates to Moses…

 

But notice!  What made Moses unique was the unique way that God spoke and revealed himself to Moses as compared to other prophets!  Upon further study, what one discovers then is that Moses not only points forward to yet another prophet who would in a fuller sense speak to God “face to face” but also Moses as a figure point back to the prophetic image of the humanity itself made in the image of God as corresponding to Adam in the garden of Eden! 

 

This dual “typological” reference as pointing both backwards and forward is a principle of interpretation that will be carried throughout our reading of Exodus. 

 

As then related to Moses himself as a prophet…

 

The Prophetic Image in Creation:

Beginning then in Genesis… humanity you will remember was created in the image of God by the power of God’s divine word… humanity as such was invested with the divine spirit such as to become “the image of God.”  As such, humanity enjoyed a continuing and intimate access to God even as God’s creative power was mediated through humanity in their vocational callings on earth!  Humanity itself, made in the image of God, was invested with a prophetic image—namely, an image of one intimately acquainted with God such as to reveal him and his glory to all creation.. thus, the ultimate purpose of humanity was intrinsically prophetic, as to glorify (reveal) God in his office of imago dei.

 

But of course, humanity fell, and with this fall, the divine image in humanity was cursed  such that according to Paul in Romans 1:

2 Claiming to be wise, they became fools;  23 and they exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling a mortal human being or birds or four-footed animals or reptiles.

 

The fall therefore anticipated a new creation as applied both to the creation itself (as it is said to grown for its renewal according to Romans 8), but especially a new creation such as to restore the image of God in humanity.  This was anticipated as early as God’s promise to Adam in Genesis 3 concerning one born of the seed of a woman who would overcome and deliver humanity from the bondage of sin and the devil… Thus, the “image” of a Prophet like unto God and a salvation as related to being set free from the bondage of sin and death was initiated by God…

Moses was prototypical—i.e. was a foreshadow of things to come, an earlier image of a later and more full image to follow. 

And this “image” was related to his prophetic type as endowed with the image of God.

Therefore, when Moses emerged as God’s prophetic servant, he emerged as one after the image of God, even as his face shown with God’s glory… he was in effect endowed, albeit still imperfectly,  with all those feature that Adam had enjoyed prior to the fall… an endowment then that would, in lessor degrees perhaps, be associated with the prophetic office…

Exodus as the “Gospel according to Moses”

 

The word “exodus” (usually trans. departure) appears in the transfiguration episode at the very beginning of Luke’s gospel (Luke 9:31)  this “exodus” discussed by Moses and Elijah..

E.g. the name Exodus, as even the title of the book describing God’s salvation of Israel and the initiation of the NC is applied then to the transfiguration account as a virtual heading for the entire passion narrative section that is introduced by this episode…

 

the relationship between the Mosaic exodus then and the exodus of Jesus can be viewed at several different places in the NT narrative.

a) Israelites departure from Egypt and the narrative of Jesus himself—starting with his departure from Jerusaelm (Mt.27:32,Rev. 11:8)… so as to be leave a city soon to be cursed on his way to Calvary—there to suffer outside the gate in the wilderness (Heb.13:12)

b) Departing Israelites through the waters of the sea and Jesus undergoing  of the baptismal ordeal as related to his identification with passing through judgment (MJt.20:22, 1Cor.10:2)

c) the triumphal emergence of the Israelites from the sea leaving behind their enemy, Pharoh and Eqypt buried in the sea with Jesus resurrection from the grace in victory over death and the devil (Heb. 13:20, Isa. 63:11-12)

 

Thus, the whole “death-resurrection complex” as related to Christ redemptive work

 

Thematically of course, we see the exodus experience as corresponding to ultimate human experience in bondage to sin.   And as in the context of the first exodus, the whole thing happens in the Passover context! 

 

The most obvious factor that links the first exodus to yet a second, is that Christ himself introduces his work on the cross by reference to the passover, even as the first passover meal is reinstuted in the Lord’s supper! 

E.g. the institution of the Lord’s supper, Jesus called the blood of his imminent sacrificial death the “blood of the new covenant” so interpreting it in terms of the covenant ratifying blood in the ritual described in Exodus 24:3-8 (Heb.9:20)

Heb. 9:18

Hence not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood.  19 For when every commandment had been told to all the people by Moses in accordance with the law, he took the blood of calves and goats, with water and scarlet wool and hyssop, and sprinkled both the scroll itself and all the people,  20 saying, “This is the blood of the covenant that God has ordained for you.”

 

Therefore, the even as the language of “redemption” is used in the book of Exodus to describe God’s saving activity on behalf of Israel (Exodus 6:6, 15:13), so then of course this points to the redemption that is by Christ. (Luke 1:68, 2:38)

Luke 1:68

“Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favorably on his people and redeemed them.

 

Luke 2:38

At that moment she came, and began to praise God and to speak about the child to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem.

 

Thus, right from the beginning of his gospel, Luke puts the career of Jesus in the exodus setting.  He indicates that Zacharias, the father of John the forerunner, echoing the word of God at the call of Moses (Exod.3:6-8) prophetically interpreted the salvation to be accomplished by Jesus as a second deliverance from the land of oppression in fulfillment of God’s covenant with the patriarchs (Lk. 1:68-74)

These are two passages that we should never forget throughout our exposition of Exodus… They guide us into a fuller redemptive historical meaning with regard to the history, teachings, laws and institutions that we will be introduced to. 

And reading them together, we will come to this conclusion about Exodus… and to say it clearly,

,  the ultimate point of this sermon and in fact every sermon for the next year or so in our reading of Exodus is this:

Exodus is nothing short of the “Gospel according to Moses” as then directing us to a salvation accomplished by Christ.   E.g. The Moses and the Exodus point to a New Moses and a New Exodus, similar in type as both relate to the good news of God’s deliverance for his people.

 

The great contribution of this book is the revelation that Yahweh is the sovereign God who provides deliverance from the slavery in which his people finds themselves. And yet, to read Exodus in context with the greater redemptive story of the Bible, one must read it in the light of the Gospel!   And in so doing, the great redemptive themes of Exodus as related to a temporal, geographical, political and even ethnic salvation historically evolve in the gospel such as to pertain to a spiritual and eternal salvation.

 

Themes like:

1) Slavery by  Egypt become slavery from slavery by sin

2) Passover by the blood of animals is accomplished by Christ’s blood.. resulting in Christian observance of Lord’s Supper in its stead.

3) Salvation through the waters of the Red Sea are spiritually related to the salvation through baptism into Christ.

2) Moses then becomes Christ

3) Promises land become heaven

4) Temporal Mosaic Law looks back to the spiritual and eternal law given to all humanity at creation and accomplished by Christ.

5) The temporal system of sacrifices instituted in the temple are

 

The major teaching of Exodus is primarily threefold.

1. The sovereignty of God.

2. The salvation of man.

3. The methods by which the sovereign God affects man's salvation.
