Exodus 2:1-22

…And his own Received him NOT!

 

 

Introduction:

So often in life we don’t know what is good for us… this is an understatement when it comes to our salvation! No less today as in the days of Moses!

To be sure, he came to his own and his own received him not…

 

Exposition:

Structure:

Three sections which function as three sequential parts of one single narrative.

Evidence for this:

1) Abruptness in each episode

2) thematic Continuity – theme of deliverance throughout.. Moses delivered, Moses attempted deliverance of his own, Moses deliverance of daughters at well.

3) and the special “consecutive “waw” in Hebrew

 

Therefore, 1-22—taken together, albeit in its three consecutive parts as follows:

Part one: Birth of Deliverer (within context of oppression)

Part two:  The Deliverer identifies with his own but is rejected by his own…

Part three: The Deliverer is received and at “home” with strangers…

 

“Binding the three parts together is the overarching theme of the preparation of the deliverer for the task of bringing God’s deliverance to god’s people, even in spite of God’s people per se. .. thus established that Gods’ deliverance is predicated less upon the people being delivered as upon God’s prior commitment to bless these people…  it is a sequence that moves from persecution to deliverance…”  Durham

“The purpose of the three narratives of chap 2 is to present us with an exceptional deliverer, exceptionally prepared, in the setting of persecution precipitated by Gold’s fulfillment of the first half of his promise and in anticipation of his fulfillment of the second half of that promise.”  Durham

E.g. From having already Multiplied Israel, not to give them their promised land…

 

 

I. Part One: (1-10) Birth of a Deliverer—born as “one of them” under oppression, God’s saving Moses from oppression then is a prelude to God delivering His people through Moses…

 

Vs. 1—Born into a priestly family-- Established the biblical ancestry of Moses within the tribe of Levi— this is pointedly mentioned as having come from BOTH parents who are according to Ex. 6:20 nephew and aunt…

E.g. this double authentication of Moses’ priestly descent is important as it clearly anticipates the statue of Moses as the spiritual/priestly role of his leadership and as Israel’s first great  and sacred hero! 

E.g. Moses is a Priest who also deliverers his people… One who will instituted sacrifices even as the one who will save the people from their enemies… this of course will direct us right to Christ who is also a great deliverer priest!

 

We know of course that Moses was born into a family with an older brother Aaron (3 yrs older according to Ex. 7:7), who would serve and assist with Moses in especially this priestly role.

 

Vs. 2-4:  the infant was of course adored by his mother, the Hebrew uses language to suggest that Moses was “treasured” by her… but of course the context is that the boy was born under oppression—his people being enslaved and his very life under great danger given the decree that all baby boys be killed…  such that for three months she tenderly and secretively hid Moses away…, eventually however, the little boy became too active  and too noisy to be hidden at home such that we are told that the mother of Moses cleverly decided to hide her son in the one place no Egyption would bother to look, in the river Nile itself, where Hebrew baby boys were taken to be drowned… 

 

(a hint of irony and shrewdness is of course in view here—the same waters that were meant for destruction became the means throughout which Moses was saved by God’s merciful providence)

 

Of particular interest is that on the surface at least, this whole story proceeds without any mention of God… But the writer gives us hint of God’s covenantal provision throughout the circumstances of motherly providence in that the papyrus- reed container  (box, chest, basket  doesn’t quite fit if you have in you mind the porous sort that we often associate with “basket)… that was carefully waterproofed with “hot tar and pitch” is called in the Hebrew an “ark’’ which is the same word used in Gen. 7-9 to describe the salvation vessel that God had Noah prepare to escape the judgment waters of God’s flood. .. it is the only other instance  that the word is used in the Bible. 

 

5-7: AS the story is proceeds, There is no evidence from the text that the mothers shrewdness went according to her plans… Most likely the plan went astray much to the immediate distress of the Mother when the daughter of the very king who had decreed to kill her son in effect found him at the Nile.  E.g. there is a certain suspense in the story wherein the baby was in harms way when brought to the Pharaoh’ daughter.  But of course we know what happened, an amazing turn of events…  

 

8-9: The unexpected delight of Pharaoh’s daughter becomes the means through which Moses is delivered from his curse… the medium of death thus become for Moses the medium of life… the very same one who was born into bondage and condemnation awaiting slavery is raised under the prosperity of the very one that would condemn him to death. 

 

Therefore, 2-10 presents the birth of the deliver/priest of God as one who is himself

1) born into condemnation such as to identify with the people under bondage

2) he was delivered himself from condemnation by the ironic circumstance of being saved by the judgment waters of the Nile and even by the very family that had condemned him to death

 

(One of course thinks of how Christ was born into our humiliation as a great deliverer priest, even as his deliverance was into Egypt)

 

II. Part 2 (11-15) Rejection of the Deliverer by His Own

 

In the next part of our narrative, The writer rather abruptly skips over about 40 years of history (according to Acts 7:23-24) without even a single comment concerning Moses life and upbringing to yet a second event that again focuses on delieverance—this time as by Moses who is shown to be identifying with his own people, such as to  jump headlong and impulsively to the deliverance of one of his own from the agony of his enslavement under a cruel slavemaster. 

 

Vs. 11:  The writer is clearly eager to communicate Moses’ identification with his people—1) repetition of the phrase “among his brethren”

Indeed, Mel S. notes:

The writer calls them his brothers, a word repeated three times in the first three verses. These were people with whom he shared the greatest affinity, for they were his own flesh and blood, but far more than that, they were the heirs of Abraham,

 

and 2) the use of the word “Hebrew”… e.g. evidently in so far as Moses had been raised by his mother he was raised in the Hebrew heritage which then explains his sympathy for their situation

he went out to his people and saw their forced labor

 

Vs. 12-13-  seeing then the oppression of one of his own, Moses seeks to deliver him even if compulsively… interestingly, the word that is used to describe the Egyptian master striking the slave (vs. 11) is the same word that is used to describe what Moses did to the slave master.  (vs. 13) The sense of the word is to describe a series of blows/strikes, not so much an attempted death-dealing blow… therefore, the text is not clear as to whether or not the intent of Moses was to kill the cruel slave master—but as it happened,

 

Vs. 13-14: Having saved the Hebrew from the cruel beating, one would think that the Hebrew people would have been very grateful, but the next event proves otherwise—for in seeing a Hebrew “striking” another Hebrew (same word again as in 11 and 12, Moses tries to intervene only to be rejected by the Hebrews for doing so. 

This is interesting—in that the Hebrews are shown here to be doing to each other what their oppressors were doing—the event propels Moses to become a reconciler among his own people…

 

Response: (From Mel S.)

a.                  “But the writer tells us that the “evil one” (yes, it describes him as one with a bad character, a bad heart) responded with impudence and scorn for Moses’s attempts at peace-making.

b.                  The man responded, not with the humility and respect that should have been given to the one sent to save God’s people — after all, who else but an incredible fool, or a great deliverer would have dared to bring down the wrath of the pharaoh upon him the way Moses had done? — yet his authority was flatly rejected. In fact, the man even turned his response into a taunt against Moses: He asks him the rhetorical question: “Who made you the man, the chief, the judge among us?”  You could say that these were the very three things that the male population were not among the Hebrews in Egypt, there were no chiefs to stand up for them, there were no judges to rule them, there were no men to act like men and protect them…

c.                  The man further taunted Moses, saying, as the Hebrew puts it, “Will you really kill me, and explain it as being just like what you did yesterday to the Egyptian?”

 

So Moses is left vulnerable—having killed an Egyptian oppressor and being rejected by the oppressed Hebrews who were his own people, Moses is now alone and cut off from both Egypt and Israel..  so that in

15—Moses fled from Pharaoh presence

Interestingly, the land to which Moses flees is Midian—a geographic location that is vague… the midianites are described in the OT as nomads and the variety of places in which they turn up affirms this description…

Theologically however, the point is clear- Moses is stranger in a foreign place! rejected by his own—he is both a fugitive of Egypt and despised by the people.

 

Therefore, Moses came to his own, a priest figure such as to identify with his people, only to find that the people he came to save did not want him... but instead rejected him...  He is now living in a place and with a people that would later become known as the enemies of God even. (Midianites)

 

 

III. Part 3: (16-22)Acceptance of the Deliver by strangers

Again, rather abruptly, and without much comment about how Moses ended up there or how long it has been sense he left Egypt, we find Moses one day at a well where and we are told about an encounter with seven daughters

 

Vs. 16--  what is most significant is that the seven daughters are of the household of the priest in Midian.. e.g. The writer is so interested for the reader to know this that he doesn’t even tell us his name at first, only that he is a priest. 

These were people who had been born to Abraham’s second wife, after the death of Sarah, to Keturah, and the Midianites later would become some of the fiercest oppressors of the Israelites themselves, so that God would have to raise up another deliverer, named Gideon, to defeat them. Yet at this early stage of the game, it is likely that the religion of Abraham had not yet dei out among them, and the priest of Midian and his family may well have been worshipers of the same God amidst a people who were perhaps not…

 

Vs. 17— the next scene is reminiscent of a prior scene—where again we see Moses acting out the role of deliverer.  But this time , the response is the exact opposite as before,

Note: AS from the  Hebrew language the oppression of the shepherd daughters by the other shepherds was recurring… i.e. without male “protectors” they were being driven away from the troughs with water so that the other shepherds could get to the water first (some interpret this to mean that the daughters had to drive their sheep away and first water the other shepherds sheep… e.g to draw water for both other households sheep and their own…regardless

 

Clearly, the point is that for the Medianite priest and their daughters were being exploited in their weakness without male shepherds…  they were much like Israel in that theirs was a position of weakness and oppression among a people who were their enemies…

 

The Bible describes his actions in strong and appropriate language — “Moses arose and he saved them.”[i]

“ came to their defense “  the verb “to save” from which the proper name (Joshua), Iesus in Greek (Jesus).

 

The great deliverer had arisen, but not for his own people, the great deliverer had saved those who were in oppression and bondage, but not his own people, for just like Jesus, the prophet greater than Moses, of whom Moses spoke,

 

Vs. 18-20—the seven daughters of the priest are depicted as being so excited by the behavior of Moses at the well that they forget their manners and rushed home to tell their father.  

The fathers response is to ask three questions in quick succession which result in his being shocked that the daughters had failed to bring Moses home with them. 

The welcome is all the stronger, as the girls’ father upbraids them for their lack of hospitality. Reuel did not care what the stranger had done at all, he simply was embarrassed that his daughters had forgotten the first law of hospitality. The contrast between the greeting they give him and the greeting he received in Egypt and from his own people could not be greater.

 

Again, the clear presentation of events is to contrast the rejection of the Hebrew people with the reception of the Midianite family, especially with respect to the deliverance brought about by Moses.

 

 

21-22: The narrator therefore leaps from Jethro’s invitation to Moses  to stay with him, Moses settlement , marriage and fatherhood… again, the message is in the brevity—clearly the focus is to show moses now at home albeit in a foreign land. 

 

A key to interpreting this brievity is the focus of Moses naming his child—the name Gershom is derived from the word which means “drive/cast out”.. e.g as Moses himself declared “for  “I have been an alien residing in a foreign land.”

E.g. “stranger” is connected with “there” as in Egypt and among even his own people… Moses was a stranger “there” i.e. I have been—Past” but not now!~

The point: Here is Moses, the priest of Israel, one among his people under oppression  and the soon to be deliverer of God’s people is now rejected by his own in a foreign land,

And yet, Moses is most at home!

 

Moses has come to a people who not only worship the God of the fathers but are free to do so and have received him and appreciated him even as he has in way brought deliverance to them from those who would persecute them.

 

So he dwelt in a strange land with covenant “outsiders” who gladly received him. (21-22)

 

Point: God’s deliverer—

Even as one who identified with his own people, his own received him not!

And yet those who were not his people, received him and his deliverance…

 

NT Application:

THERE ARE NOT ONE, BUT TWO PASSAGE THAT SPECIFICALLY INTERPRET THESE EVENTS FOR US IN THE NT…

 

 

1.  Acts 7:23-35—Those who belong to God receive the Deliverer,

 

Stephen’s sermon recounting Israel’s history which includes the story of Moses…

Apologetic Use:

-- Stephen attributes a purpose to Moses act which is not directly given in the OT account—e.g. we are told that he thought that his brothers would understand the true significance of his intervention as the instrument of God’s deliverance.. the lack of understanding  is then employed by Stephen apologetically as an example of a lager pattern of disobedience which extends throughout Israel’s history (vs. 35)  The pattern of rejection culminates in the rejection of Jesus the righteous one (vs. 52) which unleashes the implied curses of Dt. 18. 

 

E.g. the Exodus account stressed the incongruity of two Hebrews fighting each other which called forth Moses intervention.. in Acts 7:26, it is explicitly described as an act of an attempted reconciliation

 

Again, Moses in Midian is described in terms of Moses being exiled (vs. 29)… eg. The term is one that shows Moses as a sojourner…

 

The point—the fact that Moses was forced to become an exile serves to emphasize his rejection by his own people… and the moral dilemma of humanity in sin..

 

What is it about us that rejects what is good for us—how perverted we are – that we would not recognize a savior when we see him! 

And of course, they is exactly what we do when we reject Christ!

2. Heb. 11:23ff—Those who belong to God are called to follow after the patter of the Deliverer in then present age…

E.g,. in this case the use of the Moses story is less apologetic and more to encourage Christians who are suffering under persecution… E.g. The account of Moses is in the context of a whole series of OT figures that serve as “witnesses to the faith” in order to evoke a  similar response in the people of that day.  

Notice then especially 12:1—cloud of witnesses

Notice then especially 12:2—as ultimately Christ is the ultimate witness as the pioneer and perfector of the faith.” 

 

Therefore, the stress is on Moses and especially his choice of faith even when ill-treated.. .i.e. choosing rather to share ill-treatment with the people of God than to be unfaithful.

I.e. he did not pursue the fleeting pleasures of Egypt” but rather the ultimate joy in being faithful to God. … there is a certain heroic element in the way Hebrews presents the Moses story. 

 

Notice though, the writer of Hebrews understands Moses’ life as related to the ultimate redemptive historical purpose which is to direct us to Christ.  I.e. He says that the suffering endured by Moses was an “abuse suffered for Christ.” 

 

Therefore even as Moses is a type of Christ directing us to the deliverance of Christ as through his suffering, even as one who identified with those he came to save, but was rejected by his own as well… so too, Moses after the pattern of Christ’s suffering that every Christian ought to follow.  

 

Christ suffered the same rejection Moses did — and calls you His followers to the same path of potentially-painful obedience.
