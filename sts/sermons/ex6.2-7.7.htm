Play It Again

Exodus 6:2-7:7

D. Nathan Phinney

 

INTRO

Well if you’ve been here for any number of weeks as we’ve been continuing this sermon series through Exodus, this text I just read should sound pretty familiar—perhaps even very familiar.  You may even wonder whether you’ve read it all before.  And when we look through it in detail over the next few minutes, you’ll see that it really is pretty familiar.  This is all material we’ve encountered before.  Now if you’re like me, there is nothing wrong with that.  I’m the kind of guy who likes repetition.  If I have the choice between renting a new movie or one I know I like, I’ll rent the one I like. I shop at the same grocery store, hate when they reorganize the aisle.  Buy the same model running shoe year after year, to the point where I can’t even tell which shoes make a pair.  There’s nothing wrong with a little repetition.  And this text is repetitive.

The point is this, when you have repetition, when you know something well, you are in a unique position to appreciate the minor variations you encounter.  We will do the same thing with this text today.  Because of the detail of our discussions of the earlier material in Exodus, we are well equipped to see the subtleties of this “repetitive” one and so to understand what God would have us to learn.

 

SAMENESS

First let’s talk about the repetition.  “Play it again, Sam.”  Of course this is the famous line from the movie Cassablanca.  Bogart asks the piano player to repeat a tune he knows well and one that has meaning for him—one that calls to mind certain events of his life.  When we read this text we get the feeling that the author is doing the same thing for us.  The first thing we notice about this text is that it essentially repeats, albeit in abbreviated form material that we have already read, namely the material from chapters 3-4.  These chapters, if you remember recounted Moses’ call, his objections and his eventual obedience.  They told of his initial approach to the Israelites and then, with the elders, to Pharaoh.  Now, in this section (chs. 6-first part of 7), much of this story is reviewed, it seems.  What are the components of the repetition?  Well, most obviously, a surprisingly large number of motifs are reproduced AND are reproduced in the SAME ORDER as the earlier account (this has led some commentators to suggest that this material was originally a separate version of the earlier story).  In some ways it SOUNDS as if we are back by the burning bush all over again.  Let’s look at some of these similarities.  First, in both sections, God refers to himself in connection with the “fathers,” the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac and Jaccob.  In verse 3:6 (in the first story) God says to Moses, “I am the god of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob.”  In the passage we read for today, the same theme appears.  In 6:3 God says again to Moses,  “I appeared to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob as God almighty.”  The same language is used in both passages to identify God to Moses.  But in the second passage, doesn’t Moses already know who this God is?  Why the repetition?  Another motif repeated in both stories is the motif of God noticing the struggles of his people in Egypt.  In 3:7, God says, “I have seen the affliction of my people.  I have given heed to their cry.”  In 6:5a God says, “I have heard the groaning of my people.”  Again, we assume that Moses already knows this, so why does he need to hear it again?  Further, in both texts the motif of Moses’ inability to speak appears.  In 4:10, Moses after reciting his litany of excuses (3:11 (Who am I to go to Pharoah), 3:13 the people will say, Who is Yahweh? 4:1 What if the people don’t believe)—after this litany, he says finally, “I am not eloquent.”  I can’t speak.  This same theme appears in 6:12 and 6:30, which supposedly occur AFTER God has made arrangements for Aaron to speak in his stead.  In these passages, Moses tells God that he is “unskilled in speech.”  Again Aaron is assigned to speak for him.

 

SAME/DIFFERENT

What might the purpose of this repetition be?  Let’s think about the Cassablanca story.  In the movie, the request to play the song is intended to evoke memory.  And I believe that the same thing is intended here.  Why might it be important to evoke memory?  Well it is important because even though this story LOOKS like a doublet of the earlier story, we know it is not to be understood as merely another version of the initial call account.  Rather, it is SUBSEQUENT to it.  Remember what has happened between chapter 4 and chapter 6.  Chapter 5!  Chapter 5 told the troubling story of the failure of Moses and the harsh response of Pharaoh.  The people’s lives have now been made more difficult and Pharaoh is even more recalcitrant.  Further, the text itself makes clear that the Lord appeared to Moses in Egypt (6:28).  We are not simply repeating material we’ve already read, but rather material connected with the initial failure of the effort of Moses/Aaron/the elders and the harshly negative response of Pharaoh.  This difference of location makes all the difference.  Yes the stories are similar, but the SETTING of the repetition in Chapter 6 makes clear to us that Moses is not “doing it all again” as much as he is “starting over.”  In the narrative we go all the way back to the burning bush, not in terms of location, but in terms of message.  The children of Israel have been discouraged by the harsh response of Pharaoh to Moses first request.  God realizes this and responds by “starting over,” by playing the tune again.  God shows the willingness to “start over” with his people when they lose faith in him.  This willingness will be seen repeatedly as we move through the stories of the Exodus.

 

OK, so the first point to be made with this text is that it really is a sort of “Play it again, Sam.”  God, knowing his people’s (and Moses’ and our) need, condescends to say it all again.  Yet in this sameness, we already begin to see the importance of the differences.

 

But as we appreciate the sameness, we can also see differences more clearly.

Besides the setting, what is DIFFERENT about this narrative?  And then why are the differences significant?  The most important differences come in the way God’s message to Moses is discussed.  How is THIS divine communication and instruction different than the first received by Moses?  There are two themes picked up in this section that are new in comparison with what has come before.  First, unlike the account in exodus 3-4, the theme of Covenant, made between God and patriarchs is cited specifically as the primary reason that the Lord determines to lead the people out.  In Ex. 3-4, no mention is made of the ancient covenants of Gen 12, 15 and 17, but in 6:2ff it is mentioned twice.  What sort of character do these metions of Covenant have.  First in 6:4 Covenant is identified as a pre-condition for the whole interaction between Yahweh and Israel.  Prior to any event related to Egypt or the Exodus, Yahweh says (in 6:4), “I established my covenant with them to give them the land of Canaan in which they sojourned.”  The covenant predates and is the foundation for the current experience.  It even predates the people’s knowledge of Yahweh by his name (6:3).  “By my name I did not make myself known to (the patriarchs).”  They did not know me by my name, Yahweh says, but it was I who made a covenant with them, nonetheless.  And it is this covenant, a covenant made with the fathers, but through them to the current people in Israel that is the basis for Yahweh’s action.  Covenants are for keeps, he says.  And this is so important.  God reminds his people of his obligation to them.  Of course this is election, an obligation taken on “from all eternity, out of his own good pleasure,” and in his election of his people he demonstrates his faithfulness.  The covenant theme also shows up as a specific response to the “groaning and bondage” (6:5) of the people.  A similar reference occurs in 2:24 immediately prior to the call of Moses.  Thus, not only does covenant with the fathers form the basis for Yahweh’s present interaction with Israel, covenant is here linked with relief from bondage.  The verse (6:5) reads, “I have heard the groaning of my sons and have remembered my covenant.”  The implication is that the covenant people should not be in bondage or under slavery forever, and the fact that they are currently so, demands a response from the covenant maker. 

 

So the first new motif is the emphasis on covenant.  The second new motif appears twice in this section.  In 6:6-8 and 7:5.  In both of these places, God indicates that the purpose of his actions on Israel’s behalf is so that his name will be made known and that he will be recognized for who he is.  Look at 6:7.  God tells Moses to say to the Israelites, “I will take you for my people and I will be your God; and you shall know that I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians.”  This is a reason that does not appear in chapters 3-4.  It does however appear throughout the book of Ezekiel where god acts in judgment and restoration all for the sake of his name.  Here the idea is similar.  He has now told them his name, but they currently have no deed with which to associate the name.  This he says will change.  It will become clear that Yahweh is responsible for the deliverance.  But the Lord is not only interested in making sure that the Israelites know who he is.  In 7:5 he says, “The Egyptians shall know that I am Yahweh when I stretch out my hand on Egypt and bring out the sons of Israel from their midst.”  So both Israel and Egypt will come to know clearly just who this Yahweh is. 

 

These statements are particularly important given the concerns about Yahweh’s identity established in chs. 3-5.  If you remember, in 3:13, Moses is concerned that the Hebrews will not acknowledge Moses authority and will ask, “who sent you, what is his name.”  It is at this point that the divine name is revealed for the first time. It is important for Moses and Israel to know the name of the God who will deliver them.  Second, Pharaoh, upon being approached initially by Moses responds with the question (5:2) “Who is Yahweh that I should obey his voice and let Israel go.”  In this passage, God says clearly that the Egyptians will know that “I am Yahweh.” God asserts that the purpose of the fulfillment of his intention to deliver Israel is the demonstration of his sovereignty over all things and his making himself known to all.  It’s like an artist signing a painting.  She paints the thing and its beautiful, and, if she’s famous, maybe even recognizable as her handiwork.  But to make sure, she takes her brush and in dark colors puts her name in the corner.  There now can be no doubt about who painted this painting.  This is what God intends to do.  Israel’s exodus will be both his masterwork AND his signature.

 

So the two thematic differences in this passage are the emphasis on covenant (Covenant is for Keeps) and the emphasis on the glory and name of the Lord (The Painter signs his Painting).

 

The final unique feature of this passage is the genealogy.  Now it is often easy to skip over genealogies, because we don’t understand them, but this one you just can’t miss.  It is stuck smack-dab in the middle of a really good story.  Have you ever been driving down the road, and I’m thinking here of a suburban residential street?  You are cruising along at about 32 with the radio on and you come around a sweeping corner and boom, right in front of you is a flock of geese.  They are sauntering calmly across the road, completely unaware of anything in their path. 

 

That is just what this passage feels like.  You’re flying along and all of a sudden---Whaa?  Slam on the breaks.  The whole passage we read for today (6:2-7:1) is really one connected narrative split by a genealogy.  In 6:11, right before the genealogy, God tells Moses, “Go and tell Pharaoh to let the sons of Israel go out of his land.”  In 6:12 Moses protests about his inability to speak.  We expect a response from God, but the section quickly ends with this simple statement that “The Lord gave Moses and Aaron a charge to the sons of Israel and to Pharaoh king of Egypt to bring the sons of Israel out of the land of Egypt.”  There is no direct response to Moses protest—at least not here.  There immediately follows instead a genealogy beginning in 6:14 and continuing through 6:27.  Then in 6:28 the story picks up again and picks up nearly where it left off before the genealogy.  In 6:28 and 29 we get a little reminder of what had been going on and of the Lords command to Go to Pharaoh.  In 6:30 Moses protest is repeated.  Then in 7:1 and following God responds, “See I make you as God to Pharaoh and your brother Aaron shall be your prophet.  You shall speak all that I command you and your Brother Aaron shall speak to Pharaoh that he let the sons of Israel go out of his land.”  So the narrative of God’s communication with Moses seems to flow over the genealogy which breaks it in two.  Understanding the function and placement of this genealogy will make the meaning of our passage more clear.  What are these ducks doing in the road?

 

What can we discern about this genealogy? Do we know any of the characters in it?  Well, yes.  The lineage begins with Jaocb’s oldest son Reuben, then procedes to the next oldest, Simeon and then to Levi.  The order followed is the birth order given in Genesis 29.  After reading the first three verses, we expect next to see a single generation of Judah’s sons, for he is next in birth order.  Instead, however, the lineage pauses.  We get the first generation of Levi’s sons, then a second generation and then a third and then finally the generation that included Moses and Aaron.  Notice the familiar names in this generation:  Korah, Nadab, Abihu.  These names will show up later as sources of rebellion within the tribe—all from one lineage, which is interesting.  What is important for the present, however, is the inclusion of Moses and Aaron in this lineage.  By it the writer proves that these two are a part of Israel, and even brothers of the same father, something that would have been especially important for Moses.  The point the author is making is that these two are truly people of Israel, a part of the covenant.  They share in all the rights and priviliges of being an israelite.  They also share in the suffering, though each in different ways.  Note how the genealogy concludes (6:26-27).  “It was the same Aaron and Moses to whom the Lord said, “Bring out the sons of Israel from the land of Egypt according to their hosts.”  They were the ones who spoke to Pharaoh king of Egypt about bringout the sons of Israel from Egypt; it was the same Moses and Aaron.”  The Hebrew is emphatic here and wants to make the point that it is THESE guys, descended from the tribe of Levi, descendants of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob who are providing the deliverance.  By making this connection, the author identifies these two with the Israelites and makes clear that the Israelites are to identify with them.  It is not some outsider, disconnected from the plight of Israel that God has sent, no he has sent someone who can identify perfectly with all Israel suffered.  And this point is SO IMPORTANT that the writer breaks up his otherwise continuous story.  The point of the genealogy is to pause and make certain that the reader knows something very important about just who is functioning as the agent of God’s salvation for Israel.

 

 

So these are the four features we need to understand to understand this passage: Let me sum them up.  First, “Play it again, Sam.”  God repeats himself to Moses, Aaron, the Israelites, and later readers of the story to encourage them to persevere.  Second, “Covenants are for keeps.”  In his communication to Moses, God emphasizes the centrality of his covenant to his current plan and by evoking the covenant reminds Israel of his sovereign decision to bless and save them.  Third, “The painter will sign his painting.”  God in this passage indicates in a clear way that these deeds will be accomplished for his glory—and that all will recognize that Yahweh, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob has accomplished this great salvation.  Finally, “the mediator is one of us.”  The genealogy reminds us that the instrument of God’s deliverance can fully identify with his people and so effectively function for their salvation. 

 

Well, I hope that the significance of all this is obvious.  This is the gospel, is it not?  If we re-order these it is easy to see.  First covenants are for keeps, expressing God, from all eternity has sovereignly elected some to eternal life and made a covenant with them.  “For he chose us in him.  Second, the mediator is one of us.  In the fulness of time, Jesus Christ, the eternal God, became fully human to sympathize with our plight and to fully atone for our sins we do not have a high priest who can not identify.for he suffered in all manner being found in appearance as a man etc.  Third, “Play it again, Sam.”  God, in his grace repeats himself over and over and over again to sustain us in our weakness.  This happens in the sacraments he gave us in part to remind us of him—this do in remembrance of me.  In his word where we read texts like the NT one for today He who began… In texts like this one where we see him telling his servants, keep going.  And finally, “The artist will sign his work.”  In the midst of our doubt we can be cofident that as Isaiah says and the Glory of the Lord will be reveaild and all flesh shall see it together”   Or in Philippians, every knee will bow and every tongue will confess.  OR in our passage, “Then they shall know that I am Yahweh.  The painter will sign his work and the signature will be unmistakeable.

 

What you take away from this message, depends on what you need to hear.  If you are not a Christian this morning, I hope you see the gospel in this passage in Exodus—God’s election of a people to be his own and his providing a mediator through which to bring their salvation.  I urge you to hear and respond to God’s call to you.  If you are a Christian, I hope you are encouraged this morning by a God who cares enough about you to repeat himself, as many times as necessary.  And I hope you’re encouraged to know that one day, when his work is finished, he will sign it in such a way that there will be no more room for doubt.

The sharing of ourselves, the confession of our faith,  of the communion of the saintsa part of our service that symboizehem from1) the repeat feature of the text, 2) the subtle differences between this explanation of God’s intentions on behalf of Israel and the first explanation (chs. 3-4) and 3) the function of the genealogy.  If we can get a handle on these exegetical questions, we can understand better the significance of this passage for today.

 

Three things this text does:  First in it God repeats himself to his people living between promise and fulfillment.  Second, He reminds his people that the promise of their deliverance is based on a covenant he made with them, an obligation he has undertaken.  Third, he asserts that the fulfillment of the promise of deliverance will come in order to demonstrate his sovereignty and power and thus is not dependent on the people’s behavior.  Finally, he demonstrates that his instrument of salvation to his people is one of their own—one who identifies with them and with whom they can fully identify.

 

How important all of this is to us who are also living in between promise and fulfillment!

 

God repeats himself.  Don’t underestimate the significance of this.  Because this repetition governs the significance of the entire passage.  The message to Moses is repeated.  The message to the people is repeated, and in the act of reading the narrative, the message to us is repeated.  You see even the structure of the narrative functions as God’s holy word.  They needed to hear everything again.  As did Moses and Aaron.  C. S. Lewis talks about something he calls the “law of undulation” (Screwtape letters) by which he essentially means that as people who are influenced strongly by temporal things it is so often easy for us to lose sight of the eternal.  We continually vacillate, one day having strong hearts for God and another day doubting his very existence and care for us, depending on our circumstances, fears and “the state of our digestion,” as Lewis says so drolly, but truly.  Just as the Israelites needed to hear God’s promise again, just as Moses the leader needed hear it, so do we.  We need to be reminded again and again that God has not left us to wander on our own, but is even now carrying out his plan.

 

God reminds his people that his promise to save them is based on his covenant with them.  Wow, how applicable.  So often we are tempted to rely on our own ability to please God, in spite of the fact we know better, or to appeal to God’s pity if we’re in a bad situation.  The message of this text is that deliverance will come to those in Christ, not because things are bad now, and not because they (the people) are good now, but solely on the basis of God’s sovereign covenant.  He has in his mercy and grace predestined a covenant people and has entered into a covenant relationship with them.  And it is on the basis of this covenant that his promises are sure.

 

It is clear.  This is still the case today.  The purpose of God’s saving of his covenant people is so that they will know that he is the Lord and so that all others will know.  (Philippians 2:10) “at the name of Jesus every knee will bow, in heaven on earth and under the earth and every tongue will confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the father.”

 

Finally, in this text God shows his people—and here we need to think of the people, just like us, who first read this text—that the agent of their salvation was one of them.  HE was one of their own, though earlier rejected by them.  His heritage was theirs, his family line was traceable.  He was identified with them.  6:26 “It was this same Moses and Aaron to whom the Lord spoke.”  He was one of them.  Of course we understand Moses to be a type of Christ, who likewise is “one of us.”  As Paul says in Philippians, Christ, “although he existed in the form of God, did not regard equality with God a thing to be grased, but emtied himself, taking the form of a bond servant and being made in thelikeness of men.  He humbled himself and became obedient to the poiht ot death, even death on a cross” (Philippians 2:6-8).

 

Do you see the richness of this passage?  A God who reminds, a God who keeps covenants, a God who acts to show his sovereignty and a God who provides a mediator for our salvation.  Like the people of Israel, we are caught between promise and fulfillment, and like them, we confess a God who will and does remind us of his promises.  We have a God who has made a covenant with us and on that basis will be faithful to his promises.  We have a God who wants and intends to sign his name to the painting of this world—to show himself glorious in the salvation of his people.  And finally we have a God who has chosen to execute his salvation by providing a mediator who in every way is one of us—yet without sin.

 

Last week’s message for the in between is that God will keep his promise.  This week’s message is not just that God will keep his promise, but that he has provided for us a way to make it through until that day.  He reminds us.

 

One of the ways in which he reminds us is through the scriptures.  I’ve read a number of new testament texts throughout the sermon, and there are many more reminders God gives to us.  Just one is the text read in the NT reading, also from Philippians that he who began a good work in you will be faithful to complete it.

 

Another way God reminds us that he will fulfill his promises is through his sacraments.  These he has instituted as means of grace, but also as constant and visible reminders to us that he will keep his promises.
