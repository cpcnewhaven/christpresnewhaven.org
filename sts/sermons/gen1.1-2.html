A. Social/Religious Context-- Three realities both of which have an impact in Genesis.

1. The Ancient Mesopotamian Cosmologies... (Epyptian or Babylonian)

many of the parellels with Genesis are common in many Near Easter cosmologies-- i.e. watery origin of th world and the separation of land, a flood story, etc..

[ Babylonian

Enuma elish (not earlier than 1100 BC)-- such that it probably wasn't a "source" for the Genesis... Yet the oral tradition would have dated back to Genesis...

Atrahasis Epic-- also presents a primeval history as a sequence of creation-- divine displeasure resulting in a flood such that "creation is a prelude to the flood" (like Genesis)

Eyptian

Point:

"the known links of the Hebrew paatriachs with Mesopotamia and the widespread diffusion of Egyptian and Babylonian literary textes throught the late 15th century make it improbable that the writers of Genesis were completely ignorant of Babylonian and Epyptian mythology. Most likely they were conscious of a number of accounts of creation of current in the Near East of their day, and Gen. 1 is a deliberate statement of Hebrew view of Creation over against rival views. It is not merely a demythologiziation of oriental creation myths, whether Babylonian or Egyptian, rather it is a polemical repudiation of such myths... " (Wenham)

Examples:

1. Near Easter cosmologies-- dragons are rivals to the Canaanite gods whereas in Gen.1:1, there is no rival, only god as master architext creating His kingdom out of nothingness, a choas not due to a rival God but due to formless void of anything... When sea monsters are mentioned they are as under God's command in creation-- Gen.1:21.

2. Near Eastern cosmoligies decribe a struggle of the gods to separate the upper waters from th lower waters-- but in Gen. 1:6-10, the acts of separation by simple fiat command.

3. Near Easter worship of the Sun, moom and stars reacted to by Genesis in that Genesis pointedly avoids using the normal Hebrew words for sun and mood, lest they be taken as divine and says instead God created the greater and lesser lights, puting them in a cosmological hierachy that is under the animals even.

4. Babylonian tradition sees the creation of humanity as an afterthought so as to relieve the god's of work-- in Genesis, humanity is the apex of creation whereby God provided for man everything he needs for food and beauty.

Therefore, throughout genesis-- a conscious and deliberate anti-mythical polemic...

If Moses in composing Genesis was not dependent on the Near Eastern literature that exhibits parallels to Genesis, neither did he ignore it. But it would seem that where he deliberately develops the biblical account of an event so as to mirror features of the pagan version, it turns out to be for the polemical purpose of exposing and correction the world's vain wisdom by the light of revealed theology.

2. Ancient Near East Suzerainty Treaties--

Ancient Near Eastern suzerainty covenants where like "treaties" (contracts) between "lords" (suzerain) and "subjects" (vassels). Ancient treaties opened with two sections setting forth the claims of the great king upon the vassal's allegiance. and service--

Preamble: the first section identified him by his name and titles,who the suzerain is, and what he has done, which establishes his claims over the vassal (servant).

Historical Survey: the second by a historical survey of his previous dealings with the vassels.

Together, the Preamble and Historical Survey function as a "prologue" to the rest of the treaty/covenant.

In Moses' ministry: Genesis functions as preamble and historical survey to Sinai covenant, identifying the God of that covenant, and establishing his claims over all the earth. I.e. The whole of Genesis is a prologue to Sinai Covenant!

Preamble- 1:1-2:3: theological rich schamatic establishing the God of Israel as the "great king over all lesser kings"

Historical Survey: 2:4ff- end

The historical survey of God's previous dealings with his vassels leading up to Moses is presented under 10 "generations:

"the particular positioning of the several genealogical lists in Genesis was determined by the book's major thematic concern with the history of God's covenantal relationship to men. This covenant theme is traced in Genesis from the original relationship of God and man, the covenant broken, etc." (p.80, Genesis)

As the historical Survey-- Genesis introduces the great tarain of events that began with creation, leads on to the second creation of the flood, , the call of the patriarchs, the exodus from Egypt and climaxes with the law-giving at Sinai and the establishement of worship in the tabernacle... " (Westermann)
