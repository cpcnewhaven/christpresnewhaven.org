Genesis 12:10-20

Introduction:

Story about Abram going to Egypt- fine, but what does this have to do with my life-- I am struggling with the pains of divorce, or with the worries about my future, pressures at work or presures at home… so what could a story about a trip to Egypt have to do with me… w

Here again is one of those passages that has often been preached.. My guess: chances are pretty good that, if you've heard teaching on this passage, what you've gotten is a heavy dose of moralism. That what we have here is a collection of examples, whether good or bad.

1. Attempts are sometimes made to show how Abraham really wasn't so bad as it seems, that in fact he is acting out of faith such as to be imitated

2. Others Focus upon Abram's lie. Either be a sermon on how sometimes you have to lie to save your life, OR a sermon on how we shouldn't be like Abram by lying to get ourselves out of a jam.

Might there be something much more profound, something that might better fit what we would expect from Moses in Genesis?

-- for you will remember that Moses has over and over again recorded what by now has become a redemptive-historical pattern I.e. Promise, threat to promise, Divine intervention to protect promise-- all this in preserving a people who would be God's special grace community destined for redemption.

-- so again, last week we we told of God's promise through Abram and Abram response of faith to that promise leading him to leave his father's household to a land determined by God-- and his migration ultimately led him to Canaan.

What next? (What would you expect given the pattern already established in Genesis)

 

Exposition:

I. vs. 10- Desperate Situation (after the patter-- a threat to promise)

II. vs. 11-13-- Abraham's Solution to solve the problem

III. vs. 14-16-- Backfire with complications-- doesn't accomplish Abraham's problem but even adds to it…

IV. vs. 17-20--Divine intervention and Problem solved.

I. Desperate Situation

"famine"in the land of Canaan -- very common circumstance in Canaan used in 26:1, 41:54, 56; 43:1 etc.-- I.e. famine compelled all the patriarchs to leave Canaan at different times (26:1, 47:4, also Ruth 1:1) The lack of modern irregation made is susceptable and Egypt was a common refuge mostly because of the Nile river…

And so…

II. vs. 11-13-- Abram's Solution

1. . Abram decided to go to Egypt to avoid the famine.

2. There was one more problem though-- What to do about Sarah and especially the problem presented by her great beauty.

-- Abram would have been aware of the danger of immigrants being exploited that was common in that day and even recorded in the law (Exod. 22:20, 23:9) Strangers from a different land were often taken advantage of-- and especially regarding their women…

--Sarah must have been outstandingly attractive as confirmed not only by the opinion of her husband but by the way her beauty so quickly became renowned and was later noticed by Pharaoh himself. This will again become a problem in 20:1-9 with respect to Abimelech.

(Interestingly, Abram will again pass her off as his sister in an attempt to save both himself and the promise of God from extinction. Perhaps this suggests that what Abram did was not consider to be a sin by Abram, since the first episode would have seemed to correct him on this-- or perhaps a sign that Abram is simply off again)

--So then, even as Sarah was Abram's half sister, Abram decides that the safest thing to do would be to present Sarah has his sister;

-- his rationale-- Once they get a look at his wife, they'll figure that the only way to have a chance with this astoundingly beautiful woman is to bump off her husband. But, if Abram is viewed as Sarai's brother, they won't hurt him. In fact, they'll treat him with respect, seek to gain his favor, as they're in hot pursuit of Sarai. And then Abram will simply refuse to give her away in marriage.

--the plan is explained to Sarah about which the text records no coment by Sarah-- this indicating her submissive consent as confirmed in 1 Peter--how her faith was placed in God who would preserve her through her husbands leadership for the sake of God's promise.

 

And so…

vs. 14-- at first Abram's plan seems to works well-- as anticipated the Egyptians noted her beauty but didn't bring harm to Abram or Sarah.

Yet in vs. 15--an unforseen complication occurred

Discussion:

Again, all this raises the question about Abraham-- was he being noble or being cowardly, While his plan wasn't not a lie-- it was a half-truth and clearly a deception. Was he committing her to adultery? I.e. sacrificing his wife's honor for his own skin? In all fairness, there are differing opinions on this: Some seeing an act of faith, other a lack of faith, still others, good intentions though mis-applied!

a. Lack of faith--

-- There is no special revelation given to Abram… at least not that is recorded

-- "settle" a curious word since it seems to indicate that Abram is maybe already off track in his faith, leaving the land of promise in fear of starvation, trusting not in God's ability to provide for him… had he forgotten God's promise?

b. Act of faith--

-- assuming the promise and Abram's proper response to this promise in 1-9, we have no reason to believe that Abram is acting any differently now

-- so that to "leave" is itself an act of faith on the part of Abraham-- it demonstrates Abram's trust in God to preserve his plan for Canaan and bring him back in due time… that with God's promise fresh in Abram's mind, he wanted to protect himself and Sarah so as to protect the promised seed of redemption that was to come through their offspring.

A curious debate given that the passage offers no commentary whatsoever!

 

View 1-- Abraham was weak in faith-

I.e. When he choose to leave Canaan for Egypt, he failed to trust in God's provision within the promised land. His decision to present Sarah has his sister is again lacking the faith in God to preserve his promise as through Sarah his wife and himself so as to protect them both from the Egyptians. In the end, Abram was acting out of cowardice and was in fact lacking faith. For no matter how you spin it, , there is the problem of Abram's gaining great wealth at Sarah's expense such that Sarah is said to have been admitted into Pharoh's harem!

And yet…

a. negates that Abraham might have been concerned to preserve the promise of God through his own ingenuity.

b. In the light of ancient attitudes against adultery-- this seems unlikely (see 20:2-9)-- perhaps hopeful that he could fend of all those interested by refusing to give her away (as say in 24:55, Laban and Rebekah; 34:13-17, Dinah and her brothers) --

View 2-- An Act of Faith --

I.e. In choosing to leave Canaan, he chose to trust that God would bring him back in due time-- that Abram in the words of Calvin "retained in his mind possession of the land promised unto him, although being ejected from it by hunger, he fled elsewhere for the sake of obtaining food." Such that Calvin then applies this passage to our Christian morals in the following manner-- "let us be instructed by his example, that the servants of God must contend against many obstacles that they may finish the course of their vocation.. For we must recall that Abram is the father of them all so that all should form themselves to the imitation of his example." (you saw how last week, following the use of vs. 1-9 in Hebs. 11-- I followed Calvin in exhorting you to greater faith as then applied to your willingness to leave comforts if necessary in order to be a desiciple of Christ.

Abram's supposed "deception"-- it was not a lie but true and was in principle meant to entrust himself to God's promise to bless as through their seed-- his plan then being crafted so as to protect that promise-- hoping that his authority as her brother would prevent anyone from taking her and killing him.

I.e. was a plan devised assuming that all would respect Sarah's being under her brothers's authority

View 3-- Abraham was sincere but misguided in his faith--especially as concerning his plan regarding Sarah.

I.eChoice to present his wife as sister-- while motivated to preserve God's promise as through their seed, was misquided such as to soon backfire.

As again calvin will argue concern his decision to present Sarah as his sister-- "Hence it follows that Abram's end was right, but he erred in th way itself; for so it often happens to us. That even while we are tending towards god, yet by their thoughtlessness in catching at unlawful means, we swerve from his word…"

Observations:

1. Perhaps the confusion itself is revealing-- that good people over the course of church history can't seem to come to an agreement as to the morality of Abram's actions. -- and might this be indicative of the fact that the author never intended to say anthing about Abram's actions at all.

2. Unlike last week's text-- where the author explicit comments about the actions of Abram -- that he did as God had told him by faith-- this week not a word is said in commentary about Abram's actions.

3. And unlike last week's text-- where the NT explicitly applies the actions of Abram so as to teach against moralism (Gal. 3) and also exhort walking by faith in God's promises even against all odds (Hebs. 11), This week there is relative silence in the NT as to the actions of Abraham-- there is however one reference to this episode and its purpose is in fact moral-- in 1 Peter-- where women are encouraged to trust in God who will protect them as they submit to their husbands! But again, nothing is said about Abraham there!

I.e. last week-- I could simply read the NT's reference to our text and take the sermon in the direction that they did-- both to exhort against moralism and to exhort us to walk by faith and not by sight such as to be willing to imitate Abraham as it where as one of a great cloud of witnesses, etc.

This week, we are much more left with the literary pattern already established by Moses which is here repeated-- that of a promimse-- a threat of a promise-- and divine intervention so as to save the promise. And in fact, this is exactly what our passage will lead us to.

Perhaps then God never intended us to bring a moral question to this text concerning the decisions and actions of Abraham (the text is amazingly silent!) -- but rather if viewed as within the pattern already established by Moses in Genesis-- we see a promise (vs. 1-9) and a threat to the promise (10ff) and God's divine intervention again recorded that we might trust not in the resources and ingenuity of man-- but in the power of God for our salvation!

 

If we want to stand with the original intention of this text-- we would want our faith in God to be strengthened that we might persevere in that faith!

 

The best or worst of Abram's plans, whether for noble or ignoble reasons, were such as to fail without the power of God…

And so we return then to the story…

III. vs. 14-16-- Backfire with complications-- doesn't accomplish Abraham's problem but even adds to it…

-- her beauty was reported to Pharaoh and Sarah was "taken into Pharaoh's house"

-- Did Sarah obey Abraham so as to commit adultery?

a-- no doubt Abraham seemed willing for this to happen since he did receive gifts related to bethrothal… it was customary for large presents, "bride money" to be given to the bride's family at betrothal (24:52-53; Exod 22:15-16; 1 Sam.18:22-28)

b.-- it is true that the plagues against Pharaoh could suggest that Pharoah committed a great crime against Sarah

But

a. Was taken-- "laqach" is used in the context of marriage, this phrase denotes the formal taking of a woman as a wife and is distinguished from the act of marital consumation in some places. However it can be used more loosely to describe all aspects of marriage (25:1, 34:9, 16; Lev.21:7,13, Deut. 20:7)

b. It is possible to be a member of a person's harem without being introduced formally to the King "yet" for the purpose of mariatal consumation…

c. Notice the ground clause that would not longer make sense as concerning God's intervention-- 17 But the LORD afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sar'ai, Abram's wife.

d. 1 Peter seems to indicate however that the relationship remained pure such that God is shown to have vindicated and protected Sarah in submission to her husband's leadership and plan…

It therefore seems that Abram found himself in a pretty signficant compromise-- having received gifts of bethrothal, he was afraid to tell Pharaoh the truth-- the text doesn't say a thing about Abram's state of mind at this point-- perhaps he is praying for God's intervention.

IV. God's Intervention and Salvation--, vv.17-19

Results of the deception for Pharoah. God strikes him with plagues.

-- marks the turning point for the story-- the nature of the plagues not exactly described-- the hebrew word often refers to skin siseases (Lev.13-14; 2 Kgs.15:5 and perhaps even lebrousy as often associated with sin (Lev.14:12)-- see also "boils" as in Exod 9:9

-- How do we account for such results? *Does God approve of deception? *Does God function on the principle of relative righteousness? These are moot questions it seems. Rather God is here shown to keep his promise inspite of Abraham…

--12:3 I will bless those who bless you, and him who curses you I will curse

THIS FOR THE SAKE OF HIS PROMISE OF REDEMPTION THROUGH THE SEED OF ABRAHAM AND SARAH!

Conclusion: God will fulfil his purpose of redemption not only through his appointed servants, but here, regardless of them. The text is amazingly silent about Abraham and what to think about his decisions and actions-- the silence might cause us problems only if we are bringing an agenda to this text that the author never assumed.

 

Whether Abram was acting as the cowardly husband and faithless covenant representative, or acting by faith trusting in the power of God--

Sarai is endangered, the one through whom the covenant seed will come, God intervenes as protector of his own covenant.

The whole circumstance gave occasion for a manifestation of the Lord's faithfulness as covenant-Protector, cursing those who were inimical to the fulfilment of the covenant-programme and preserving the appointed ancestress of Israel to bear the heir of the promise such that all the miscalculations or acts of faith by men are as nothing compared to God's intention to accomplish His salvation for the sake of His name among all the nations…

There's no way that God will allow anothing to happen except what He has decreed-- that in this case Abram will be made into a great nation, and all the nations will be blessed through him.

 

Mainpoint: God is faithful to insure his redemptive promise through Abram and this not the result of any works by Abraham but by the mere God pleasure and activity of God -- that even in what seemed to be a hopeless situation, God delivers-- AGAIN!

A. Hasn't this been the point of Genesis over and over again so that our faith and hipe rests not in the power of man but in the power of God for salvation.

How even in the creation account-- we see God bringing order out of chaos, salvation out of sin…

As promised to Adam-- that in spite of their falling, God will deliver them to paradise as through the seed of Eve.

As accomplished through the birth of Seth even after the first seed of Eve was killed by Cain

As accomplished through the flood which saved the fledgling remnant family of 8 from the surrounding powers that where then allied against God's covenant line-- even the spiritual adversaries of Noah,

As again preserved as through the line of Shem to Terah Abraham's father

AND HERE TO ABRAHAM, EVEN INSPITE OF ABRAHAM'S MIS-APPLIED CUNNING.

B. And wont' this be the story throughout redemptive history?

1. One cannot help but remember here that Genesis is a prologue to Mosaic covenant-- while history is real, it is told in a way that ought to immediately establish a pattern all too familiar to any person living during Mose's day-

I.e.

Both experience a severe famine

Both journey into Egypt

Both involve great wealth bestowed on God's people

Both involve the theme of killing the males (in this case Abraham's fear of being killed)

Both involve threat to covnant promise as through the covenant geneology

Both involve plauges upon the Egyptians

Both involve departure from Egypt and journey into the Negev

2. Then how the first exodus as through the blood of a sacrificed animal upon the doors of God's people foreshadows the ultimate passover of God in the person of Christ who then rescues the elect from the bondage not of Egypt of of that tyrannt only foreshadowed by Egypt, that tyrannt of sin itself and our being freed so as to be no longer under the curse of the law and under the bondage of sin.

Application:

This then applies to our faith--

you should see here a picture of a pattern for salvation that is ultimately revealed in Christ. For like the protection of the promise in Abraham's day, so God protected the promise as revealed ultamtely and finally in Christ-- that even when everything political, social, even physical stacked up against the promised seed of Abraham, God interveened and raised him from the dead. IN Christ, we see the amen and yes to our story.

1. God preserved Christ from decay

2. God preserved all those who put their trust in Christ

More specifically:

We are perhaps all set back by the lust and violence that impacts our own society today, the proponderance of wars and terrorism…

-- scandals in the presidency, scandals in our own city hal where We are perhaps tempted to loose heart when it is discovered that perhaps worse even than the scandals themselves is the sense that our society doesn't even care-- that all moral fiber has been burned out of our souls by the acids of modernity, by infiltrating forces of humanism and atheism, neo-paganism in the form of licentious spiritualities and spirituality movement

-- the popularity and even religion of sport and fun at the expense of industry and good will toward men

-- what seems to be a kind of individualism that puts personal interest above loyalty to any given community of church, state or family…

And all this then leads us to despair, perhaps a temptation to take things into our own hands and cunningly establish plans that are politically savey perhaps but in the end less than noble and honest. Oh how we need this passage, as with the whole of Genesis, to remember that God is sitting on his throne, that God is now what He has always been, ruler of all things, King of Kings and Lord of Lords-- that we then can be so instructed that God will take whatever actions are necessary to preserve his promised redemption and with it a redemption of a special people that are with Christ as their head-- that church that will as Christ said withstand even the currants of hell against her.

Said simply-- you are on the winning side when you are named among the true people of God…

In this, I think Calvin's final exhortations are applicable to this text and to our lives, namely,

Instructed by such examples, we may also learn that however th world may hold us in contempt on account of the smallness of our number, and our weakness, we are yet so precious in the sight of God, that he will for out sake, declare himself an enemy to kings and even to the whole world. Let us know, that we are covered by his protection, in order that the lust and violence of those who are more powerful may not oppress us.

This passage should then encourage you as Moses would have had it encourage His flock, as the author of Hebrews would encourage his flock -- who…

What then would perseverance look like to the believer:

1. It would mean that we would not lose heart in doing good-- I.e. good as given to us in scripture.

2. I twould mean that we not forget that the church is God's organ for redemption in the present age-- that as we say last week and was proven this week, those who set themselves against the church are cursed, those who set themselve as with the church are blessed-- you can then do what it takes to present your gifts to the church where it is promised that Christ will be its head and that God will through it bring salvation to all the nations.

So today-- as we come then to this table-- we exercise our faith not in faith itself, not in our own works of rightousness perhaps done this week-- we don't come saying-- oh God will you accept this or that which was done my me this week-- we don't come saying, "couldn't you accept even my attempt at doing good"-- rather you come saying and believing-- oh God, thank you that my identity is not in my own rightousness but in Christ's rightousness, that I am in Christ by faith and your grace in giving me faith is sufficient to me.
