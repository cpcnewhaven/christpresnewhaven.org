Exposition: (Context)

Notice this with me...notice the context of Hebrews chapter 11. Chapter 11 comes on the tail end of the fourth warning in the Epistle of Hebrews, 10:26-31. It is a warning against turning back. In other words, throughout this book, the writer of Hebrews is concerned to about this group of Christians turning back from Christ. He tells them in 10:19-25 that "we have confidence to enter the holy place by the blood of Jesus"(10:19) and because of that "let us draw near"(10:22) and "let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering"(10:23). Then he gives the fourth warning in verses 26-31: Don’t draw back, don’t turn away from Christ. He uses covenantal language, and says that for those who turn back, those people will receive covenant curses.

You see, the writer here, or preacher is not preaching in a vaccum; he’s addressing a real and urgent pastoral problem...one that seems incredibly relevant to our contemporary situation. His congregation is exhausted and his congregation is tempted.

They are exhausted. They are tired---tired of serving the Lord, tired of serving the world, tired of serving each other, tired of worship, tired of Christian education, tired of being peculiar, tired of being talked about behind their back...tired of the struggle known as the Christian life.

They’re also tempted. They are tempted to turn away from Christ. They are tempted to throw in the towel. They are tempted to trade in Jesus for a safer, more a socially acceptable "religion." They are tempted to trade in this Jesus so that they will no longer be marginalized, no longer disenfranchised from the religious establishment of the day, no longer ostracized for this "King Jesus," who frankly, doesn’t seem, doesn’t appear to be reigning at all...at least if He is, He’s sure not helping them out very much. The very real possibility exists that due to persecution and the lack of "pay off" for following Christ, this congregation is tempted to syncretize Christian practice together with the practices of Judaism such that this new syncretized Christian-Judaism will no longer have the same "edge" to it. And the writer of Hebrews is warning them...that type of syncretism results in no Christianity at all.

Notice, immediately following this warning, in verse 10:32, the writer gives his audience a command:

32 Remember(!) those earlier days after you had received the light, when you stood your ground in a great contest in the face of suffering. 33 Sometimes you were publicly exposed to insult and persecution; at other times you stood side by side with those who were so treated. 34 You sympathized with those in prison and joyfully accepted the confiscation of your property, because you knew that you yourselves had better and lasting possessions.

Did you notice the tone of the writer of Hebrews...it’s one of exhortation to remember the earlier days when his audience had been faithful to Christ despite suffering, despite insult, despite persecution. Notice too, that the writer uses an athletic metaphor, "contest"(v.32). We’ll see him use another athletic metaphor at the end of our passage in Hebrews 12:1, when he encourages them to "run with endurance the race marked out for them."

Also, notice in verse 34, the writer commends them for identifying with those who had been imprisoned for the faith. But not only that, these Christians had also suffered the seizure or confiscation of their property...think of the images of Nazi soldiers confiscating the wealth, precious family heirlooms, paintings, etc...of the persecuted Jews in WWII. And amazingly, according to the writer, these Christians had done so joyfully.

How could these Christians have endured the confiscation of their property joyfully? Notice the writer clearly explains the reason: "because you knew that you yourselves had better and lasting possessions."

Now the word "better" is used at least 13x in Hebrews and is a major theme of the book. We’ll also see the same comparative judgement "better" used 5x in Hebrews chapter 11. In fact, for us to understand what this book is all about we need to know that the writer of Hebrews has one main argument throughout the book: Jesus, the Son, is better!

Trace that out in previous chapters...

Ch 1 Jesus is better than the angels.

Ch 3 Jesus is better than Moses.

Ch 4 Jesus gives us a better sabbath rest than Joshua

Ch 5 Jesus is better than other high priests

Ch 6 Jesus is the fulfillment to The Promise to Abraham

Ch 7 Jesus is the guarantee of a "better covenant" (v.22) and is a better High Priest

Ch 8 Jesus is the mediator of a "better covenant" (v.6)

Ch 9 Jesus has made a better way into the presence of God, the Holy of holies---

"He entered the holy place once for all, having obtained eternal redemption."(v12)

In other words...these Christians are considering "turning back" or defecting from the Christian faith. More specifically, they are considering re-lapsing back into a form of Judaism that would deny the superiority of what Christ has accomplished for them on the Christ. And the writer of Hebrews is saying: Don’t do it, don’t turn back, Christ is better! If you defect now, you’re defecting for something that’s less than what Christ offers you. The point is, perservere in your faith in Christ.

That’s where the writer goes next...take a second look at verse 35 & 36:

35 So do not throw away your confidence; it will be richly rewarded.

10:35 "reward" is closely related to God’s "promise" 11:9, 13, 17, 33, 39.

36 You need to persevere so that when you have done the will of God, you will receive what he has promised. 37 For in just a very little while,

"He who is coming will come and will not delay.

38 But my righteous one will live by faith.

And if he shrinks back,

I will not be pleased with him."

39 But we are not of those who shrink back and are destroyed, but of those who believe (NAS of those who have faith) and are saved.

10:37-39 "righteous" key word in Hebrews (11:4, 7, 33; 12:11, 23)

"people" though grammatically singular, the meaning is "anyone who has a right relationship with me". While the writer of Hebrews is considered for individuals, he always addresses his readers as a group. It may be best to use plural expressions, "My righteous people"

v. 39 "we" is emphatic, "we are not the kind who turn back"

The writer of Hebrews uses a quote from Habakuk 2:4 which is used two other times in the NT, . Habakuk is actually quoting God as saying in v.38: "The righteous shall/will live by faith."

But then God warns, if that one who is living by faith "shrinks back" or "falls back", then God will not be pleased with him. But notice what the writer of Hebrews thinks about these Christians he is addressing...he doesn’t think they will "shrink back" or "turns their back on God". In fact, it’s just the reverse. Notice verse 39 begins with a contrast, "but" and also that he uses "we", identifying himself with these Christians when he says, "But we are not of those who shrink back and are destroyed, but we are of those who believe (or of those who have faith) and are saved."

Do you see what the writer of Hebrews is saying? He’s saying this:

"You need to perservere in your faith. God has said that the righteous live by faith. This is who we are! We’re not like those that turn back or turn away from Christ, we are "of those who have faith." The category of people to which we belong is "those of faith...the people of faith" and not just those who give lipservice to faith in Christ. We’re not just professors, we’re not just professing to have faith; we actually possess faith...this is the real deal, authentic faith, the kind that perserveres and results in salvation. We are of those with perservering faith."

You see this is the context for Hebrews chapter 11 and the discussion of "faith." The writer is specifically encouraging these Christians to perservere in their faith. From this preceding section 10:32-39, we know that his concern is for his Christian audience to endure...to continue on...not to quit...not to go AWOL...not to defect from the Christian faith. To have perservering faith. So, that’s his point in chapter 11 when he refers to these OT saints of old...it’s as if he’s saying, "Look, this is what it means to have faith, perservering faith, faith that doesn’t quit, despite the circumstances, despite the suffering you may endure."

So, let’s look again at our chapter, Hebrews chapter 11, with this in mind.

Exposition: Text

1Now faith is being sure (assurance)of what we hope for and certain of what we do not see. 2 This is what the ancients were commended for.

1 "To have faith is to be sure of the things we hope for, to be certain of the things we cannot see."

v. 2 "It was by their faith that people of ancient times won God’s approval."

"Because the people of old trusted God, they won His approval."

A suggested translation is: "Those who trust God are sure that he will give them what they hope for [promise]; they are certain that things they do not yet see are real."

The word for "ancients" here is the same word from which we get "elders", "men of old" but notice there are women in this hall of faith. Also, notice "commended" or "gained approval" "obtained the witness", or "received testimony" that’s going to be used repeatedly in this chapter.

Look back at Hebrews 4:2 "For we also have had the gospel preached to us, just as they did; but the message they heard was of no value to them, because those who heard did not combine it with faith" or "did not accept it with faith."

"Faith" may suggest a body of teaching rather than a relationship with God; this would be quite unsuitable for an entire chapter of examples of faith expressed in action. "To have faith" or "to trust God" is an event, not an object. In several places in this chapter, what is meant by "by faith" is trusted. There is no doubt that God is the one who is trusted in this chapter.

Notice, this verse is not necessarily a definition of faith. Speaks more of what faith does than is. The way the writer is going to use this chapter is to make reference to the stories of the saints of the OT, evoke the images of their faith as a demonstration of what true perservering faith looks like. And the writer’s point is to use these images of faith to motivate his Christian audience to perservere in the faith, not to give up and turn away from Christ in the midst of their suffering. So rather than understanding verse one as simply a definition, what the writer wants to emphasize is, this is what faith does. The emphasis of Hebrews 11 as a whole is on righteous actions carried out by faith

Why are these OT saints relevant? Well, let’s look together at the chapter and see how the author uses these stories from the Hall of Faith.

Verses 13-16 emphasize not death, but the promises of God. In other words the author’s main concern in the middle section was to show that the OT saints died while anticipating God’s promises by faith. This point is also seen in the introduction (1-3) and conclusion (39-40).

What aspect of faith are emphasized? Faith has a future orientation.

"without having received the promises"(13), "having seen them [God’s promises] and having welcomed them from a distance"(13), "seeking a country [homeland] of their own"(14), "reaching out for a better place"(16), "He has prepared a city for them"(16)---all these expressions indicate that their faith had a forward-looking eschatological outlook.

Vanhoye and Lane agree that 11:13-16 is the center of 11:8-22. Rhee thinks it is the center of 11:1-40.

3. The Paradox of the Promise of Faith...

  • Eschatological paradox...already, but not yet Hebrews 3:8-9
  • Faith has an "Already" (present) dimension, and a "Not yet" (future) dimension. For example, God has already crowned Jesus with honor and glory (2:9);
  • yet He is waiting until His enemies will be made a footstool for His feet (10:13).

4. The People of Faith...

  • Their identity, unity, continuity...not completed w/o us...our leg of relay to run.
  • There will be a people of God, life of faith will be lived...? Contingent on us?
  • "peculiar," persecuted, perservering

Do we appreciate the river of the people of faith of which we are now apart?

"...the living faith of the dead" Pelikan’s distinction between ‘tradition’ and ‘traditionalism’. Along with responsibility. It’s our turn now...relay race, passing the baton for us to run our leg of the race. Illus. "Saving Private Ryan"

What is there for us to be scared of anyway? There is no security outside of God’s will.

The Nature of Faith

Faith means "to trust."

This sense is prominent where OT influence is strong, as in Heb. 11, and cf. Mk. 5:36; Acts 3:16; 14:9. A connection with prayer emerges in Mk. 11:22ff.; Jms. 1:6. Paul describes Abraham’s faith as trust in God’s miracle-working power (Rom. 4:17ff.; cf. also 9:33; 10:11).

Faith means "to hope."

The relation between faith and hope is clear in Rom. 4:18 and Heb. 11:13. When hope is directed to what is invisible, it entails trust. Only faith, not sense, can perceive the heavenly reality and grasp the promised future (Heb. 11:1). When faith is specifically faith in Christ, hope is mentioned separately, but such hope contains an element of believing confidence (1 Th. 1:3; 1 Cor. 13:13; 1 Pet. 1:21).

Faith means "to obey."

Heb. 11 stresses that to believe is to obey, as in the OT. Paul in Rom. 1:8. 1 Th. 1:8 (cf. Rom. 15:18. 16:19) shows, too, that believing means obeying. He speaks about the obedience of faith in Rom. 1:5, and cf. 10:3; 2 Cor. 9:13.

Object of Faith

Faith has its ground in God’s past actions, and has its own relation to the past in the form of faithfulness. But it also relates to the future as an assurance that God will do what he has promised, and to the present as obedience to the commands in demonstration of covenant faithfulness.

Since believing is dynamic, it may be weak or strong (Rom. 12:3; 14:1), it may grow (2 Cor. 10:15), it may endure (Col. 1:23), and there may also be references to its fullness (Acts 6:5), practice (1 Th. 1:3), and unity (Eph. 4:13).

4:1 What then shall we say that Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh, has found? 2 For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about; but not before God. 3 For what does the Scripture say? "And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness." 4 Now to the one who works, his wage is not reckoned as a favor, but as what is due. 5 But to the one who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness, 6 just as David also speaks of the blessing upon the man to whom God reckons righteousness apart from works:

7 "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds have been forgiven,

And whose sins have been covered.

8 "Blessed is the man whose sin the Lord will not take into account." 9 Is this blessing then upon the circumcised, or upon the uncircumcised also? For we say, "Faith was reckoned to Abraham as righteousness." 10 How then was it reckoned? While he was circumcised, or uncircumcised? Not while circumcised, but while uncircumcised; 11 and he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had while uncircumcised, that he might be the father of all who believe without being circumcised, that righteousness might be reckoned to them, 12 and the father of circumcision to those who not only are of the circumcision, but who also follow in the steps of the faith of our father Abraham which he had while uncircumcised. 13 For the promise to Abraham or to his descendants that he would be heir of the world was not through the Law, but through the righteousness of faith. 14 For if those who are of the Law are heirs, faith is made void and the promise is nullified; 15 for the Law brings about wrath, but where there is no law, neither is there violation. 16 For this reason it is by faith, that it might be in accordance with grace, in order that the promise may be certain to all the descendants, not only to those who are of the Law, but also to those who are of the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all, 17 (as it is written, "A father of many nations have I made you" ) in the sight of Him whom he believed, even God, who gives life to the dead and calls into being that which does not exist. 18 In hope against hope he believed, in order that he might become a father of many nations, according to that which had been spoken, "So shall your descendants be." 19 And without becoming weak in faith he contemplated his own body, now as good as dead since he was about a hundred years old, and the deadness of Sarah’s womb; 20 yet, with respect to the promise of God, he did not waver in unbelief, but grew strong in faith, giving glory to God, 21 and being fully assured that what He had promised, He was able also to perform. 22 Therefore also it was reckoned to him as righteousness. 23 Now not for his sake only was it written, that it was reckoned to him, 24 but for our sake also, to whom it will be reckoned, as those who believe in Him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead, 25 He who was delivered up because of our transgressions, and was raised because of our justification.
