II. A Warning against taking a loan: vs. 22:26

A. vs. 26: A Command against "shaking hands in making pledges for a loan"

1. With the command language, this is clearly the action (in this case, the inaction) that is being exhorted by this saying.

2. It concerns "shaking hands in making a pledge for a loan"

Here: "pledge" refers to exhanging an oath for money-- i.e. a loan or "usury" as in

Job 17:3 "Now put down a pledge for me with Yourself. Who is he who will shake hands with me?

Prov. 6:1 My son, if you become surety for your friend, If you have shaken hands in pledge for a stranger,

Prov. 17:18 A man devoid of understanding shakes hands in a pledge, And becomes surety for his friend.

3. Proverb creates a mental picture with explicit language concerning the shaking of hands in pledge so as to describe an economic transaction-- much like today where deals were made with a hand shake so as to form an oath.

4. In general, the OT law frowned upon those who would make loans and those who would enter into loans-- since in general-- a loan was a form of bondage... while having the appearance of helping the poor, it enslaved them...

Ex. 22:25 "If you lend money to any of My people who are poor among you, you shall not be like a moneylender to him; you shall not charge him interest. (I.e. give not loan)

Lev. 25:37 "You shall not lend him your money for usury, nor lend him your food at a profit.

Neh. 5:7 After serious thought, I rebuked the nobles and rulers, and said to them, "Each of you is exacting usury from his brother." So I called a great assembly against them.

Neh. 5:10 "I also, with my brethren and my servants, am lending them money and grain. Please, let us stop this usury!

This is an important observation from the law, since this seems to indicate that debt was considered to be an abuse of power-- power that was controled and regulated by capital!

Notice then the rationale...

B. vs. 27: The rationale for the command- warning:

If you (have ) nothing to offer , why (should) he take away your bed from under you?

1. "bed" --The bed, as the place of rest, is used as an image (metynomy) for all that is home.

2. "nothing to offer"-- here lies the point-- that in effect a loan is an system of exchange-- an oath for capital-- or so we think!

I.e.,

Since we can't necessary control the future--

Since the an oath has been made to give back what was taken

It might very well be that you will end up give what you never originally intended to include in the exhange-- here your home!

There is a kind of fraud to give security for more than you are worth; promissing what you are unable to perform-- the creditor may fairly in this case proceed to extremities--- not as against thedebtor himself, but with his surety-- with something that the debter has that he would never have intended to give away but will be taken by the creditor.

And why-- the wise man asks-- should you rashly incur such beggary and ruin, so as to have the bed taken from under you?.. (Bridges)

Mainpoint: We should avoid the entanglements of the dept-trap-- owing anyone what we can't pay or would be unwilling to pay.

This Proverb teaches that:

1. That there is a kind of tyranny-- not at the abuse of the states sword, but at the capitalists money...

Regardless of what you think of Carl Marx's social philosophy and theology-- his observations as to the power of capital to tyrannize and enslave are here in this passage made explicit...

Money does represent power-- we must be very careful then how we manage it, and who we allow to manage our own..

And when we exhange an oath for money-- we have given power over our lives to the one we have entered an oath with!

Discussion: Two Questions

I. Is Debt a Sin? NO! This is not the point of this proverb nor does anything in the Bible indicate that debt is a sin!

Rather, there is a certain kind of debt that is FOOLISH AND PERHAPS EVEN SINFUL!

1. Debt is foolish when it doesn't make economic sense assumes as surety what you would be unwilling to offer.

I.e. Three categorical instances where debt is foolish:

a. I.e. We should not make an oath assuming what we can't necessarily control-- the future-- and assuming as surety what we would never be willing to give--

Say-- having to give up my house for the pleasure of having a sailboat for instance...

Or having to give up my child's education (if this is something you feel you should do) for a vacation... etc.

But your optimism says-- I don't intend to do that-- the point is that you are foolish to bet on the future since you can't control the future!

The point is that we should count the cost of having this or that thing by loan-- as if the future did NOT turn out the way we had hoped... would you be willing to in effect gamble something valuable to you for something less valuable-- here its getting loan by betting the future against your bed-- this the proverb says is foolish and commands that for the sake of wisdom-- we would never do!

This is why, as a general rule, people advise that you not take a loan for that which depreciates in value, lest you end up giving more than you got in the loan!

Wise families don't bet the well-being of their family on anything!

b. Second categorical reason for why debt could be foolish is when it doesn't make good financial sense!

When what we end up paying for something is no longer worth what you are getting.

Example: When you purchase a home for say $100,000 with a 30 year mortgage of 10%, you have purchased the home for 316,000 dollars-- is the home worth that to you...

Again, if the rate of inflation is such that your house keeps up with the amortorized cost of you house-- then perhaps you are fine-- but you should count that cost first

Example: When you purchase a car for 10,000 with a 4 year loan of 12.5%, you purchased the car for 12,758-- is the car worth that to you.

In the case of your care, you don't expect that it will appreciate-- so

a. either have yet another sourse you are willing to sacrifice for the use of your car

b. have a such a need for the car for your lively hood that it warrents the risk-

Perhaps a person out of college getting a moderate or inexpensive car on loan in order to get a job... (but in this case, you would want to get a car that could do the job- not that would necessarily sataify your asthetic tastes, etc)

c. Debt is foolish when it gets us into a "trap"--

Note-- it takes no effort to get in debt-- especially with credit cards-- but it can be next to impossible to get out of debt-- we are very easily entrapped in debt-- thus the tyranny of debt--

Even borrowing the intitial money for investments or starting a business can be almost effortless--- but consider the difficultly of getting out of debt...

After the glamor of this or that thing which you have purchased on debt as begun to dull-- you life could be in a economic spin out of control.

Example (listen carefully)-- A couple overspends their income $1,000 each year for 10 years on impulsive an credit card spending and vacationing-- after 10 years, they want to get out of debt and realize that they now owe $10,000 right? Wrong!-- By this time, at ONLY 10% interest rate, they are paying at leat $1,000 per year in interest.

So when they want to get out of debt--

1. They will need to curb their spending to stop getting in debt

2. They will need to pay back the accumulated debt

3. All the while continueing to pay the interest.

Therefore, after ten years, their real cost for getting out of debt will be $31,000. (See Ron Blue's Managing your money p.59) If you add the income tax that will be charged on that additional money that youwillneed to make for get out of debt-- it comes to much more even that this.

So then the point of the proverb-- what might seem to be an innocent and harmless "overspending" so as to get into debt-- is really giving power to others to rule your life and perhaps ruin your life!

(Not accidently, finances then becomes the #1 reason for divorce-- the stress of all this is just too great)

And these three reasons are why debt is foolish-- but even more--

2. Debt is morally reprehensible when it tyrannizes either yourself, your dependants or other people.

a. It is fraudulent to enter into a debt that you know you will not be able to pay back if called upon to do this. Again, you should NEVER bet on the future, but only with what you have currently, if you bet at all!

Perhaps the most dangerous kind of debt is "investment debt"-- since you might not even have a tangible asset for what you received a loan for...

b. It is morally reprehensible to risk a God-given responsibility on something that is not a God-given responsibility-- here again the point of our proverb

I.e. To take a loan that would in effect risk your families lively hood, etc.

This would be in effect to tyrannize your dependents by your abuse of capital...

c. It is morally reprehensible to give a loan if you knew that the person was risking something he/she could not justifiabliy risk..

The lender/capital controler can too easily make a debter the creature of his own will. This is a kind of "captitolistic" tyranny that ought to be avoided!

 

For all these reasons, we see the wisdom of our proverb-- that we ought to avoid the entanglements of the debt trap. Debt is debilitating and demoralizing.

Is debt a sin? NO

And there are some cases when debt is pehaps even the better part of wisdom-- but you should always count the cost-- consider what you are gaining for the price of the debt and if in fact you have what you need in order to pay back your creditors if for some unforseen reason the future doesn't smile upon you-- Debt ought to be considered as a last resort and one should endeavor to get out of debt as soon as possible.

As a rule then:

Whenever you borrow money for any reason, there must be a guaranteed way to pay it back-- and with something you would be willing to loose for what you are getting.

II. What is the real problem behind debt?

Debt is never the real problem, it is only symptomatic of the real problem-- greed, self-indulgence, impatience, fear, poor self-image, lack of self-worth, lack of self-discipline, and perhaps many other things...

Unwise and unethical debt ultimately represents a spiritual problem! We must face it.

1. Perhaps you grew up poor watching the rich world go by-- thinking that you would be somebody if only you could live like those people-- and you get in debt to prop up you self-image

2. Perhaps you grew up wealthy-- thinking that you needed a certain kind of life-style in order to have self-worth-- so you get in debt to find you self-worth

3. Perhaps you are caught in a cosmic search for happiness-- having not yet discerned that the happiness this world can bring with cloths, cars, houses, toys, vactions-- are always short lived and are things spoken of by Christ as corruptable and destined to tarnish---

Ultimately, debt reflects a spiritual problem-- even if you are a Christian-- it is still waiting of you to find your security and significance in Christ.

Consider then the one, the only one-- who can make you complete as you were made to be in the image of God.

Consider then the one who can give you great worth-- by his choosing you to be his son or daughter as a child of the living God.

Consider that you treasure ought not to be in this world where moth, dust an dfire can corrupt, but in the eternal place whre Christ is nwo seated.

Die then to the things of this world that you might live in Christ!
