I. First Part: The command

A. First Word-- (which can indicate focus or emphasis)-- "Dedicate-Consecrate" as the main verb.

Usage: Only four places, all but the current passage -- to "dedicate"

Consecrate for sacred use-- either sacrifice, house of God or home...

Deut. 20:5 "Then the officers shall speak to the people, saying: "What man is there who has built a new house and has not dedicated it? Let him go and return to his house, lest he die in the battle and another man dedicate it.

Therefore-- this saying is clearly about religous training-- about a parents duty to consecrate their children for sacred use!

(not that we want to distinguish this from everyday life-- rather like the home being consecrated in OT, everday life was to become sacred by the way it functioned...

Language clearly refers to "religious training..."

Two Prepositional phrases:

Children...

C. second prep-- answers the question how: "under the influence of "mouth"

II. Part two: Consequence

part two linked to first with a linking work which can be translated in association or "in consequence"

So, In consequence of consecrating your child to the ancient sacred paths through training.... i

when he is old he will not be disciplined for it.

A Key word -- not be disiplined (corrected)

Word can mean "chasten, correct, punish, etc.

Lev. 26:18 ¶ "And after all this, if you do not obey Me, then I will punish you seven times more for your sins.

Prov. 19:18 Chasten your son while there is hope, And do not set your heart on his destruction.

As in Prov. 19:18, the point is that childhood "punishment/chastening" is much less hurtful than adult chastening, and even more so, to be chastened now is much better than being chastened in judgment!

i.e. correct them early and they will not need correcting later (Implication, later correction is more harsh than early correction!)

Mainpoint: It is better to consecrate your child to sacred uses early through training and discipline than to have them be disciplined later in life.

3. Duty for parents to assume responsibility for religious instruction and discipline is recognized in our tradition:

wonderfully summarized in our Book of Church Order as follows:

28-1. The spiritual nurture, instruction and training of the children of the church are committed by God primarily to their parents. They are responsible to the church for the faithful discharge of their obligations. It is a principal duty of the church to promote true religion in the home. True discipleship involves learning the Word of God under the guidance of the Holy Spirit both at home and in the church. Without learning there is no growth and without growth there is no discipline and without discipline there is sin and iniquity (1 Timothy 4:7).

There should therefore be no doubt that religous instuction and training is a parental duty! As a Christian, you assume this responsibility the moment you became a parent!

Of course there are some that would argue against religious instruction-- by an large those who are not themselves religious... but all the same...

4. Advantages of Religious Instruction:-

a. Response to those who would say-- let children be raised without religious instruction for the sake of letting them make their own minds.

Response: Call it the "myth" of neutrality...

This assumes that

1. There is such a thing as not being raised "religous"-- the fact is that everytime you teach an "ought" to your child, you have made a moral judgment-- the question is who's-- yours or God's!

I.e. this objectioin gets back to the first and principle sin-- who should determine right from wrong-- God who made us or ourselves?

2. This assumes that our children are actually born neutral... of course, this is not what the Bible teaches-- we are born with dispositions of rebellion and sin (only let a parent ask..)

b. Developmental critique of religous instruction-- "they are not able to understand... "

I.e. the myth of that religious development is somehow different from all other kinds of development...

Response-- the moment you say "you ought" is the moment you have already judged your child able to receive religious instruction.

a. Sure, our religious instruction ought to correspond to your childs stage in development-- out point is that if you can teach your child to read, you can teach them to read the bible... IF you can teach your child to throw a ball, you can teach your child religious manners .. etc.

I.e. another way of responding to this "develpmental critique" is to reaffirm that our children are not merely bodies and brains, they are souls-- just as we need to train the body and brain for usefulness, so too we need to train the soul for sacredness!

The nature or our relationships with our children is therefore not fully seen by merely regarding the infant as a beautiful animal, organized, in miniature, after the kind of the parents. It is the mysterious propagation of a rational soul that fills the reflecting mind with awe. When the parent looks upon the infantile smile; he should see beneath that smile an immortal spark which has been kindled, but can never be quenched, and ought to be nutured for blessing rather than for curse.

I.e. When it comes down to it-- to deny our children religious instruction is to admit ourselves that we are not religious-- and we are simply using our kids to avoid our own guilt in the matter...
