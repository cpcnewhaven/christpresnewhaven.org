Revelation 1:10-18

The Glorified Christ

 

Introduction:

 

Exposition:

verse 10:

Last week, we studied the context for today's vision, a context that has both "tribulation" and "kingdom" themes, giving us a kingdom that is intensely ironic.

Thus, WE are warned against both triumphal-ism and pessimism with respect to our posture in this life. Instead, our disposition and expectation ought to be that of endurance. This presupposes that we should be realistic about the present trials while being optimistic about the presence and power of the kingdom of God.

Today, we will turn to a vision that would inspire our endurance!

Again, the key to understanding John's intention will be to match this dream to the OT context that John used to frame this dream.

 

I. The Liturgical Context

Verse 10-11

And it came to pass in the spirit on the Lord's day and I heard behind me a great voice as (while) a trumpet saying, "What you see write in a book and send it to the seven churches, to Ephesus, To Smyrna, To Pergamum, to Thyatrira, to Sardis, to Philadelphia and to Laodicea… And so I turned to see the voice that spoke to me and turning

 

What vs. 10-11 tell us is that this vision is meant to evoke a "worship experience" and inform us of what our own worship ought to accomplish. Why do I say this… well notice first of all,

Lord's Day--

The phrase "the Lord's Day" is different from that of "the day of the Lord".

Only one other time in the NT is the word "Kuriakos" (possessive 'Lord's') used, and that is in reference to the "Lord's Supper" on Lord's Day.

1Cor. 11:20

When you come together, it is not really to eat the Lord's supper. 21 For when the time comes to eat, each of you goes ahead with your own supper, and one goes hungry and another becomes drunk.

In John's day, the word "Kuriakos" was never used in the phrasing of the "day of the Lord" in the LXX or by the early fathers. It was only from the second century on that the word was clearly and consistently used to describe Sunday. Instead, John understood the OT idea of the Day of the Lord to be that of a restricted period of the final judgment. (Rev. 6:17, 16:14)

Voice--

The "great voice as a trumpet" alludes to the voice that Yahweh used to reveal himself to Moses on Mt. Sinai (Ex.19:16, 19-20) .

Ex. 19:16

¶ On the morning of the third day there was thunder and lightning, as well as a thick cloud on the mountain, and a blast of a trumpet so loud that all the people who were in the camp trembled.

Ex. 19:19

As the blast of the trumpet grew louder and louder, Moses would speak and God would answer him in thunder. 20 When the LORD descended upon Mount Sinai, to the top of the mountain, the LORD summoned Moses to the top of the mountain, and Moses went u

This would enhance John's identification with prophetic authority since Exodus 19 focuses on Moses as the prophetic representative who receives God's word and conveys it to the people (Exodus 19:19-21).

More significantly, the phrase "great voice as a trumpet" also invokes the context for covenant worship as being when God speaks, thus the context for covenant worship is set apart from all other contexts by God's speaking! It is the context for our worship today, on the Lord's day.

Lampstands and the vestments(clothes) of the vision--

These are clearly derived from the OT context of worship in the Temple. Upon entering the temple one would have seen these lampstands in front of the 'bread of The Presence' (or 'the holy place'), and in the midst of this holy place was the "holy of holies", which was where the presence of God inhabited.

Heb. 9:2

For a tent was constructed, the first one, in which were the lampstand, the table, and the bread of the Presence; this is called the Holy Place. 3 Behind the second curtain was a tent called the Holy of Holies. 4 In it stood the golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant overlaid on all sides with gold, in which there were a golden urn holding the manna, and Aaron's rod that budded, and the tablets of the covenant; 5 above it were the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat. Of these things we cannot speak now in detail.

 

Who are the worshippers? They are the church.

Or rather, it is the "humiliated church" in need of a glorious vision! This is expressed here as by the phrase:

seven churches--

This phrase symbolizes the church universal in both a geographical and a temporal sense. This is evidenced by

1. the use of the number seven itself

2. the fact that at the conclusion of each letter to a church, John extends its application to all the churches. Therefore, what is written in those letters is potentially relevant to the church of every time and place!

Notice that the context is a liturgical: the Lord's Day, the voice of a messenger from God. John wanted the reader to see a heavenly scene on the day of corporate worship, thus informing and transforming his earthly worship and worship context. In some ways, John depicted what all worship ought to accomplish, albeit perhaps not so dramatically as this special revelatory event.

II. The Ancient of Days

12-16) I saw seven golden lambstands and in the middle of the lampstands (one) like son of man clothed with a foot-length robe and with a golden wrap around (sash) around his chest. Now his head and hair were white as white wool, as snow, and his eyes as a flame of fire and his feet like refined bronze as refined in a furnace and his voice as the sound of much waters and having in his right hand seven stars and from his mouth a sharp two edged sword and his face like the sun shining in it's strength.

Two major components to the vision:

1. Seven golden lampstands--

We know of course that the seven lampstands represent the seven churches from John's own interpretation given in verse 20.

Rev. 1:20

As for the mystery of the seven stars that you saw in my right hand, and the seven golden lampstands: the seven stars are the angels of the seven churches, and the seven lampstands are the seven churches.

We also know that the seven churches, while particularized in John's day, are symbolic of the holy catholic church (seven being the number of completion and universality).

But what is their significance? Why draw the readers' attention to these lampstands?

Notice their significance in the OT in Exodus 25, Exodus 37, Numbers 8, and especially Zech.4:2-10.

Zech. 4:2

He said to me, "What do you see?" And I said, "I see a lampstand all of gold, with a bowl on the top of it; there are seven lamps on it, with seven lips on each of the lamps that are on the top of it. 3 And by it there are two olive trees, one on the right of the bowl and the other on its left." 4 I said to the angel who talked with me, "What are these, my lord?" 5 Then the angel who talked with me answered me, "Do you not know what these are?" I said, "No, my lord." 6 He said to me, "This is the word of the LORD to Zerubbabel: Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, says the LORD of hosts. 7 What are you, O great mountain? Before Zerubbabel you shall become a plain; and he shall bring out the top stone amid shouts of‘Grace, grace to it!’"

Zech. 4:8

¶ Moreover the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 9 "The hands of Zerubbabel have laid the foundation of this house; his hands shall also complete it. Then you will know that the LORD of hosts has sent me to you. 10 For whoever has despised the day of small things shall rejoice, and shall see the plummet in the hand of Zerubbabel.

¶ "These seven are the eyes of the LORD, which range through the whole earth."

The importance of this prophecy is how Zechariah interprets the vision to mean that the small things, here being the faithful Israel, will eventually triumph!

Let me briefly explain:

-- In Zech. 4, the lampstand is part of the temple furniture and thus by extension represented the faithful Israel who are required to live "not by earthly might nor power but by my Spirit".

-- Because the lamps was set upon it, the lampstand was in the presence of God.

---The lamps on the lampstand represent God's presence or "Spirit", and it is this presence that empowers the true Israel (the lampstands) to finish rebuilding the temple despite resistance (Zech 4:6-9)

WE BEGIN TO SEE THE POINT OF JOHN'S VISION.
Just as Zechariah encouraged the people of true Israel to endure against great odds with a vision that put Israel into the presence of God and all his saving grace, JOHN HAS PUT THE CHURCH INTO THE PRESENCE OF GOD in a vision so that THEY MIGHT BE ENCOURAGED TO PERSEVERE!

The true Israel is identified with the heavenly temple, and in Exodus 19:6, we are told that the mission of the true Israel is to be a royal priesthood of God! Also, the true Israel of Zechariah's day was told that even the gates of hell would not prevail against the building of God's Temple. Because we know that the church is the true Israel, John's vision was to say in effect what Christ had said about the church:

Matt. 5:14

¶ "You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid. 15 No one after lighting a lamp puts it under the bushel basket, but on the lampstand, and it gives light to all in the house.

Interestingly, Targums and various other Jewish commentary and traditions foresee that the successful building of the temple in the midst of the world's opposition will be achieved ultimately by the anointed one who will rule over all kingdoms. Therefore, in light of Rev. 1:5-6, Christ's death and resurrection have laid the foundation for the new temple, which he will build through the Spirit ( who is "the lamps on the lampstand")

Rev. 2:1

¶ "To the angel of the church in Ephesus write: These are the words of him who holds the seven stars in his right hand, who walks among the seven golden lampstands:

2. The One whose presence the true church is in!

You can almost feel John's loss of breath at the glimpse of the "one like the son of man"

in the middle one like son of man…

The phrase is missing a verb and an article, revealing a surprise and a kind of "loss of breath" experience!

Notice that the image of the "son of man" replaces the image of the "Holy of Holies" in the OT temple.

and lo, I am with you…

This phrase affirms that God was with his people as mediated through the Holy of Holies. But now, it is Jesus Christ who is depicted here, who mediates between the church and God through the NT means of grace in NT worship. And again, in order to understand the meaning of the details, we need to see the vision in the context of the OT!

Son of man--

Clearly it is a phrase that has precedence in Daniel 7:13 's "son of man". The depiction of this "son of man" is similar to that here. The "son of man" vision in Daniel would have encouraged the people of Daniel's time that God's reign was certain (His "reign" was made clear in vs. 7:13-14).

14 To him was given dominion and glory and kingship, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that shall not pass away, and his kingship is one that shall never be destroyed.

- This "kingdom" inaugurated by the "son of man" is the same kingdom spoken of by Daniel in Chapter 2 ("the God of heaven will set up which shall never be destroyed"… and "shall break in pieces and consume all these of the kingdoms and it shall stand forever…")

-- The son of man is also said to be the "ancient of days" as depicted in Daniel 10:5-6. In fact, John's description of the son of man is almost identical to that in Daniel 10:5-6.

Dan. 10:5

I looked up and saw a man clothed in linen, with a belt of gold from Uphaz around his waist. 6 His body was like beryl, his face like lightning, his eyes like flaming torches, his arms and legs like the gleam of burnished bronze, and the sound of his words like the roar of a multitude.

Point: This description of the son of man is in contrast to that of the "Kingdom beasts" in Dan. 2 and 7, and in contrast to the Roman emperor "beast" that was seeking to destroy God's kingdom in John's day. That Jesus who was crucified is really none other than Daniel's "ancient of Days" (7:9) would have encouraged the church to see with the eyes of faith that the kingdoms of this world are really not reigning.

While the mainpoint is not in the details, there is at least something very significant about the description of the son of man; that is, Christ is portrayed as a glorified prophet, priest and king! (see Daniel 2,7 and 10 for comparison.)

  1. Jesus as priestly temple leader--

    2. One role of an OT priest was to tend to the lampstands. The priest would trim the lamps, remove the wick and old oil, and refill the lamps with fresh oil, things which in Jewish literature symbolized the workings of God: trimming and refreshing Israel so as to make here fruitful!

    2. Jesus as king--

    The portrayal of the son of man's head and hair is taken from that of the ancient of Days in Dan. 7:9, thus noting his divinity. His clothing resembles both a king's attire and that of a priest and an angel coming out of the heavenly temple. The ambiguity is deliberate-- to invoke apocalyptic visions of the heavenly worship in a temple made without hands.

  2. Jesus as prophet--

The portrayal of Christ's feet symbolizes His moral purity.

His voice is set in a heavenly temple context, thus further underscoring Christ's divine attributes in a prophetic sense as the "word" of God. The sharp two-edged sword from Jesus' mouth is based on the prophecies in Isaiah 11:4 and 49:2, adding further to His eschatological role as a prophetic judge and as the one who is beginning to fulfill the messianic expectation of striking the nations (Isaiah 11:4). Thus, the christians in Asia Minor were to understand that Jesus will do battle in this manner, and not to distrust in the power of God's presence as mediated through the word, sacraments, and prayer, especially since Christ is regulating these

Summary-- 'the "new temple" is one built without hands by the power of the spirit working through the church. This then sets the stage for the great vision of the one who is the temple's priest/king, the one who in the OT would have serviced the lamps on the lampstand.

III. The Intended Effect: "Do not fear"

17-18) And when I beheld him, I fell at his feet as dead and he placed his right hand upon me saying, "Do not fear" I am and the first and the last (ancient of days), and the one who lives and it came to pass that I was dead but behold, I am living forever and ever and I have the keys of death and Hades.

John's response to the image of Christ follows the fourfold pattern found in Daniel 8 and 10: the prophet observes a vision, falls on his face in fear, is strengthened by a heavenly being, and then receives further revelation (see Dan. 10:5-11 and 10:12-20). Notice the effect it had upon Daniel (10:vs. 7ff):

Dan. 10:7

I, Daniel, alone saw the vision; the people who were with me did not see the vision, though a great trembling fell upon them, and they fled and hid themselves. 8 So I was left alone to see this great vision. My strength left me, and my complexion grew deathly pale, and I retained no strength. 9 Then I heard the sound of his words; and when I heard the sound of his words, I fell into a trance, face to the ground. Dan. 10:10 But then a hand touched me and roused me to my hands and knees.

Fear not--

This phrase is used in moments when a person catches a glimpse of the greatness and superiority of God whose presence he was in, and responds fittingly. Notice John's Fitting Response: prostration in worship in great fear. Jesus extends His great power not in anger but in mercy when he said, "do not fear". On the surface , the word "Fear" seems to refer only to Christ's exhortation not to be afraid of His exalted appearance; however, Rev. 2:8-11 shows that the exhortation also extends to not being afraid of suffering. Instead, John and thus the church are to trust in Christ's victory over death and consequently supremacy over history. Note that 1 Peter exhorts christians not to "fear what they fear, do not be intimidated… rather fear Christ who then can say even do not fear since his great power is for us and not against us."

1Pet. 3:14

But even if you do suffer for doing what is right, you are blessed. Do not fear what they fear, and do not be intimidated, 15 but in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord. Always be ready to make your defense to anyone who demands from you an accounting for the hope that is in you; 16 yet do it with gentleness and reverence. Keep your conscience clear, so that, when you are maligned, those who abuse you for your good conduct in Christ may be put to shame.

 

Main Point: The visions of a Glorified Christ and of a Kingdom that is secured by the Ancient of Days Himself give the church confidence to remain faithful in her witness!

The Glorified Christ:

The effect of this vision is to make sure that we know that we live and move within the presence of God as especially revealed in Jesus Christ. He is everywhere watching and everywhere working to protect His interest; so when things look bleak, the glory of God is still present!

Such "glorified" visions of God are not new in the Bible:

Exodus 3:1-6-- like a burning fire

Ezekiel 1:25-28-- as of a man like glowing metal (bronze) looking life fire with a surrounding radiance like a rainbow

Daniel 7, 9 and 10-- like bright flaming fire and like shinning bronze

Each of these visions were given at times when everything seemed lost for the people of God! Again depicting the irony of the kingdom of God. It is in suffering when God's power is manifest!

These OT visions of the glorified God are clearly alluded to by those of Christ as the divine one:

Mt.17:1-8-- --Transfiguration

Acts 9:3-6, 17, 20) Paul's vision on road to Damascus

Jesus' self-proclamation after His resurrection:

Mt.28:18-20-- that he now had all authority in heaven and earth!

Apostolic Proclamations:

Peter-- Acts 2:29-36-- that Christ was the victorious king over death and was now both Lord and Christ with all authority.

Paul-- Col.2:8-10-- It was the fullness of deity and is the head over all rule and authority

Hebrews-- 1:1-3-- "right hand of God"

See also Eph.1:18-23, 2 Peter 1:15-18

More Proclamations about Christ:

1. the Eternal and Unchanging Creator -- John 6:4-41, Col.1:15-17, John 1:1-3

2. the Holiness of God-- Is.6:1-5, Heb.4:14-16, 7:23-28, John.6:68-69

3. the Revelation of God's Love --1 John 4:9-11, Rom.8:35-39

4. the Wisdom of God-- 1Cor.2:1-22, Eph.1:8-10

5. Sovereign-- Acts 2:29-36, Eph.1:11-23

7. the Righteous Judge-- John 5:21-29, Acts 17:30-31

8. Vindicated as the Lord-- Acts 4:23-35

John's point is to pack all of this into a picture, to jolt those who despaired with a glimpse of the glorified Christ so that they might persevere.

Illustration:

Prince Caspian, C. S. Lewis (book 4 of the Chronicles of Narnia)

Plot: The new Narnians (Prince Caspian's father and the evil Telemarines) are trying to defeat the old Narnians (Prince Caspian and the four previnces) in order to take over Narnia.

Mood: there is great anticipation of a great battle.

Scene: Trumpkin (dwarf) and the four previnces (Peter, Susan, Edmund, and Lucy--- representing the church) are walking through the forest trying to find the old Narnians, and they are about to give up because they are lost. The river they are following no longer seems familiar; it has "changed". They are feeling defeated and their spirits are low. All signs tell of their being lost. Things are looking really bad. They begin to bicker. Hopelessness, despair, and the temptation to cut short their journey begin to pervade the little band of pilgrims as they travel through the land of Narnia

Susan turns on Peter: "Oh, do let's go back and go the other way… I knew all along we would get lost in these woods…" (she wants to give up on the journey altogether).

Lucy turns on Susan: " Susan", snapped Lucy reproachfully, "don't nag at Peter like that, it's so rotten and he's doing all he can."

Edmund turns on Lucy: "And don't you snap at Sue like that either" said Edmund, " I think she's quite right…"

Ange r gets to all of them (P. 130). It is not a happy scene under the tribulation of their disillusionment and despair.

A glimpse that changes everything:

And about that time, "look, look, look" cried Lucy, "the Lion" said Lucy, "Aslan himself. Didn't you see?"

Her face had changed completely and her eyes shone.

"Do you really mean? " Asked Peter.

Right when they were tempted to give up and in the midst of their turning on one another-- a mere glimpse of the great lion-king of Narnia was enough to change everything. They began to recognize the river they were following, and eventually, with their hopes revived, they will join up with the other pilgrims, and of course Narnia is eventually won through the great Aslan's power!

This familiar story illustrates how this book would have been to the early christians who were filled with the despair of living in the world but not of the world, on a journey that often seemed to be going nowhere, in a world that seemed dominated by overwhelming forces set against the kingdom of God.

Christians are tempted to compromise, turn on one another for their misfortunes, even despair and want to turn back to the ways of the world, where there might be found a life of ease and better suited to the pressures that surround us. It is the despair that most tempts us to not only be in the world but also of the world. It is not because we are looking to deny Christ, but because we have lost hope that Christ is even around, that He is out of sight, out of mind.

And so when a believer is given this vision-- a vision very much like what Lucy saw up on the top of the mountain-- a vision of the glorified King-- one as a mighty Lion-- undefeated and powerful…, everything changes. He feels

A renewed commitment to remain on the straight and narrow path that so few in the world dare to travel…

A renewed trust that the battle, in the light of the glorified king, is as if already won. All he must do is press on and the rest will be taken care of.

A renewed loyalty to our fellow travelers-- with the hope of success and the renewed vision of glory-- we are able now to work together toward a common vision of the glorified Christ,

Too often today-- we get lost focusing upon our circumstances. Our vision of Christ is too small and people and circumstances get too big.

Edward Welch's little book: "When People are Big and God is small"

Shows how most of our worries and how most of what prevents us from being confident in the gospel even is not related to any deficiency in us or our gospel-- but in our fear what people might think of us… We begin to worry about our shoes, our homes, our cloths, our cars, our jobs, etc. etc. But of course, the problem is not really our "shoes", its what others think about our shoes…

 

 

Without a vision of the glorified Christ, we cave into peer pressure, we trust in money, politics and prestige…

I.e. Do we cower at the sight of non Christians-- as if we have the weak God and they have the strong gods.

Are you intimidated by those who are rich?

Are you intimidated by those who have academic accolades

Are you intimidated by those who are the political power brokers…

Are you intimidated by your coach, your teacher, your social peers-- as if their "gods" are superior to your God?

We are too often postured not only with humility but with embarrassment, we are wimps rather than gentle…

And then, we are so easily defeated, we begin to cave in, and veer off the straight and narrow path for a more comfortable life-- which usually means a life whereby our reputation is more secure in the eyes of the world!

Please don't misunderstand me here-- we are called to be ambitious-- but is it ambition for the applause of man, or ambition to do that which can serve the restoration of man in both its common and special grace aspects?

"Look, Look, Look"-- this is the message behind the vision itself-- look not at your circumstances, look not to each other, look to the Christ, the glorified Christ-- even one who like you suffered humiliation for a while-- is now the glorified Christ and the battle is all but won!

The vision of the apocalypse is not merely about the future, it is about now-- it is the cry to "look" that we might be transformed by the seeing!

Ours is this God-- the god whose image would make your peers to tremble-- why then would we be afraid to boast of our God to them-- to tell of his wonderful power, his mercies and his ultimate control over this world…

Has it gripped your minds that you are on the winning side-- that your life in Christ will be vindicated, that your fruit will pass through the scorching fire of Christ's scrutiny and theirs that is built on the wood and stubble of wordly pursuits will NOT

This ought to make us want to get on the proverbial great white horse and ride down the streets of New haven shouting boldly that the king reigning in spite of what they may think, that their pomp will be reduced to ruble and that Christ will be exalted…

Our transformation then is like this:

Our "look, look" of this age leads us to walk the walk of faith-- and if in the context of John's divine worship service, we came in tempted to give up, tempted to compromise so as to be more like the world for the sake of being more comfortable in this world--, if as we approached the "lord's day" we were doubting the Lord's sovereign coming and the ultimate victory of the kingdom of God over the great Narnia of this world… then to "look, look" at the glorified Christ" will transform us to great anticipation and hope. And our shouts of "look" are turned to the prayers of all true saints who while in the midst of suffering tribulation know of the present kingdom that is not yet fully here.. and we leave our great worship service saying… -- Come Lord Jesus! Come Lord's Jesus!
