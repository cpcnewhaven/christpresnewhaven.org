A Redemptive Historical Interpretation

Introduction:

Reverse (usually inductive method… in-out, today, deductive-- out-in-- i.e. like the intro to a good play-- give the interpretive details before you read)

What attracts us to a good movie,, or a good novel, or perhaps a play or poetry, or even a painting on a canvass? It is that they get us in touch with deeper feelings and questions that we are often too busy to think about otherwise. It's as if we instinctively know that there is something bigger going on than what we busily go about doing on the surface.

Rev is a book of mental "visions" painted then upon the canvass of our imaginations in order to both command our attention and so as to clarify for us what is behind and under the otherwise mundane events of everyday life.

Like good literature or a piece of excellent drama or even vivid images conjured up by good poetry that enable us to get in touch with things that we normally are too busy to feel or see -- so are the allegorical visions given to us in Rev.

And what Rev. gets us in touch with is a battle/conflict that is behind all battles-- the internal battles of soul, conscience, the external battles of wars and rumors of war, the conflicts played out in our offices and homes-- i.e. it’s the battle that we all know intuitively is going on in life everyday. It is the battle between good and evil. One that rages both within and without.

We can try to ignore it, this tension between good and evil, or we can pretend that it is really not there as if life is only the succession of materialistic chance-- but we would ignore it in vain… for who are we kidding, for what movie, novel, piece of poetry or even the paintings drawn on tapestries don't wrestle with the problem of good and evil in some form or fashion.

And so, we might be tempted to discount the wild images of Rev. as mere Religious mumbo jumbo-- but to do this would be to miss something very special and profound as is here depicted by these visions, visions and pictures that are based on ancient tradition dating back to the times of Moses even.

Here's the thing:

These pictures were never meant to be taken literally, but these picture were meant to be taken seriously. For they direct us to realities that are very real and that are at work in the world that you and I live in. And especially, they direct us to the meaning of life in a way unmatched by any other. They direct us to what life is all about and how it will end..

So allow yourself to picture what is depicted for us by these visions. But don't merely envision what is here depicted-- rather we should allow them to jolt our imaginations even as they put us in touch with a true but sometimes invisible narrative that is driving our everyday of our lives and history itself.

And for those thinking about the Easter this morning, our narrative is perfect-- for it directs us not to the fact of the resurrection of Christ (this is taken for granted here) but to the meaning of the resurrection as related to the conflict between good and evil.

See if you can discern the resurrection as I read…

Exposition:

I. Victory Songs…

Lest the details obscure the obvious-- the narrative concludes with a great song of victory beside a glassy sea!

15:2 And they sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb…

But what's all the singing about? Whatever it is about-- it has something to do with what happened to those standing by the Red Sea who are identified with Moses which is related then to what happened to those who will be standing by the glassy sea who are identified with "the lamb. " And all of this is put into the narrative about the beasts, and those who follow the beast vs. those who don't.

Moreover, we know that the victory song is sung by a relatively few in comparison to those who "could not learn the song" (14:3) and that the few who can sing it are those 4 who have not been defiled…(those who) follow the Lamb wherever he goes. They have been redeemed from humankind as first fruits for God and the Lamb, 5 and in their mouth no lie was found; they are blameless.

Again, sometimes we allow the details to obscure the message-- for whatever is the victory song as sung by the saints who stand beside the glassy sea in Rev. 15:2-4, it is to climax the events told about in Rev. 12-14…

i.e.

Last week, we learned how Rev. 12 begins a new series of visions which end at 15:4.

The section is divided by the repeated vision formulas like "and I saw" and "and behold"- into 7 sections or "vision/signs" are described:

Vision 1-- conflict of the dragon with the woman and her seed (12)

Dragon = Devil and his power of evil even as this evil was corporately expressed through Egypt and other evil expressions such as Babylon, etc.

Women = the church of the Old and New Testament, out from which comes the Messiah who conquers the dragon and brings victory to his people, the church.

The 1st vision is about the experience of the church living in the last days leading up to the final and great day of God's victory over Satan. The historical experience of the last days is patterned after both

a. the history of Israel and the Exodus event

b. the life of Christ as culminating in his resurrection and ascension.

That the experience of those living between the ascension of Christ andd his reture may be compared to both the experience of those who had been rescued from Egypt in the OT and to the experience of Christ himself in his ministry.

The point was clearly that by Christ's death and resurrection, the church, in so far as it is in union with Christ, is also victorious over its persecutors and the accusations and condemnations of Satan! Even as Israel, by God's miraculous intervention at the Red Sea, was made victorious over the bondage of Egypt.

the following 5 visions (13-14) put into slow motion replay the content of Chapt 12 such as to climax with the Victory Song of the Lamb in the final vision 15:2-4).

AND AS WAS TRUE IN CHAPTER 12, THE VICTORY SONG OF THE LAMB FOR THE CHURCH OF THE LAST DAYS IS RELATED TO THE EXODUS EVENT AND THE VICTORY SONG OF MOSES AFTER THEIR Salvation at the Red Sea-- AND SO,

I. Victory Song at the Red Sea-- Ex. 14:31-15:18

In Exodus 14:P31-15:18, we have the "song of Moses" as sung by those who were identified with Moses standing victoriously beside the Red Sea…

I.e. The Song of victory then was about the defeat of Egypt as by God's miraculous intervention at the Red Sea…

You will remember how the story included

1. Persecution of Egypt against Israel-- God's chosen nation.

2. Plagues against Egypt which climax with the plague of killing the first born.

3. The Passover meal provided for the Israelites- Blood of a lamb in substitution for the blood of the first born of Israel.

4. The Judgment of God as through the waters of the Red Sea

5. The Salvation and Vindication of Israel as through the judgment waters of God such as to climax with a song of victory-- a song that spoke of God's triumph on behalf of his people:

Notice especially the themes of that ancient song…

2. Song the Lamb after the Pattern of the Song of Moses

And they sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb

I.e. Again, we see that the complex set of events surrounding the Exodus are in view as an interpretive schematic for understanding the complex set of events surrounding the Cross…

I.e. Christ is a second Moses (or Moses was a type of Christ)

I.e. Resurrection is a second Exodus (or Exodus was a type of the Resurrection)

Both-- the "victory song" of Moses is the victory song of Christians.

standing beside the sea of glass

Jewish tradition depicted the Red Sea as becoming a "sea of glass)

(The exodus is also discerned in the mention of "plagues" in vs. 1 which are clearly modeled after the plagues of Egypt (notice exp. Chapt. 16)

So then, John is appropriating OT images of the Red Sea as depicted as "glass" whereby evil is judged-- after the pattern of the Exodus event, here as accomplished by Christ.

Thus the "victory song" of the Israelites on the other side of the Read Sea after being saved from the evil empire Egypt is spiritually synonymous with the victory song of the Christians on the other side of the resurrection after being saved from Satan's condemnations and accusations as through persecution.

They are victorious only because the lamb has conquered and granted them a share in the effects of this victory at the sea. They are to participate in the Lamb's victory because of their enduring faith in the midst of persecution and their resistance to compromising their faith with the world-- thus " and those who had emerged victorious from the beast and from its image and from the number of its name"
