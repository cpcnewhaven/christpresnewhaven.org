The Seven Bowls of Judgment

Revelation 15:1, 5 - 16:21



Introduction:

It is inevitable that we would come to this topic of "hell" in the book of revelation about the last days leading up to the last day.



And what comes to your mind when you think about hell.?



No doubt, the images in your mind have been conjured up by the great literary images given to us through history-- one thinks especially of Dante's "inferno" (hell)-- often confused with his purgatory…



It has been argued by Dorothy Sayers, contrary to St. Thomas Aquinas , that Dante's purgatory is about the hellishness of a time (whether after death or before death) prior to one's final judgment, so that such hellishness would move some to repentance while others to a greater unrepentance and a hardened heart…

I.e. The inferno is for those who are so hardened in their sin that they are merely given over to the desires of their hearts-- only to find that it is like fire being poured out over them!



This is very much the depiction of our text this morning-- it is about what happens to those who do not repent, who are not led to Christ by the sufferings of this life, and persecute those who do; they are destined to hell.



Exposition:

Remember the transparency effect-- I.e. trumpets and bowls are both related to the same judgments. Both symbolize judgments against the non-elect who are "hardened" in their hearts. Both present the plagues in the same order and parallel them literally, grammatically and temporally. This correspondence is as follows:

Trumpet/bowl 1-- Exodus 9:22ff (trumpet) and 8ff (bowl)



Trumpet/bowl 2-- Exodus 7:17ff



Trumpet/bowl 3-- Exodus 7:17ff



Trumpet/bowl 4-- Exodus 10:21ff (trumpet) and 9:22ff (bowl)





What is particularly emphasized here:

a. The last day is very much like the plagues-- God's judgment is upon those who "hardened their hearts" (notice that this is made explicit in our passage two times)

b. However, the final judgment is made more clear as to the fiery fury that will follow.

A. The command to pour out the bowls

A. vs. 5: the Tent of testimony



Acts 7:44

1. The Tabernacle of testimony-- is the heavenly equivalent of the tabernacle that was with Israel in the wilderness. Compare the exodus context of vs. 2-4 with that of Exodus 28:26, 40:34, etc. Term is used in the Greek OT at least 140 times, 130 of which is in Exodus through Deuteronomy.

a. "testimony" refers to the 10 commandments placed in the ark of the tabernacle. Therefore, the law of the Lord is the testimony, by which all people will be judged, either in Christ or without Christ…

b. The tabernacle represented God's presence with them and his will to reveal himself through Israel to the nations.

c. The Tabernacle also represented the mercy of God as did the mercy seat of the ark, that is, judgment by law but salvation by grace through the sacrificial atonement upon the mercy seat.



Therefore, the "testimony" in Rev. 15:5 is the law that became incarnate in and fulfilled by Christ; that is, it is the testimony of Jesus who summed up the OT commandments of God in himself (see Rev. 12:17.)

III. The first Five Bowls (Revelation 16:1-11)

A. General observation:



1. Vs. 6--the bowl punishments are called the seven plagues (note also Rev. 15:1, 6, 8, 21:9)

The same phrase is used in Leviticus 26:21 to describe the woes that God will send in response to the idolatry of Israel. There it is said four times that God will judge Israel "seven times" if His people are unfaithful. Moreover, the patterns of 4 and 7 are here in the judgment visions: four sets of seven trumpets, visions, bowls, etc.



Thus the plagues as depicted against Egypt are here depicted against idolatry (vs. any ethnic correspondence)



2. Vs. 7-- again, we see the reference to the temple altar. The connection with the altar shows that the bowl punishments are God's answer to the saints' prayers for vindication and forgiveness as was also true for the OT church at the temple altar. Again the altar directs us to the sacrificial system of the OT…



3. Vs. 8--again, notice that the vision focuses on God's presence; the temple is described as filled with the smoke from God's glory and from his power (see also Exodus 40:24-25, 2Chronicles 5:13, Isaiah 6:1). Note especially Ezekiel 10:2-4 where an angelic being clothed in linen stands close to the four cherubims and the heavenly temple, and the temple is filled with the brightness of the glory of the Lord. God's presence and expression of wrath is so awesome that not even heavenly beings can stand in his midst.

7. vs. 10-11-- God Punishes Hardened Idolaters through suffering caused by revealing to them their irremediable separation from him



Rev. 16:10

The word "darkness:" refers to the same in Exodus 10:23 where the darkness was so dense that the Egyptians were visually separated from one another (e.g. "no man saw his brother"). Here, the darkness represents man's separation from God such that his idolatry is in vain. Thus the punished idolaters now recognize God as the cause of their suffering but this doesn't work repentance in them. This interpretation is in keeping with that of the early Jewish tradition, which understood the Egyptians during the plagues to be so intractable in their unrepentant attitude that they were condemned for not being reformed by that correction (see Wis. 12). Though a remnant of Egypt did repent and did come out of Egypt with Israel, the vast majority refused to trust in Israel's god. The Egyptians' works included murder and thievery as well as sorcery, idol worship, fornication, etc. They did not repent.

IV. Sixth and Seventh Bowls-- The Final Judgment on the Evil World System (Rev. 16:12-21)

A. Sixth Bowl-- God gathers together ungodly forces in order to punish them decisively at the end of the age (Rev. 12-16)



Rev. 16:12

¶ The sixth angel poured his bowl on the great river Euphrates, and its water was dried up in order to prepare the way for the kings from the east. 13 And I saw three foul spirits like frogs coming from the mouth of the dragon, from the mouth of the beast, and from the mouth of the false prophet. 14 These are demonic spirits, performing signs, who go abroad to the kings of the whole world, to assemble them for battle on the great day of God the Almighty. 15 ("See, I am coming like a thief! Blessed is the one who stays awake and is clothed, not going about naked and exposed to shame.") 16 And they assembled them at the place that in Hebrew is called Armageddon.



The sixth bowl is depicted according to the description of God's judgment on Babylon and Israel's restoration. The drying up of the Euphrates river is reminiscent of the Red Sea judgment (Isaiah 11:15, 44:27). The Red Sea judgment was fulfilled when King Cyrus diverted the river in Isaiah 44:27-28, thus allowing his army to cross the river, enter Babylon unexpectedly and defeat it. Cyrus was the means by which God delivered the godly, and the "drying up water" is sometimes the means by which He punished the ungodly. Therefore, the kings that came from the east by way of the Euphrates in Rev. 16:12 evoke the OT prophecy of a northern army beyond the Euphrates whom god will bring to judge the sinful Israel.



Babylon has become a universal symbol of sin/evil (see Rev. 11:8) such that the river Euphrates in Revelation cannot be a literal geographical reference to the Euphrates in modern Iraq, Syria and Turkey but rather is figurative (despite those who contend that the reference is literal).



Thus image of Babylon as the harlot that sits on many waters builds upon the image of Euphrates and its waters that will then be dried up by God's judgment in order to plunder Babylon.



Note that all of the images so far conjure up ones from the OT past.

NOTE THEN THE CROSS AND OTHER IMAGES IN Rev..17:1 - 19:21, which is a long interpretive review of the 6th and 7th bowls, foretelling the final judgment of Babylon.



Main point: Hell awaits those who are unrepentant and hardened of heart



Propositions about hell and judgment:

1. Everyone everywhere awaits God's final judgment, when they will be examined for their thoughts, words, and deeds.



Westminster Confession Ch. 33 ¶1

God hath appointed a day, wherein he will judge the world, in righteousness, by Jesus Christ, to whom all power and judgment is given of the Father. In which day, not only the apostate angels shall be judged, but likewise all persons that have lived upon earth shall appear before the tribunal of Christ, to give an account of their thoughts, words, and deeds; and to receive according to what they have done in the body, whether good or evil.



The criteria for this judgment will be whether or not you have done good or evil, and No one is exempted from this judgment



2. Prior to the Lord's return, when we die



Westminster Confession Ch. 32 ¶1

The bodies of men, after death, return to dust, and see corruption: but their souls, which neither die nor sleep, having an immortal subsistence, immediately return to God who gave them: the souls of the righteous, being then made perfect in holiness, are received into the highest heavens, where they behold the face of God, in light and glory, waiting for the full redemption of their bodies. And the souls of the wicked are cast into hell, where they remain in torments and utter darkness, reserved to the judgment of the great day. Beside these two places, for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none.



There are no purgatory and "soul sleep" souls who neither die nor sleep.

(note: Dante's purgatory was interpreted by St. Thomas to be the modern notion of a place bordering Hell and rather like it except that Christians remained there only for a while. This was probably not Dante's vision.) Dorothy Sayers wrote: "Dante will have none of this, the lowest part of his Purgatory is as remote from Hell as the surface of Earth from the Centre-- its summit soars beyond Earth's atmosphere to a height no eye can reach. Hell is black, confined, stinking, noisy and suffocating. The great Mountain of Purgator rises in a pure sunlit solitude out the windswept southern sea… and by night and day, flaming hoses of heaven, which never were seen in Hell-- the undimmed sun, the moon like burnished maze, the starry habitations."





Instead, we believe in the immediate return of Christians to God for judgment, and that there is only one of two options as to how they will be judged. Hell is to be thought of as less a place than as a state of existence; not that it will not be located in a place someday but rather it is state of existence that our souls, which are not spatial/ temporal, can be cast in, that is "cast into hell". Hell is more a state of existence than a place of existence, that is, a state wherein we are under God's punishment.



Note: What is Hell? Perhaps, the fire of God's wrath is merely the absence of God's grace.

Dorothy Sayers wrote: "Hell is the enjoyment of your won way for ever. IF you really want God's way for you, you will get it in Heaven, and the pains of purgatory will not deter you, they will be welcomed as a means to the end. " (note, she understood Dante's purgatory to be this life)



"It has been said that the fire of Hell is simply the light of God as experienced by those who reject it; to those that is, who hold fast to their darling illusion of sin, the burning reality of holiness is a thing unbearable." P.16, D. Sayers



"Hell is the fleeing deeper into the iron-bound prison of the self- for the dammed also, after their manner, seek their own torment: … the fire of heavenly justice stings and spurs them so that all their fear is changed into desire." (Dante)





3. ON the last Day, the day of the Lord's Return, our same bodies will be raised and reunited with our souls under the conditions that are souls have been judged.



Westminster Confession Ch. 32 ¶2

At the last day, such as are found alive shall not die, but be changed: and all the dead shall be raised up, with the selfsame bodies, and none other (although with different qualities), which shall be united again to their souls forever.



Westminster Confession Ch. 32 ¶3

The bodies of the unjust shall, by the power of Christ, be raised to dishonor: the bodies of the just, by his Spirit, unto honor; and be made conformable to his own glorious body.



Therefore, every soul will be reunited with the same bodies, which will be raised up by the same spirit that raised Christ from the dead. Again, only one of two options: those bodies united to condemned souls will then be raised to dishonor…



4. The ultimate purpose of this judgment is that all people everywhere will ultimately respect and glorify God-- either in the power of his mercy in salvation or the power of his wrath in damnation.



Westminster Confession Ch. 33 ¶2

The end of God's appointing this day is for the manifestation of the glory of his mercy, in the eternal salvation of the elect; and of his justice, in the damnation of the reprobate, who are wicked and disobedient. For then shall the righteous go into everlasting life, and receive that fullness of joy and refreshing, which shall come from the presence of the Lord; but the wicked who know not God, and obey not the gospel of Jesus Christ, shall be cast into eternal torments, and be punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power.



5. The purpose of God's allowing us to know this now is to direct us to Christ in order to be "just"-- now before it is too late.

It has been argued by Dorothy Sayers that Dante's Purgatory was simply the experience of hell before it became final (keep in mind that however one reads Dante's purgatory, it was NOT hell.)


Perhaps, if we read Purgatory as the sufferings of this life, then we would be lead to either repentance or deeper hardening and unrepentance. Also, purgatory would not be so bad theologically (except perhaps for its overtly zealous concept of human freedom, etc.)



The key difference then between purgatory and hell is whether the hellishness of God's judgment as experienced in this life is to create repentance or not.



When every allowance is made (and Dante makes generous allowance) and when mercy and pity and grace have done all they can, the consequences of sin are the sinner's, to be borne, at his own choice, in a spirit of sullen rebellion or of ready acquiescence.



Perhaps the purpose of Dante's purgatory was to show the necessity of persuading the culprit to accept judgment. If a man becomes convinced of his own guilt, and he is sentenced by a just tribunal, whatever punishment given is remedial to him because he made it so; if he is not so convinced, then any punishment however enlightened remains merely vindictive, since he perceives it so and will not make it otherwise.
