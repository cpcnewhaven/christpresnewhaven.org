What Does It Mean to Have Faith?

[What follows is an excerpt from the prayer letter of George Levesque, who Assists us in campus ministry. But before I let George tell you about a recent event on campus, I should tell you that your prayers for Bim have been answered. Bim's father has changed dramatically in his response to Bim's Christian baptism. While he still does not endorse the Christian faith, he has made his peace with the fact that Bim is a Christian and feels this to be a respectable way of life. Thankfully, Bim's willingness to follow Christ, even at the risk of losing his earthly family, has been realized without loss of family! A great answer to prayer! Now George]

Last night, after a talk I gave at an outreach forum on campus, one of the students named Rebecca asked me, "I'm not a Christian. Tell me, 'What does it mean to have faith?'" This question was refreshingly direct, and as far as I could tell, it was sincere. After a pause, I tried to demonstrate that the real question is not whether we have faith, but what is the object of our faith. I couldn't answer all her questions during our Q. & A. session, but she agreed to participate in a three-week, follow-up study on the life of Christ.

The topic of the forum last night was "Life Perspectives" and it involved a discussion of a questionnaire given out to 75 students in two of the residential colleges on campus. What was most striking to me were the responses to two particular questions: "At the end of your life, what three things would you like to say you've accomplished?" and "What is the one question you would most like answered about life?" For the first question, almost everyone spoke of a commitment to certain goals (having a loving family, contributing to society, having a respectable professional life), and for the other question, almost everyone asked a variation of the question, "What is the purpose to life?"

Isn't that an incredible irony? On the one hand, people have a strong yearning for certain ideals, yet on the other hand, there is a deep anxiety about whether those ideals mean anything. In other words, people desire a "good life," but at the end of the day they are very unsure whether that life means anything. This irony struck me as a sad commentary on how the modern university has failed in its calling to bring "unity in diversity." I was reminded of the great hunger for meaning in our society, and of the power of the Gospel--the message of Christ who is "the way, the truth, and life." I was also inspired to pray and believe that God could use our church in downtown New Haven, to transform the lives of future leaders whereby they might bring all things under the lordship of Christ. Please join us in that prayer. Pray particularly for Rebecca and Kelvin, and for the three other students (Nigel, Grace, and Virginia) who expressed an interest in our investigative study.
